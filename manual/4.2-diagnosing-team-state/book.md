# 4.2 Diagnosing team state: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 4, pp. 282 to 285. Full text: `extraction/build/s09/book.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## Start with the starting point

Once you have decided you need a lasting team rather than a temporary structure, assess where you stand. Do you have the team you need? If not, go hire (see Chapter 3). If you do have the team, or most of it, ask how it is doing. This is a question you should be able to answer at any time, and it matters especially when you take over a new team: assess whether it has the structures, plan, and people to deliver excellent results (p. 282 to 283).

Come back to the operating system (Operating Principle 4). Check whether the team's mission or goals need clarifying (Chapter 2). Then look at the people and the dynamics: are the right people in the right jobs, how happy and fulfilled is the team with its work, does it work well together, are deadlines met and metrics healthy. If the honest answer to "is the team executing well and having a positive impact" is not a clear yes, dig into why (p. 283).

## Surveying the team

To start the assessment, survey the group before a meeting or offsite. The team leader, an HR partner, or an outside facilitator can run it, watching for the core elements of a high-functioning team and any signals of dysfunction (p. 283).

Her reference point is Patrick Lencioni's five dysfunctions: absence of trust, fear of conflict, lack of commitment, avoidance of accountability, and inattention to results. If a survey turns up one or more of these, discuss the results with the team to build mutual self-awareness and decide what to do about it. This makes for a good offsite session (p. 283). Once the team has a diagnosis and has committed to change, the manager's job is to model the new behaviors and hold the team accountable to moving from dysfunctional to functional to, with work, highly functional (pp. 283 to 284).

## The skill-will matrix

Before a team can exceed the sum of its parts, first assess whether it is capable of that as a whole; if not, look at each member individually. For this she uses the skill-will matrix, developed by executive coach Max Landsberg: skill is the ability to do a task, will is the motivation to do it (p. 284).

How to read each quadrant (pp. 284 to 286):

- **Too little skill.** The team cannot do the work required. Mentor or train existing members; if that is not enough, bring on people who have the skills.
- **Too much skill.** The team loses motivation. Rethink the team's mission and goals so they require more and better output.
- **Too little will.** The team is unproductive. Try inspiring it with a bolder mission (her example: broadening "build the education platform for the company" to "for our company and our users"). Use an offsite to say the thing you think you cannot say and name a possible dysfunction directly. Consider whether specific individuals are sapping the team's will and address it with them.
- **Too much will.** The team overpromises and under-delivers. Define expectations with the team and with those it owes work to, help it prioritize and scope, and check whether its talent matches its goals.

## Reading the quadrants in practice

Low skill and low will is a combination most high-growth companies cannot afford and should not carry. Low skill and high will is common with early-career employees or people who have moved laterally into a new function; it is worth investing in them, ideally with others on the team helping, and worth building in checkpoints to see whether skill catches up to will (p. 286).

High skill and high will is what a manager wants: the goal is to get people there and then delegate and empower enough to work yourself out of a job. High skill and low will is one of the hardest quadrants. The job is to find the source of the lost motivation: is it new, episodic, or persistent, is something happening in the person's life, or do they need a new role or team (p. 286; she returns to this in the section on delivering feedback, p. 391).
