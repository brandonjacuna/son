# 4.2 Diagnosing team state: working session

## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** How Sŏn reads its own state: what a diagnosis looks at, in what order, from what evidence, who writes it, on what rhythm, and how a finding becomes a change. The book's section is short (pp. 282 to 286): assess whether the team has the structures, plan, and people to deliver; survey it against Lencioni's five dysfunctions; place the team and then each person on the skill-will matrix. The white paper has its own diagnostic, five failure modes with a rule that the mode is identified before anyone intervenes at the person (WP p. 14). The chunk's central question is how those two instruments relate.

**Already decided upstream.** What counts as a team (4.1.2); when the structure is reexamined (4.1.32); how a lead's load is relieved and the seat load reading (4.1.35, 4.1.36); the accountability mechanisms (2.2.29); the leads' review, house review, gate review, and mechanism reset (2.3.13, 2.3.22, 2.3.26, 2.3.30); the check-in and what the person page holds (2.3.9, 2.3.11); the pulse and the feedback channel's window (2.3.15, 2.3.16). Where any of these is still open, this session records positions and marks the dependency.

**Who else must agree.** Dominic on everything at the founder level, and especially on 4.2.12 (whether the founders' own seats are read). The chef partner on who writes the kitchen's lines (4.2.8). Counsel on whether counted records carry weight in a dispute (cons. 12).

**Why it sits here.** 4.1 built the structure; this chunk decides how the house knows whether the structure is working. 4.3 (changes) and 4.4 (rebuilding, delegating) act on what this chunk reads. The first pass runs before the opening gate (4.2.19), so the frame has to exist before the leads arrive.

## 2. Agenda

A default order; start where he wants.

1. The read: what a diagnosis looks at and in what order (4.2.2), then the frame (4.2.3). These two set everything else.
2. The book's two instruments: the survey (4.2.4) and the skill-will matrix (4.2.5).
3. Evidence: sources (4.2.6), the line and the person rule (4.2.7), writers (4.2.8).
4. Rhythm and thresholds: 4.2.9, 4.2.10, then the coupling to change (4.2.11).
5. The founders' seats (4.2.12).
6. If time: a lead taking over a running domain (4.2.13).

Items 1 to 3 are the core; if the session is short, stop after 3 and draft the rest from what he said.

## 3. Briefs

### 4.2.2 Decide what a diagnosis reads at Sŏn, and the order the read follows

**The question:** when something is wrong, what does the house look at first, and when, if ever, does the look land on a named person?

**Why it matters now:** every later decision in this chunk assumes an answer. If the person comes first, the book's instruments (survey, matrix) fit; if the structure comes first, the white paper's modes fit and the book's instruments need a place. The first pass before opening (4.2.19) can only read what this decision says exists.

**Openers:**
- A Tuesday in the second month. Tickets are slow, a server is short with a runner, the close was thin two nights running. Walk me through what you actually do the next morning. Who do you talk to first?
- The white paper says a departure is "tension loss, not slot vacancy" (WP p. 09). Say what that means for the week after someone quits, in operational terms.
- Sideways: you have been diagnosing restaurants for other people for years. When you walk into someone else's room, what do you look at before you look at anyone?
- The earlier work counted six things a read can land on. Name them without looking. Which one do you think a lead will actually reach for?
- A problem that fits no mode: the overnight crew leaves the walk-in propped open twice. Whose is that? What gets written?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Is the person ever the first object of a read, or only the last?
- If the structure comes first, how does a lead raise a person-level problem they can see plainly, without waiting for the mode read? Which door?
- Does a read of a "service roster" mean anything after the night ends, or is the roster only the brief's business?
- What is written when a problem fits no mode?

**What the book says:** read the team as a whole first; only if it cannot exceed the sum of its parts, look at each member (p. 284). When taking over a team, assess structures, plan, and people (pp. 282 to 283).

**White-paper default:** "Before intervening at the people level, identify which failure mode is actually present" (WP p. 14, stated outright). The five objects beneath the house are not in the white paper; they are the earlier work's reading of it.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Safety-critical industries (aviation, hospitals using root-cause analysis) formalize "system before person" in incident review; the reviewer is required to name the process failure before any individual factor. The book's own "blame the process" language (WP p. 17) is the same posture.

**Options:** (a) the book's order, the lead's judgment as instrument; (b) the white paper's strict order, person last and only through tension slack after the domain fix; (c) the white paper's order as default with a named second door for a person-level problem a lead can see. Depth: cons. 1.

**Watch for:** an answer that says "structure first" but describes a lead forming a view of a person in the first breath; an order so strict that a real person-level problem has no door until a quarter passes; the roster treated as a team that persists.

**A finished answer:** the objects listed, the order stated, the person rule stated with its path, the no-mode outcome named, and Brandon can run the Tuesday example through it out loud.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.2.3 Decide the frame: the five failure modes, the book's five dysfunctions, or both

**The question:** what is the vocabulary every lead uses to say what is wrong?

**Why it matters now:** the frame decides what the survey asks (4.2.4), what the line records (4.2.7), what the scorecard shows (4.2.15), and what a new lead is taught. Two vocabularies at once is a real cost in a house with two leads and a kitchen still unshaped.

**Openers:**
- Say the five modes out loud, in order, with the fix for each. Now say Lencioni's five. Which set did you have to think about?
- A line cook stops speaking up at the brief. Is that absence of trust, sequence corruption, or cooling failure? What tells you which?
- Sideways: the white paper's four machine failures (WP p. 13) and its five modes (WP p. 14) don't line up; memory failure has no twin. Does that bother you, or is memory failure what the whole system exists to prevent, so it needs no line?
- If a lead can only remember one frame under load, which one would you want it to be?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Do the dysfunctions describe anything the modes cannot? Trust and conflict are the two candidates.
- Where does psychological safety live: here, or in 4.5 with norms?
- Is the frame taught in onboarding (3.3.45), and tested?
- Which frame does the scorecard show the founders each quarter?

**What the book says:** Lencioni's five dysfunctions are the reference for what a survey should find; the skill-will matrix gives four team-level readings (pp. 283 to 286).

**White-paper default:** the five modes, "use this as a diagnostic against any operational problem" (WP p. 14, stated outright).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Lencioni's model is used with a short self-assessment and a facilitated conversation; it reads a team's interior. Google's own team study found psychological safety the strongest factor in team effectiveness, which argues for placing the safety question somewhere deliberate rather than dropping it between frames.

**Options:** (a) the modes alone, dysfunctions mapped as symptoms; (b) the dysfunctions alone; (c) both, modes for structure and dysfunctions for the one true team's interior. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** a mapping that forces every dysfunction into a mode to make the frame look complete; "trust" left to no chunk; a frame chosen because it is the white paper's rather than because a lead can use it at eleven at night.

**A finished answer:** one frame named (or two with a rule for which reads what), each dysfunction placed or parked, the safety question handed to 4.5 explicitly, and a one-sentence plain definition of "failure mode" Brandon would put on the team home.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.2.4 Decide whether the house is surveyed on how it works together, in what form, and whether the results are discussed with the house

**The question:** does Sŏn ever ask the whole house, in writing, how the house is doing as a team, and does the house hear the answer?

**Why it matters now:** the pulse (2.3.15) and the hiring questions (3.4.15) are being built; adding to them is cheap now and disruptive later. The quarterly gathering (2.3.25) may or may not exist; if this survey wants an offsite, that decision moves.

**Openers:**
- The pulse asks one question. What is the second question you would most want the house to answer honestly, and would they?
- Sideways: at Coqodaq's opening, was there ever a moment the team was asked, all together, how it was going? What happened to the answer?
- The book says discuss the results with the team. Picture reading a low "trust" score aloud to forty people on a closed Monday. Do you do it?
- The white paper says feedback runs both directions (WP p. 19). Through what, exactly, does a server tell the founders the leads are not working well together?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Survey or no survey? If yes: the book's, or questions added to the pulse?
- Who reads it first: the leads, the founders, or the house?
- Is there any finding the house should not hear? Where is that line (2.2.6)?
- Does a survey pass 2.3.3's test: what does it buy that the pulse, the channel, the check-in's upward question, and the close do not?

**What the book says:** survey before a meeting or offsite; the leader, an HR partner, or an outside facilitator runs it; discuss results with the team; a good offsite session (p. 283).

**White-paper default:** employee NPS tracked regularly (WP p. 22); engagement measured across psychological safety and values alignment (WP p. 23); feedback both directions (WP p. 19). Silent on a one-off survey.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Short recurring pulses have largely replaced annual surveys where companies measure this at all; Gallup's twelve-question set is the best-known fixed instrument. Most restaurants only survey at exit.

**Options:** (a) no separate survey, a few questions in the pulse; (b) the book's survey before a house gathering, results discussed openly; (c) the survey runs, leads and founders read it, the house hears the change. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** a survey nobody is named to read; results promised to the house and then not shown; a psychological-safety question that this chunk and 4.5 both claim or neither does.

**A finished answer:** yes or no, the form, the reader, the rhythm, and what the house hears; the safety half handed to 4.5.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.2.5 Decide whether the skill-will matrix is used at Sŏn, on what, and who may write a will reading

**The question:** does anyone at Sŏn ever write down how motivated someone is?

**Why it matters now:** the person page carries no rating (2.3.11) and the check-in refuses a verdict (2.3.9). If leads are to use the matrix, those rules need a companion; if not, the book's hardest case (high skill, low will) needs a named home before anyone reaches it.

**Openers:**
- Your best server, month four, has gone quiet: still flawless, no longer generous. The book calls this the hardest quadrant. What do you want the lead to do that week, and what do you want written?
- Sideways: you were once the high-will, low-skill person on a floor at Alinea. What did the people managing you get right about that, and would you have wanted it on paper?
- The white paper says the system is the advocate and no one's advancement waits on a manager's judgment (WP p. 11). Can that be true if a lead holds a private will reading on each person?
- The team-level readings: "too much will" is over-promising. Where would you see that at Sŏn first, the goal page or the brief?

**Narrowing questions:**
- House and domain level only, person level too, or not at all?
- If a will reading exists, who writes it, where, and does the person see it?
- Where does "find the source of the lost motivation" (p. 286) get done: 5.1, 5.2, 5.8?
- Is "too little skill" across strands a deficit or a strand mismatch (WP pp. 10 to 11)?

**What the book says:** skill is ability, will is motivation; read the team, then each member; the four quadrants and their responses (pp. 284 to 286).

**White-paper default:** the path is documented and visible; advancement does not wait on a manager's attention or judgment (WP p. 11). Silent on motivation as something read.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. The matrix descends from situational leadership, built to adjust a manager's style to a person's readiness; most companies that use it do so in conversation, unrecorded.

**Options:** (a) as the book has it, privately held by the lead; (b) team level only, from records, no person placed; (c) not used, Chapter 5 carries the person-level cases. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** "we don't rate people" followed by a description of leads rating people in their heads and acting on it; the low-will case sent to a chunk that has not agreed to hold it; a will reading that is really a bandwidth reading (cooling failure) in disguise.

**A finished answer:** the level stated, the writer and the visibility stated if a reading exists, the low-will case's home named, and the strand-mismatch rule stated.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.2.6 Decide the evidence each mode is read from, and what is never evidence

**The question:** when a lead says a mode is present, what do they have to be able to point to?

**Why it matters now:** the records that feed the read (shift close, check-in lines, pulse, schedule, designation block, readiness rows) are being specified in 2.3 and 3.3 now; a source this chunk needs that no record captures has to be added before opening. The floor-management seat's load, the most likely first finding, needs its sources named.

**Openers:**
- Tension slack: someone is present but not carrying load, and others are compensating "without visibility" (WP p. 14). If it is invisible by definition, what record would show it? Walk through what the close, the schedule, and the brief would each show.
- Sideways: describe the last time you knew a room was in trouble before any number said so. What were you reading? Could a record have carried it?
- The white paper lists retention as evidence of cooling failure (WP p. 14) and as a slow measure read over time (WP p. 23). Which is it for the reset?
- Exit interviews are to be read "for what could have been better" (WP p. 23). By whom?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Records only, or a lead's marked observation too?
- Counts of services and reviews only, never money, never a percentage the stack generates?
- For the Maitre d seat: which of the nine proposed load inputs stay (check-in count against hours, moved check-ins, deferred calls, mentoring load, incident patterns, review load, coaching load)?
- Who reads exit interviews, or is that 5.10's to decide?

**What the book says:** are deadlines met and metrics healthy, does the team work well together, is it happy with its work (p. 283).

**White-paper default:** leading indicators are the nervous system's signal, lagging ones the X-ray (WP p. 12); the named leading indicators are employee NPS, cultural labor score, customer recognition rate (WP pp. 12, 22); incidents are captured at the close (WP p. 13).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Hospitals read staffing strain from counted events (missed breaks, overtime, incident reports) rather than from a manager's sense of it, precisely because the sense arrives late; the white paper's "X-ray after the fracture" is the same argument.

**Options:** (a) records only, counts only; (b) records first, a marked observation admitted with stated weight; (c) the book's mix, metrics plus judgment, unmarked. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** a source list that assumes a record the stack does not yet keep; money creeping in as a count; the Maitre d's load read from the Maitre d's own report alone.

**A finished answer:** a source list per mode, the floor-management seat's inputs fixed with 4.1.35's ruled-out fixes carried over, the never-evidence list stated, the retention question answered for the reset, and the exit-interview reader named or handed to 5.10.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.2.7 Decide the form of the evidence line, and whether it ever names a person

**The question:** what does one written finding look like, and can a name appear in it?

**Why it matters now:** the line is the unit the scorecard (4.2.15), the thresholds (4.2.10), and the change entry (4.2.11) all consume; its fields have to be fixed before any of them is built. The person rule is the one field with legal and dignity weight.

**Openers:**
- Write one line now, from memory, for the Tuesday example in 4.2.2. Which field did you not know how to fill?
- Sideways: the shift close already says what happened last night. What is the line that the close cannot be?
- If a line under tension slack must name who is compensating, it names seats. Does it ever get to name who is slack?
- Would you want to read a line about your own seat written by Dominic? What would make it fair?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The seven fields: which stay, which go, which are missing?
- Never a person; a person only under tension slack after the domain fix; or the seat-holder when the mode is on a seat?
- Does a line ever carry a verdict, a recommendation, or a number in money?
- Same form at every rhythm, or a shorter weekly one?

**What the book says:** a shared diagnosis the team can discuss and commit to (p. 283).

**White-paper default:** post-mortems are private and process-focused; the first question is what allowed this, not who failed (WP p. 17); the tension-slack fix starts at adjacent nodes (WP p. 14).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Blameless post-mortems in software operations record contributing factors and actions without attributing fault to individuals; the format is public inside the company and has held up because the rule is absolute rather than usual.

**Options:** (a) never a person; (b) a person only under tension slack, after the domain fix, in the person's own check-in record; (c) the seat-holder when the mode is on a seat. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** a "state" field that becomes a grade; fields that only a founder could fill; a line form that a lead cannot complete in five minutes after a review.

**A finished answer:** the fields fixed, the person rule stated with its path, no verdict and no money confirmed, and one example line written in the session.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.2.8 Decide who writes each domain's lines, and who integrates the house's

**The question:** whose job is it to say how each part of the house is doing, and whose to say how the whole is?

**Why it matters now:** 2.2.5 named who holds each lead's check-in; this is the mirror, who reads each lead's domain. It has to be in the leads' seat descriptions and their onboarding (3.3.45).

**Openers:**
- The Maitre d writes the floor's lines. Who writes the line about the Maitre d's seat?
- Sideways: in the rooms you have run, who actually knew how the whole house was doing? Was it the person whose job it was?
- Beverage: its own line, or inside the floor's? (4.1.5 may have answered this.)
- The kitchen's lines: held open for the chef partner, or a founder writes them until then?

**Narrowing questions:**
- One writer per domain and strand, plus one for system-side lines, plus one integrator?
- When a lead is the subject, does the partner who holds their conversation (2.3.14) write it?
- Does the integrator have authority to change a domain's line, or only to lay them side by side?

**What the book says:** the leader, an HR partner, or an outside facilitator can run the assessment (p. 283).

**White-paper default:** the Operations Lead is accountable to systems' outputs; the Maitre d to experience quality and team health (WP p. 10). Silent on a house-level reader.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Where a company has no HR function, the second-in-command or a peer manager usually reads the first manager's team; the rule that no one is their own sole reviewer is common.

**Options:** (a) writers by seat as the earlier work laid out, one integrator, the check-in holder reads the lead's own seat; (b) each lead writes their own domain and their own seat, the founders integrate; (c) the founders write everything until the house has run a quarter. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** an integrator seat that is really a third manager; the kitchen's lines written by a founder and never handed over; a lead reading their own seat.

**A finished answer:** a name or seat per domain, strand, and system side; one integrator; the lead-as-subject rule; the kitchen marked pending.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; kitchen pending the chef partner.

### 4.2.9 Decide the rhythms the diagnostic runs on, and whether it adds a meeting

**The question:** when is the house read, and does reading it cost a new hour on the calendar?

**Why it matters now:** 2.3 built the calendar and set a test a rhythm must pass to enter it (2.3.3). This chunk either rides the existing four or asks for a fifth.

**Openers:**
- The leads' review is weekly and already carries a training line (3.3.28) and an interface line (4.1.37). How much more can it carry before it stops being a review?
- Sideways: the book's manager diagnoses at an offsite, once, with a facilitator. Is there anything about a closed room away from the building that a weekly line cannot give you?
- What is read at a gate that is not read weekly?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Four existing rhythms, one line each: what is read and written at each?
- Any new meeting? If so, what does it buy (2.3.3)?
- Is the reset the only place a fix is designed, or can the leads' review fix something small?

**What the book says:** a survey before a meeting or offsite; the offsite is the moment (p. 283); a manager should be able to answer "how is the team doing" at any time (pp. 282 to 283).

**White-paper default:** none on rhythm.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies that run a weekly operating review and a quarterly business review usually put the health read inside the quarterly one rather than adding a meeting; the offsite is kept for direction, not diagnosis.

**Options:** (a) the four existing rhythms, no new meeting; (b) a standing diagnostic review; (c) the book's offsite as the moment. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** a leads' review that becomes a reporting session; a reset a year away holding every fix; the offsite chosen because it is pleasant.

**A finished answer:** one line per rhythm saying what is read and written there; a yes or no on any new meeting with 2.3.3's test applied.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.2.10 Set the recurrence thresholds as counts, and where each repeat surfaces

**The question:** how many times does the same thing have to happen before it climbs a level?

**Why it matters now:** the stack can surface repeats automatically (4.2.18) only if the counts exist; without them, escalation depends on someone remembering, which is the failure the white paper calls bus failure (WP p. 13).

**Openers:**
- The same close note three Fridays running. When does that stop being a note and start being a question about the seat?
- Sideways: in your consulting work, what was the pattern clients always noticed too late? How many repeats had there been?
- Should a mode present at a gate ever be waived to open on time?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Starting values for: one occurrence; the same signal on the same object across consecutive leads' reviews; the same mode on consecutive house reviews after a fix; a mode at a gate.
- Where does each surface?
- All marked for the first reset (2.3.30)?
- Counts of services and reviews only?

**What the book says:** moving from dysfunctional to functional takes sustained attention (pp. 283 to 284).

**White-paper default:** none; 2.3.30's rule that parameters are re-set on real data applies.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Incident-management practice sets a small fixed count (often three) for "recurring" before a problem review opens; the number matters less than that it is written down.

**Options:** (a) the earlier work's counts (one; three consecutive weekly; two consecutive quarterly after a fix; any at a gate); (b) lower counts for the first quarter, raised at the reset; (c) no thresholds until the first reset, everything surfaces by judgment. Depth: cons. 8.

**Watch for:** a gate threshold that the opening date will pressure; a threshold expressed in money; counts nobody will actually tally.

**A finished answer:** each threshold a count, each with a destination, all marked for the reset.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.2.11 Decide how a diagnosis becomes a change, and how the change is read back

**The question:** what has to be true before a finding turns into a change, and how does the house know the change worked?

**Why it matters now:** 4.1.33 built the change entry with a "reason first" rule; this decides whether the reason must be a line, and what the lead does between the change and its read-back. It is the book's "ninety percent execution."

**Openers:**
- A lead wants to move a station because "it isn't working." Under this rule, what do they have to bring?
- Sideways: the book says the manager models the new behavior and holds the team to it. Picture the Maitre d doing that on a Saturday. Is that leadership or is that the center absorbing load (WP p. 14)?
- Who writes the read-back, the person who proposed the change or the source that raised the problem?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Must a change entry carry a line as its reason? Always, or normally?
- A change whose stated reason is a person: refused, or routed to Chapter 5?
- When is the read-back, and by whom?
- What is the lead's part in between?

**What the book says:** once the team has a diagnosis and commits, the manager models the new behaviors and holds the team accountable (pp. 283 to 284); ten percent plan, ninety percent execution (p. 287).

**White-paper default:** the process that allowed the failure gets updated so the next person inherits a system that has learned (WP p. 13); blame the process, fix the process (WP p. 17).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Change-control practice in operations pairs every change with a stated reason and a scheduled review of whether it did what it was meant to; the review is the part most often skipped.

**Options:** (a) a line as the reason, a person as reason refused; (b) a line normally, a person allowed when Chapter 5's record supports it; (c) the book's posture, the lead owns and models the plan, the record follows. Depth: cons. 8.

**Watch for:** a coupling so strict that a small fix waits a quarter for a line; read-backs nobody schedules; "modeling" that is a lead working every service.

**A finished answer:** the reason rule, the person-as-reason rule, the read-back's timing and writer, and the lead's part stated in one sentence.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.2.12 Decide whether the founders' own seats are read by the same diagnostic, and from what sources

**The question:** does the instrument that reads every seat read the two founders' seats too, and who holds the pen?

**Why it matters now:** the white paper promises a company built to run without the founders (WP p. 06); the most likely early overload is a founder still doing a lead's work. 0.3 asked whether the partners hold the basics for each other; this is the operational form of that answer.

**Openers:**
- The white paper's example of CPU failure is a general manager resolving scheduling conflicts (WP p. 13). Three weeks before opening, who is resolving scheduling conflicts at Sŏn?
- Sideways: what would it take for you to accept a written line from Dominic saying your seat is overloaded, and act on it? What would make you dismiss it?
- The earlier work ruled out a chief of staff or an assistant as a fix for founder overload. Do you agree, and why?
- A lead's only direct signal of their own overload is what they say in their check-in. If that check-in is with you, where does it go?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Read, or not?
- Sources allowed and refused?
- Fixes in scope and out?
- Who writes: the other partner, rotating; the integrator; no one?
- Where a lead's "what's in my way" lands when the holder is a founder (5.9).

**What the book says:** a manager should be able to say how the team is doing at any time, including their own contribution; Operating Principle 4 (pp. 282 to 283).

**White-paper default:** "a company built to run without them" (WP p. 06); CPU failure (WP p. 13). Silent on who reads a founder.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Founding partners who review each other formally usually do it on a fixed rhythm with a fixed form, precisely so it does not depend on either one raising it; the ones who leave it informal mostly report that it did not happen.

**Options:** (a) same instrument, stated sources and fixes, the other partner writes; (b) counts from the seat load reading only, no fix list; (c) a partners' matter outside the diagnostic. Depth: cons. 9.

**Watch for:** anything in the record that reads as a judgment of either founder's character or style rather than a seat's load; a source list that admits "I feel"; an operating-agreement question (2.1.1) unasked.

**A finished answer:** read or not; if read, the sources, the fixes, the writer, and the rotation; the lead's-signal question routed to 5.9.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.2.13 Decide what a lead taking over a running domain reads first, and what they hold off on before their first check-ins

**The question:** when a later lead inherits a domain that is already running, what do they read, in what order, and what may they not do yet?

**Why it matters now:** it belongs in the lead onboarding plan (3.3.45), which is written before opening; a later lead will arrive with the book's instinct to size up each person in week one.

**Openers:**
- A new Operations Lead starts in month nine. What is on their desk on day one, and what should they read before they meet anyone?
- Sideways: when you consulted into a running room, what did you wish existed on paper that never did?
- Their first arithmetic: how many check-ins does the seat owe per month against the hours it works? Is that the first read?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Order: structure, plan, evidence history, then people through their own pages?
- The first two concrete reads?
- What is off limits before the first round of check-ins: a survey, a placement, anything written about a person?
