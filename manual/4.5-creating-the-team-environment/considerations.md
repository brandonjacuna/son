# 4.5 Creating the team environment: considerations

What the white paper holds, what the earlier work is worth keeping, the options, and where Sŏn may diverge from the book. Topics follow the book's order (pp. 304 to 330). "WP p. N" is the white paper; "cons. N" is a section here. Nothing here decides anything.

## 1. The frame: rituals, and what Sŏn already has

The book builds a team's fabric by convening it: offsites and meetings (p. 304). Sŏn's earlier work started elsewhere, and it shapes every option below: most of what an offsite or a meeting exists to create was assigned upstream to standing mechanisms that run without anyone calling a room together: the brief and the close (2.3.7, 2.3.8), the check-in as the 1:1 (2.3.9), the leads' review (2.3.13), the partners' review (2.3.4), the house review (2.3.22), the reset and the founders' annual block (2.3.30), the decision-rights register (2.2.4), and per-service designations (4.1.20). The white paper backs that reading: great nights "feel like distributed intelligence rather than coordinated direction," which is "not a management achievement. It is the structure functioning correctly" (WP p. 8).

So this chunk's job is narrower than the chapter: how people are treated inside the rooms already built, what the house does that no review does, where every kind of speech goes, and how the whole is read. Task 4.5.1 lists the upstream decisions so nothing here reopens one.

One naming problem runs through the whole chunk. The book's "check-in" is a short round at the start of a meeting. Sŏn's "check-in" is its form of the 1:1 (2.3.9). This file uses "opening round" and "closing round" for the book's practice.

## 2. Offsites

**What the book holds.** Three purposes: turn a work group into a team, set near-term priorities, build long-term thinking (p. 305). Not for talent problems. Senior teams two or three times a year; junior teams at least annually (p. 306). A shared planning document, a fixed agenda with purpose and limit per section, a distinctive place that becomes shared memory (pp. 309 to 311).

**What the white paper holds.** Nothing. It never mentions an offsite, a retreat, or a staff gathering. Dinner runs alone for at least a quarter before anything is added (WP p. 36); a closed day of service to hold one would be a real cost the white paper does not contemplate.

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** Three groups were considered. The whole staff: an offsite costs a closed day, the cohort and training services already do the forming work, and team state is read from records. The leads: they meet weekly and monthly already. The founders: one closed-hours block a year, which 2.3.30 now decides. The book's scaffolding was gone through piece by piece: an opening round and icebreakers declined, a per-section agenda and a shared pre-read already the practice, a rotating notetaker declined in favor of each mechanism's record, a spoken closing round replaced by a written reflection read at the next review, a feedback form declined as a survey. These were positions; they are options here.

**What the earlier work left open.** It never asked the book's actual question for a senior team: does the leadership team, once both leads are hired, get a block of its own? That is where the book's argument is strongest: a new leadership team is forming (cons. 3), and a forming team "benefits from clarity-building work and work-style exercises" (pp. 308 to 309). The working-with-me documents (1.1) and the lead onboarding plan (3.3.45) are the material.

**Options for 4.5.4.**
- (a) No leadership block. The weekly leads' review, the monthly partner conversation (2.3.14), and the lead onboarding plan carry the forming work. Commits Sŏn to reading the leadership team's state from those records alone, and to doing any long-horizon thinking inside the reset and the founders' block.
- (b) One pre-opening block only, once both leads are hired, then none. Commits Sŏn to a few closed hours before opening (no service cost) and to the position that a formed team needs no repeat.
- (c) A recurring leadership block, one to three times a year, on the closed day if one exists (2.3.12). Commits Sŏn to the book's cadence for its senior team and to a page for each block (4.5.16 has the variant).
- Under (b) or (c), a second choice: how much of the book's scaffolding to keep. A written pre-read and a fixed agenda are cheap. An opening round and a closing round are decided in 4.5.6. Off the premises or in the room is a taste question with one real consequence: the book's shared-memory effect (p. 311) is stronger somewhere distinctive.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. A pre-opening period in which the leadership team works together before any staff arrives is common in restaurant openings; the work is usually operational (menu, systems, hiring) rather than team-focused, which is the gap the book names. Stripe held a multi-hour leadership offsite as a recurring creator-mode meeting and learned not to over-program it (pp. 319 to 320).

**Divergence.** If (a), Sŏn declines the book's senior-team offsite outright. Note it; the reason on record is that the standing structures carry the content and a closed day is not free.

## 3. Development stages, and the three teams the stages land on

**What the book holds.** Forming, storming, norming, performing, with no standard length (p. 306). Psychological safety moves a team between stages. A lead is more directive while forming, frames conflict as normal while storming, keeps honing while norming, raises the challenge while performing (pp. 307 to 308).

**What the white paper holds.** Onboarding is where a new person forms: the why before the how, a mentor from day one, a ninety-day plan (WP p. 18). Dinner runs alone for at least a quarter (WP p. 36). Nothing on how the leadership team or the whole house is expected to move through stages.

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** The book assumes one fixed group meeting repeatedly. Sŏn has several objects the word "team" lands on (4.1.2 decides which count). The nightly roster dissolves at close, so it cannot storm or norm; it forms at the brief and performs on its first service, with conflict settled by pre-set rules and anything unanticipated captured at close. The cohort forms through the training sequence, which already matches the book's forming guidance. The whole house forms once, pre-opening, storms during dinner's first quarter, and norms at the first reset. A domain does not form as a team; its people relate to their lead one-on-one. The leadership team, which the earlier work did not examine, forms when the second lead is hired; its storming is the first real disagreement between the co-leads (2.2.3).

**The freeze question (4.5.22).** The earlier work proposed that rules not move at all during dinner's first quarter: the loose edge guarded by rules staying fixed, the tight edge by the first reset being the one moment they can be revisited on evidence. The book's storming guidance points the other way: "work to clean up goals, roles, and processes" while the team storms (p. 307). The white paper pulls both ways too: a capture updates the process so "the next server inherits a system that has already learned from it" (WP p. 13), and dinner runs alone for a quarter so the heart is solid (WP p. 36).

**Options for 4.5.22.**
- (a) Full freeze: nothing changes until the reset except a safety fix. Commits Sŏn to living with a bad rule for up to a quarter and to a heavy first reset.
- (b) Process moves, norms do not: fixes from captures and tool fixes flow continuously (WP p. 13), while norms, structure, roles, and the register wait for the reset. Commits Sŏn to defining the line between a process and a norm clearly enough that a lead can apply it at close.
- (c) No freeze; changes run through the structure-change entry (4.1.34) at any time. Commits Sŏn to the book's storming guidance and to a house whose rules can move under someone in their third week.

**Divergence.** (a) and (b) diverge from the book's storming guidance. The reason on record is that a rule that moves during storming is hard to tell from a lead's preference (cons. 7).

## 4. Psychological safety: the environment's half

**What the book holds.** Psychological safety is the ability to ask a question, dissent, discuss a failure, or show vulnerability without fear of repercussions (p. 306, after Google's Project Aristotle). Norms make it: no topics are undiscussable (pp. 317 to 318), be inclusive (p. 318), criticize ideas not people (p. 327).

**What the white paper holds, stated outright.** The first question is what allowed this to happen, not who failed; management absorbs blame from staff; post-mortems are private and process-focused (WP p. 17). Feedback runs both directions; team members weigh in on management effectiveness (WP p. 19). A dedicated channel syncs to the execution system and the loop gets closed (WP p. 22). Pre-authorized generosity "signals trust to staff" (WP p. 26). The cultural measure includes "engagement results across psychological safety and values alignment" (WP p. 23). A real employee area with lockers "is not a perk. It is basic dignity, and dignity is a precondition for presence" (WP pp. 19 to 20).

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** Each of the book's four risk acts was given a structural destination rather than a coaching conversation: a question goes to a mentor, the check-in, or the channel, none of which records anything about the asker; a dissent goes to a written joint document (4.1.35 for the leads; 4.5.10 asks whether it extends to anyone); a failure goes to the close's capture under blame-the-process; vulnerability is not solicited, and the house offers physical dignity instead. Whether that last position holds is 4.5.6's question, since an opening round is a solicitation.

The useful inversion: "no topics are undiscussable" becomes "every topic has a named destination," and the failure to watch for is a topic with none. One exists today: a concern about the lead who holds a person's own check-in; it waits on 2.2.5. Scheduling, benefits, leave, and harassment or discrimination reports route separately and never wait on a lead noticing. A concern about a founder routes like any concern, and a founder is never an automatic destination during service. Pay questions route to 5.5; a check-in never negotiates pay.

Handed on, not decided here: a failure that is a person's and not the process's (a serious violation) reaches senior leadership the same day, is never aired in a brief or close, and belongs to 5.10 and counsel; how the response varies with severity or repetition belongs to 5.1 and 5.8.

**Measurement (4.5.25).** The white paper's measure is survey-shaped (WP p. 23), alongside employee NPS tracked regularly (WP p. 22). The earlier work avoided surveys and proposed behaviour signals: the capture rate, process changes following a capture, dissent write-ups filed, channel items closed inside the window, and one recurring pulse question. None equals a psychological-safety survey, and all need a first-quarter baseline (2.2.18).

**Options for 4.5.25.**
- (a) Survey question(s), on the employee NPS pulse (2.3.15). Commits Sŏn to the white paper's measure as written and to reading a small team's answers with care for anonymity.
- (b) Behaviour signals only. Commits Sŏn to the position that safety shows in what people do (report, dissent, ask), and to a divergence from the white paper's own measure.
- (c) Both, with the survey read as the check on the signals. Commits Sŏn to two instruments and one owner.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Google's Project Aristotle, which the book cites, found psychological safety the strongest predictor of team effectiveness, and measured it by survey. Toyota's practice of stopping the line for any defect and asking why repeatedly treats an error as the process's first; the capture at close is the same move.

## 5. Meetings: groundwork, roles, and norms

**What the book holds.** Meetings only work as well as the relationships in them (pp. 312 to 313). Three roles: DRI, facilitator, notetaker (pp. 313 to 314). Notes are not a transcript; they hold topics, decisions, follow-ups with owners and dates, open questions (p. 314). Every participant owns the meeting (pp. 314 to 315). Purpose, agenda, limit (pp. 315 to 316). Norms: logistics, no undiscussable topics, inclusion, disagree and commit, a parking lot, action items, reconfigure every three to six months (pp. 316 to 319). Operator-mode meetings decide; creator-mode meetings brainstorm (pp. 319 to 320).

**What the white paper holds.** No meeting is described except the shift brief (WP p. 14, as part of the nervous system). The cost argument is present in another form: bandwidth is what is left after survival, and an organization that cannot measure it measures output (WP p. 12). A traditional house carries roughly fourteen management positions around one dinner; Sŏn plans roughly eight leadership lines across the whole day (WP p. 22), which means each standing meeting costs a larger share of a smaller group.

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** There is no meeting during service; the brief addresses a crew about to work and the close one that has just finished. A time-cost ledger of what every standing mechanism costs each person in paid time was proposed, arguing the hourly total stays small. The book's roles were mapped: the DRI declined in favor of a mechanism owner on the register, the facilitator folded into whoever chairs (2.3.13 for the leads' review), the notetaker declined in favor of each mechanism's record. The norms were mapped: fixed times, a hard stop, and pre-reads a day ahead already the case; "disagree and commit" rewritten as "the disagreement is written down and the decision is checked later against what happened"; the parking lot declined because off-agenda items have a channel; reconfiguration handed to the reset.

**Options for 4.5.5 (roles).** (a) The book's three roles by name; commits Sŏn to a word (DRI) no one on the floor uses elsewhere. (b) Owner, chair, record: the owner is the register entry, the chair runs the room, the record is the mechanism's form; commits Sŏn to naming a chair for every standing meeting and deciding whether it rotates. (c) Owner and chair always the same person; simplest, and loses the book's mutual-ownership device.

**Options for 4.5.6 (rounds).** (a) None; the pre-read opens and the record closes. (b) A work round (what each person needs from this meeting) and a one-line closing round. (c) A round that may be personal by choice, never required. The brief is a special case: its length is fixed (2.3.7) and 4.5.13 governs its non-operational content.

**Options for 4.5.9 (norms).** The choices are few: pre-reads read beforehand or together in silence at the start; delegates or not; devices; what a chair does with an off-agenda item; how a dissent is recorded once a decision stands. Each is one sentence; 4.5.14 is the page.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Amazon opens many meetings with silent reading of a memo, the book's "read together" option. Danny Meyer's Union Square Hospitality Group has long used the pre-service lineup to carry culture as well as the menu, the same tension 4.5.13 settles.

**Divergence.** Declining the DRI, the notetaker, and the parking lot diverges from the book's mechanics; the reason on record is that each function exists under another name. The risk is that a function with no name stops being done, which the meeting audit (4.5.29) should ask.

## 6. Decisions in a room: the method, the log, and unblocking

**What the book holds.** Name who decides and how: autocratic, consensus, democratic, consultative, delegated (pp. 324 to 326). The senior person speaks last. A decision log helps if kept current (pp. 326 to 327). Stripe's unblocking process: first ask whether the decision is worth escalating; if it is, the parties jointly write the problem, options, and open trade-offs; then their managers decide (pp. 372 to 377).

**What the white paper holds.** Authority lives in the domain, and the domain owner is accountable to its outcomes (WP pp. 7 to 8). The server owns the table and resolves within an authorized range (WP p. 13). Lunch, if ever, is "an equal decision among all three partners" (WP p. 36), the only place the white paper names a decision method.

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** The register (2.2.4) fixes most decision rights in advance, so the method rarely needs naming live: "delegated" is the default, and "consultative" is a rule for decisions that must consult affected parties first. The partner-level rule is 2.1.13. The decision frameworks the book lists (RAPID, SPADE, the seven-step) were declined; the register is the one framework taught. On the log, the book's own caution (it works only when kept current) was the reason for embedding decisions in each mechanism's record rather than a separate sheet that goes stale.

**Options for 4.5.7.** (a) Register only; for a topic not on it, the chair names an owner on the spot and the register is updated after. (b) Register plus the book's five methods by name for anything off-register. Either combines with (c) senior-speaks-last for founders and leads, with the book's exception for a deliberate provocation, or not.

**Options for 4.5.26.** (a) No separate log; each mechanism's record holds its decisions and the register holds who may make them. Commits Sŏn to a person finding a rule's reason by knowing which meeting made it. (b) One log, a line per decision (what, when, why, who, the dissent), kept by each chair. Commits Sŏn to keeping it current or watching it die. (c) A decision line in each record, surfaced in the team home (2.2.15). Commits Sŏn to a small build.

**Options for 4.5.10.** (a) The book's process for everyone. (b) The joint write-up only between people who each own something; anyone else raises it with their lead, who decides. (c) Reversible disagreements are decided by the nearest domain owner and revisited at the close. The book's first test ("is it worth escalating") already covers most floor disagreements.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. "Disagree and commit" is Amazon's stated principle; the book credits Andy Grove (p. 318). The unblocking process is Stripe's.

## 7. Correcting behavior, and the lead's own standard

**What the book holds.** Criticize ideas, not people. Name a norm violation in the moment; correct a work-style pattern privately, and only when the team has the shared vocabulary to hear it; otherwise give feedback outside the room, promptly, specific and brief (pp. 327 to 328).

**What the white paper holds, stated outright.** Post-mortems are private and process-focused; management absorbs blame from staff (WP p. 17).

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** The book's own example of naming someone's work style in a room ("acting a bit red") was read as a negative example: no one is corrected or characterized in front of the group. A chair may decline an item, hold time, and name a norm; any correction of a person happens one-on-one. The work-style vocabulary's team use is decided in 1.1.4 and is not reopened here; 4.5.35 only checks that it appears nowhere it should not.

The earlier work also named the drift the design is least defended against: a lead's personal standard becoming an unwritten rule ("here's how I want it done tonight"), because no document holds the emotional standard of the room (that is the Maitre d's domain, WP p. 10). Two guards were proposed: a change not on a versioned register, sheet, or module is not a rule, and check-in lines naming "the lead's way" are read as a friction signal at the reset.

**Options for 4.5.8.** (a) The book's rule as written, including private correction of a pattern once vocabulary exists. (b) Stricter: nothing about a person in any group setting, ever; the chair's powers are the item, the clock, and the norm. (c) Same as (b), plus the two guards against a lead's unwritten rule. Commits Sŏn under (b) or (c) to a private conversation existing and being used (5.2, 5.3).

## 8. The leadership team

**What the book holds.** A leadership team meeting is distinguished by breadth and stakes, and it models what the rest of the company does (pp. 328 to 329). Lencioni's first team: a leader's commitment to the leadership team comes before their commitment to the team they manage (p. 329). Stripe's Monday meeting ran on written snippets sent the night before (pp. 329 to 330). An expanded leadership team and later an operating group gave newer leaders a forum; both were retired as needs changed (p. 330).

**What the white paper holds.** Two co-leads, parallel, neither reporting to the other, serving the same center (WP p. 10). The chef partner is a founding seat owning culinary vision (WP p. 3). Designations mark accountability in the moment and "are not permanent elevations above anyone" (WP p. 10). Roughly eight leadership lines across the day (WP p. 22). Nothing on what the leads owe each other as a group.

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** The first-team rule was declined as a strict ordering: the leads are co-equal, and asking each to prioritize a shared meeting over their own domain could itself create a management layer above them. What the rule protects against was kept as a signal-integrity problem: a domain issue that never reaches the cross-domain room. Stripe's snippets map to the lead's weekly note (2.2.33) and the nightly close note. No permanent supplemental tier; a leadership line is added only when a staffing plan requires it (4.1.36).

**Options for 4.5.2.** (a) The book's first team: each lead is asked to put the leadership team's success first, and the chef partner sits in it. Commits Sŏn to saying so at the lead interviews and to a review whose agenda earns that priority. (b) Co-equal leads with a duty to surface: no ordering of loyalties, but any domain problem that could touch the other domain reaches the leads' review the same week. Commits Sŏn to defining "could touch" and reading the record for what did not arrive. (c) The founders are the first team and the leads' review is coordination. Commits Sŏn to the leads reading themselves as reports, which sits uneasily with the white paper's parallel structure.

**Options for 4.5.36.** (a) No wider forum. (b) Designation holders join the leads' review for one fixed item. (c) A wider forum is created at a gate (a headcount, a second service period), with a retire date, as the book's own arc suggests.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Lencioni's first-team idea is widely used. The general manager and executive chef pairing is the usual restaurant shape of two co-leads, and its common failure is meeting only when something has gone wrong.

## 9. Rituals the book's chapter implies and Sŏn has not placed

**The staff meal (4.5.12).** Neither the white paper nor any other Sŏn source mentions one. It is the one team-building practice the industry already runs daily, at little incremental "meeting" cost, and it would sit inside the paid pre-service window the white paper already funds (WP p. 26) but never sizes. The practical constraint: a meal that fits inside existing kitchen prep is a different decision from one that displaces the brief or adds kitchen labor on a busy night. It should be decided before the window's shape locks in during training services, because adding it later displaces something already scheduled there. It needs the chef partner's read on cost and time (4.1.25, 2.3.29), and the morning and late-night periods may answer differently. Options: (a) a daily meal before dinner, eaten together, cooked by the kitchen; (b) a meal in shifts, so the floor and the kitchen never all sit at once; (c) no meal, and the window holds only the check-in and the brief; (d) a meal on the closed day or one day a week. A counsel note: time inside the paid window needs no separate treatment; if any meal period is ever unpaid, it has conditions on length and being fully relieved of duty, which counsel should confirm before it is written down.

**The brief's team slot (4.5.13).** 2.3.7 fixed the brief's operational content and left a bounded slot after it. The earlier design: a few minutes on a full window, nothing on a compressed one; one item from a short priority list (a one-line word from each designation holder for the night; a lesson from a recent incident told as process, never as a person's fault; roughly weekly, a principle checked against something that actually happened; a milestone told as fact, such as a readiness read passed); never anything else about a person, a script, a survey, a policy change, or anything past a cutoff before service. The record holds only which item ran or that the slot was skipped, so the skip rate over a quarter reads as a sign of a compressed window, not a manager's fault. The kitchen's version needs the chef partner's agreement.

**Moments the house marks (4.5.23).** The book's point about shared memory (p. 311) has no Sŏn mechanism. Candidates: a first solo service, a skill signed off, a period's opening, the anniversary. The white paper's recognition is peer-to-peer in the moment (WP p. 19); what is marked publicly and what is never marked (pay, a departure's reason, anything that reads as a rating) is the decision.

**Community after the opening cohort (4.5.27).** The cohort gives the opening team each other. After opening, hires arrive one at a time. The earlier position was no new mechanism (the mentor, paired shifts, a first solo named at the brief, peer recognition), said plainly to build a thinner belonging for staff than the white paper designs for customers (a five-visit arc, WP pp. 24 to 26, against lockers and a break space, WP pp. 19 to 20). One option with a real trade-off: batching later hires to a shared start date, at the cost of a seat staying open longer (3.3.16).

**The mentor after the pairing ends (4.5.28).** 3.3.14 decides how a pairing ends. The earlier position on what follows: not tracked, honored as a scheduling preference, never a role (advocate, coach, channel to leadership), and a former mentee becomes eligible to mentor once their own plan closes. Anything recorded is in the person's own words on the person page (2.3.11). The earlier work's one-time career conversation belongs to 5.1.

## 10. Reading the environment: bandwidth first

The white paper orders health as "bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results" (WP p. 12). The earlier work made bandwidth the primary read of the environment: a mechanism that ran only by cutting into rest or prep is a failed mechanism, and a goal met at a bandwidth cost is scored as such (2.2.23). Two signals specific to this chunk go to the metric register (4.5.31): how often the brief's team slot is skipped, and how often the close's capture and closing note compete for the same minutes. Three gaps are named: the cultural labor score (2.2.20) is not yet built; the close window's length is unstated; and slack in the labor model cannot be verified from outside, since a lean schedule and one with slack look the same on a calm night.

## 11. What a person can count on: the environment checklist

The earlier work decomposed an obligation stated in the brand material (excluded here; the name is dropped) into ten checkable things a person can rely on, and rated each against the mechanisms built so far. The list stands without its origin; 4.5.30 rewrites it against current decisions. The ten, with the white paper's line and the gap found:

1. Rules they can read before acting (WP p. 14: skill invisibility is a failure mode). Gap: the why session is empty until the founding documents exist (2.1, 3.3.5).
2. A stated range: what they may do without asking, and where the edge is (WP p. 13, 26). Gap: the range's money edge is a sentence, not a number, and financials are not a source for this work; 2.2.4 and 3.3.40 own it.
3. Judgment built, not procedure only (WP p. 19: no one touches a table until ready). Gap: nothing reads whether judgment keeps growing after the readiness read (5.1).
4. Safety to err (WP p. 17). The most complete: capture at close, private post-mortem, no verdict on the person's file.
5. A destination for what they need to say (WP p. 19, 22). Gap: a concern about one's own lead (2.2.5).
6. Being known, in their own words (WP p. 18: the mentor; 2.3.11: the person page). Gap: thin among peers, since the roster changes nightly.
7. A visible path (WP p. 11: the path is explicit before they start). Gap: waits on the learning platform and the first modules (3.3.38).
8. Time that is not taken: the paid pre-service window, the check-in on the clock, the close, slack (WP p. 26). Gap: the close window's length and the cultural labor score.
9. A say in the rules (WP p. 13, 17: employees are the users; the process learns from a capture). Gap: the reset is run by leadership; the floor's input is read from records, not asked live. The gathering's open question (4.5.24) would be the one direct channel.
10. A place of their own (WP pp. 19 to 20: basic dignity). Gap: the least built; 3.3.44 confirms it before the first cohort.

Two notes. Belonging is an outcome the ten produce, not an eleventh. And the same lens, turned on the founders, found time not taken and a visible path weaker for them than for staff, because the design looks at the team and not at its builders. That routes to 6.1 and 6.3 and is never recorded here as anything about either founder.

## 12. Divergences from the book, collected

- No whole-staff offsite; the leadership block is an open question (cons. 2).
- The DRI, the notetaker, the parking lot, and the decision frameworks are declined in favor of functions that already exist under other names (cons. 5, 6).
- "No topics are undiscussable" becomes "every topic has a destination" (cons. 4).
- A first-quarter freeze, if adopted, runs against the book's storming guidance (cons. 3).
- The first-team rule may be declined as an ordering of loyalties (cons. 8).
- Correction in a group is narrower than the book allows (cons. 7).
- Psychological safety may be read by behaviour rather than survey, which is also a divergence from the white paper's own measure (cons. 4).

## 13. Counsel questions

- The staff meal's treatment as paid time inside the pre-service window, and the conditions on any unpaid meal period, before either is written down (4.5.12).
- Nothing else in this chunk needs counsel. The handling of a serious violation by a person, which the destinations page only routes, needs employment-law review when 5.10 drafts it.
