# 4.1 Team structures: working session

## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** The shape of the house: what counts as a team, who the leadership lines are, how a seat relates to its lead, whether there are levels, what a per-service designation is, how the structure changes, and the list of seats everything else hangs on. The book's chapter is about arranging teams to serve strategy and adding layers as a company grows (pp. 261 to 281). Sŏn's structure is a web that grows outward (WP p. 09), so most of the book's instruments (dotted lines, business units, layers) get translated or refused here, deliberately.

**Already decided upstream.** The floor manager and the Maitre d are one seat (2026-09-13, recorded in 2.2's `decisions.md`). That is not reopened; 4.1.1 notes what it settles and what it leaves open. From 2.2, if decided by the time this session runs: the decision-rights form (2.2.2), the cross-domain rule (2.2.3), who holds each lead's check-in (2.2.5), emergency authority (2.2.7), the channels a change travels (2.2.11), and the accountability mechanisms (2.2.29). Where they are not yet decided, this session records positions and marks the dependency.

**Who else must agree.** Dominic on everything at the founder level. The chef partner on the kitchen's spine, the mid-service call (4.1.22), the pass card, and everything below the spine (4.1.25), which is theirs outright. Counsel on the salaried test and designation-borne authority (cons. 11).

**Why it sits here.** 2.2 built the operating system on units it did not fully define; 2.2.36 (the variety test) waits on this chunk's line list. 3.1 and 3.2 cannot write a candidate sheet without a direct lead per seat (WP p. 18). 4.2 to 4.8 build the team's environment and need to know what "the team" is. 5.5 prices what this chunk defines (fluency, salaried seats).

## 3. Briefs

### 4.1.2 Decide what counts as a team at Sŏn, and whether the service periods are one team or several

**The question:** when the book says "team," what is it pointing at in this building, and are the early morning, dinner, and late night one team or three?

**Why it matters now:** 4.5 builds norms and a team environment for whatever this names; 2.2.1 put goals on units; the schedule, cross-training, and the uniform arc all follow from whether the periods share one labor pool. Get it wrong and the second period is staffed as a separate restaurant, which is the failure the white paper opens with (WP p. 02).

**Openers:**
- A runner who works the morning and dinner asks at the close whether she is on two teams or one. What do you tell her?
- Sideways: the peer room you opened added lunch and it went flat (WP p. 02). Picture the lunch roster on its first week. Who did they think they were part of?
- When you say "the team" to Dominic, what are you picturing: the whole house, the floor, or tonight's roster?
- The book's test is a shared mission of at least a year and lasting norms (p. 266). Name the smallest group here that passes it.

**Narrowing questions:**
- Is a domain a team or a lead's seats? If a team, does the kitchen need its own charter and norms separate from the floor's?
- What is the test that says a new period is the same restaurant and not a bolt-on? The earlier work proposed the charter's interface fields (cons. 1). Would you open a period whose charter names nothing it hands to dinner?
- What does "my team" mean in a lead's mouth, and "tonight's team" in the brief? Are you comfortable with the word doing three jobs?

**What the book says:** most so-called teams are individuals in parallel; a real team has a mission of a year or more, persistent jobs, and lasting structure; a team that has developed absorbs a new person without an overhaul (pp. 262 to 266).

**White-paper default:** one restaurant met at different times of day, not several concepts under one roof (WP p. 33, stated outright); a great night is the whole room in relationship (WP p. 08). Silent on the internal meaning of "team."

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Multi-period restaurants that keep one team usually keep one schedule and one training path across periods; those that split by period usually end up with a manager per period and a culture per period.

**Options:** (a) the house is the one team; domains and rosters are not teams; periods pass the interface test or do not open. Commits Sŏn to one labor pool and to 4.5 done once. (b) each domain is a team. Commits Sŏn to domain charters and a managed seam. (c) each period is a team. Commits Sŏn to the peer's pattern. Depth: cons. 1.

**Watch for:** answering "one team" as a value while the schedule, uniforms, and training quietly split by period; treating a period's charter as a team because it has goals.

**A finished answer:** the word "team" assigned to one unit for norms and culture, the periods named as one or several with the test that decides it, and the two other uses of the word ("my team," "tonight's team") named for what they are.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.1.3 Decide the reporting rule: one lead per seat, and what a second lead may read

**The question:** does any seat have two leads, and if a second lead's judgment must reach a seat's work, what may that lead do?

**Why it matters now:** the candidate sheet names one direct lead (WP p. 18); 2.2.5 assigns check-in holders; the Head of Beverage's outputs are read by the Operations Lead (costing, purchasing) and its people work is closer to the Maitre d. Without a rule, the bar's seats get two bosses by drift.

**Openers:**
- The bartender's inventory is off two weeks running. Who talks to her, and who is told?
- Sideways: at the peer room, who did the bar manager actually answer to on a Tuesday, and who wrote the review? Were they the same person?
- The book's dual reporting has both managers doing goals and reviews, separately (p. 275). Say out loud what that would feel like for a runner here.

**Narrowing questions:**
- What are the three things a lead holds for a seat (the earlier work said: the check-in, opening the seat, deciding the hire)? Is that the full list?
- What exactly may a second lead do: read a record, raise it at the leads' review, set a goal, hold a check-in? Where is the line?
- Does the rule survive the kitchen? A runner's pass work is read by the chef partner; is that a record or a line?

**What the book says:** solid and dotted lines are instruments of strategy; Stripe formalizes dual reporting in its systems with both managers doing goal-setting and reviews (pp. 265, 275 to 276).

**White-paper default:** authority lives in the domain, its owner is accountable to its outcomes (WP pp. 07 to 08, stated outright); the two leads are parallel (WP p. 10). Silent below the leads.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Matrix reporting in operating companies tends to work only where the secondary line's rights are written down; where they are implied, the person with two managers arbitrates between them.

**Options:** (a) one lead per seat, a second lead reads a record only. (b) a primary and a named secondary who may set a goal but not review. (c) the book's dual reporting for seats serving two domains. Each option's commitments: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** "it depends on the person," which is a line without a rule; a second lead given "input" with no definition of what input is.

**A finished answer:** one paragraph stating the count of leads per seat, what the lead holds, and what a second lead may and may not do, with the bar worked as the example.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.1.4 Decide who leads the floor's inward-facing strand

**The question:** runners and hosts do timing and sequence work; who holds their check-ins, opens their seats, and decides their hires?

**Why it matters now:** no runner or host seat can be opened until this is named (the old carryover 86akh7qvp). The ruling made the Maitre d master of the house; whether that includes the strand the customer never feels is the first structural consequence of the ruling.

**Openers:**
- The first runner you hire asks who her lead is. Answer in one name and then explain why.
- Sideways: describe a runner who was extraordinary in a room you worked. Who developed her, and who would have noticed if no one had?
- The Operations Lead's outcomes are labor efficiency, cost, compliance, vendors, facility (WP p. 10). Read that list aloud and ask whether "developing a runner" belongs on it.

**Narrowing questions:**
- Is the floor one domain with two strands, or two domains? The white paper says strands (WP p. 10).
- If the Maitre d holds both strands, what does the Operations Lead read about the inward strand, as a record?
- If someone else holds the inward strand, where does the seam sit on a Saturday at eight: between the server and the runner at the same table?

**What the book says:** horizontal teams cut across verticals; who leads them is a structural choice (pp. 271 to 272).

**White-paper default:** the Maitre d holds "the floor and the team," team development and training, and is accountable to team health (WP p. 10, stated outright); the inward strand's intelligence is described (WP pp. 10 to 11) but its lead is not named.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most rooms put runners and bussers under the service director or a floor manager, not under operations; the exceptions are rooms where expediting sits with the kitchen.

**Options:** (a) the Maitre d. Commits that seat to the largest load in the house and makes the room, door, and sequence designations load-bearing. (b) the Operations Lead. Commits a seat without people outcomes to check-ins and splits the floor. (c) a separate strand lead. Commits Sŏn to a line the count has no room for. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** choosing (a) without accepting what it does to the Maitre d's load (4.1.36 and the load reading); choosing (b) because the language matches, without asking who develops the person.

**A finished answer:** one seat named for runners and hosts, the reason, the consequence for that seat's load, and what the Operations Lead reads as a record.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.1.5 Decide whether the Head of Beverage is a seat and a leadership line

**The question:** is the Head of Beverage a standing seat that leads the bar's people, or a designation on a shift?

**Why it matters now:** the white paper says it three ways (WP pp. 10, 11, 22); the bar's seats need a lead before a bartender is hired; 2.2.5 needs to know whose check-in to place.

**Openers:**

- Sideways: the best bar you have stood behind. Who owned the list, and who owned the people? Same person?
- If the Head of Beverage is a designation, who opens a bartender seat?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Does the program need a standing owner between services, or can it be owned in the leads' review?
- If a seat and a line, who holds its check-in: the Maitre d (experience and team health), a founder, or the Operations Lead (costing)? Hand it to 2.2.5 if not settled here.
- Is it salaried under the test in 4.1.9?

**What the book says:** what a structure emphasizes should match what the strategy needs from that division (pp. 264 to 265).

**White-paper default:** three descriptions, none dominant (WP pp. 10, 11, 22). The earlier work read it as a seat and a line, checked in by the Maitre d, with the Operations Lead reading its outputs.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Beverage director and bar manager are almost always two people at this level; one seat holding both depends on the buying half living in software, which is the white paper's claim.

**Options:** (a) a seat and a line. (b) a seat in the floor's domain under the Maitre d. (c) a designation only. Commitments: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** deciding by the peer count (eight lines) rather than by what the bar's people need; leaving the program's execution designation on a shift undefined if (a) is chosen.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.1.7 Decide whether Sŏn has levels and ladders, and what carries a ladder's functions if not

**The question:** does anyone here have a level, and if not, what answers "how do I get promoted"?

**Why it matters now:** the candidate sheet promises an advancement path (WP p. 18); onboarding needs the "outward, not upward" answer written; 5.5 needs to know whether pay follows rungs or unlocks.

**Openers:**
- A server, eight months in and very good, asks what her next title is. What do you say?
- Sideways: name a restaurant where the ladder was real and one where it was a poster. What did people in the second one do about it?
- The kitchen has two tiers by name (WP p. 11). Is that a level, or a kind?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The five things a ladder does: show the next step, mark seniority, price progression, confer authority, answer the promotion question. For each, what carries it here?
- Can a single house keep the promise a ladder implies? If not, is that an argument against ladders or for honesty about them?
- Is a designation held often a kind of seniority? Should it be visible?

**What the book says:** levels, titles, and layers are the ordinary instruments; add a layer when reports outgrow bandwidth (pp. 280 to 281).

**White-paper default:** no altitude, only reach; progression is connections; competency requirements are visible to anyone (WP pp. 09, 11, stated outright).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Station-based progression in kitchens and skill-certified pay in some open-book restaurants do the ladder's pricing work without titles; they fail when no one owns the record.

**Options:** (a) none; five carriers named. (b) two tiers everywhere. (c) small levels. Commitments: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** refusing levels while the pay table (5.5) quietly has rungs; a designation that is a title in all but name.

**A finished answer:** the level question answered, a carrier named for each of the five functions, and the two-sentence promotion answer written.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.1.10 Define cross-strand fluency and cross-period fluency so each can be demonstrated

**The question:** what would a lead have to see, on the record, to say a person holds both strands, or both periods?

**Openers:**

### 4.1.11 Decide who schedules, scores, and records an advancement assessment, including across domains

**The question:** the white paper says advancement never waits on a manager (p. 11) and that the person is signed off by a lead (p. 19). Which is it, and who does what?

### 4.1.12 Decide how a structure change is made and how it reaches people

**The question:** when a seat is created or retired, a line changes, or a designation's rule changes, what has to be written, who agrees, and how does the affected person hear?

**Why it matters now:** the book says to tell people up front that the structure will change (p. 265); candidates should hear how. The first entries are this session's own outputs.

**Openers:**
- Six months in, you retire the sequence designation because the stack absorbed it. Walk me through who hears, in what order.
- Sideways: the worst restructure you lived through. What was said first, and what should have been?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Is the reason always structural (a tension lost, a class unowned, a gate, a charter), never a person?
- Proposed at the leads' review, finalized by the partners: is that the path?
- Four channels (check-in, seat sheet, brief, team home) and no announcement: does 2.2.11 allow it?
- Do you keep the book's one-to-one message for anyone a change moves?

**What the book says:** sketch without names, narrate, consider people, message each affected person with a monitoring plan, then communicate broadly (pp. 280 to 281).

**White-paper default:** fix the process, not the person; knowledge lives in the system (WP pp. 07 to 08, 17). No process given.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Organizations that version their structure (a dated entry per change) find the entry's "reason" line is what keeps a change from being about a person.

**Options:** (a) the entry process with the book's one-to-one message kept. (b) the book's process with the partners as the leader. (c) entries for seats and lines only. Commitments: cons. 9.

**Watch for:** a change that reaches the brief before it reaches the person; "care" fixes accumulating into a structure no one versioned.

**A finished answer:** the classes of change, the reason rule, the path, the channels, and the person-affected rule, each accepted or amended.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.1.20 Decide what a per-service designation is, what it may hold, and the rotation floor

**The question:** what is a designation allowed to carry, what may it never carry, who is eligible, and how do you keep it from becoming one person's job?

### 4.1.21 Decide the room designation, and how the threshold is held when the Maitre d is not working

**The question:** on the services the Maitre d does not work, who holds the room, and is the threshold a post the Maitre d stands at or a function someone holds per service?

**Why it matters now:** the ruling made one seat the room's owner; one seat cannot be present every open hour once the morning and late night open (WP p. 22); every "manager" the white paper mentions in the plural (pouring tableside, reviewing incidents, WP pp. 13, 26) has to resolve to someone on the night.

**Openers:**
- Tuesday, the Maitre d's night off, a table's wrong-order recovery exceeds a server's range. Who does the server turn to, and what may that person do?
- Sideways: the maitre d at the threshold is the picture in the white paper. Picture the Maitre d at the threshold at eight on a Saturday while a section is in trouble. Where should she be?
- What does the room's holder on a Tuesday explicitly not get to do?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The room: coverage, escalation destination, the transition trigger, emergency authority, and nothing else. Five functions; six refusals (no check-ins, no hiring, no advancement, and so on). Agreed?
- The door on a host seat, with the Maitre d there in person when free: is the threshold a register or a post?
- The sequence on a runner seat as the floor's side of the pass: needed, or is it the room's holder's?
- Who can hold the room: which seats, with what readiness?

**What the book says:** a horizontal function must be held every hour it runs; the leader's bandwidth is finite (pp. 271 to 272, 280).

**White-paper default:** the Maitre d holds the floor and is freed from the host stand; the Lead Host owns the door (WP p. 10, stated outright); roughly eight lines run the day (WP p. 22); node overload is the named failure (WP p. 14). The threshold-as-register reading departs from the literal picture and its customer-facing wording is the naming session's (WP p. 15).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Rooms with one salaried floor lead and more than one service period name a shift lead per service; the question is always whether the assignment carries permanence.

**Options:** (a) room, door, and sequence as three designations. (b) room only. (c) a second standing floor seat. Commitments: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** the room's holder becoming a manager by accretion; a door holder who is really the Maitre d's stand-in for everything.

**A finished answer:** the room designation's functions and refusals listed, the door and sequence accepted or folded, the Maitre d's presence at the threshold named as a register or a post, and the customer-facing wording handed on.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.1.22 Decide who calls a mid-service failure that spans the floor and the kitchen

**The question:** when one call has to cover both the room and the kitchen in the same minute, who makes it?

**Why it matters now:** each side has an owner; the intersection does not; training services will hit this before opening does.

**Openers:**
- A section's tickets are lost and two tables are already waiting past the range. The kitchen wants to refire; the room wants to comp and move on. Who decides, in the next thirty seconds?
- Sideways: at the opening you ran, who made that call, and did the kitchen know it was theirs to lose?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Each owner calls their own side; the room's owner decides when one call must cover both; the kitchen's owner is told immediately. Which of those would the chef partner push back on?
- The Operations Lead is not called mid-service; founders after service, never during. Agreed?
- What is the "told immediately" channel?

**What the book says:** the hinge is where authority pivots between two structures; place it deliberately (pp. 272 to 273).

**White-paper default:** each side's owner is named (WP p. 10); dinner is what everything protects (WP p. 33). Silent on the joint call.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most rooms give the floor the call on customer-facing recovery and the kitchen the call on food; the fight is over which a failure is, and the rule that settles it is usually who faces the customer.

**Options:** (a) the room's owner decides, the kitchen's told. (b) the originating side decides. (c) whoever holds emergency authority for the service decides. Commitments: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** a rule that requires diagnosing origin in the moment; an entry with no range, only an owner.

**A finished answer:** a decision-rights entry (owner, range, triggers) ready for the register, with Brandon's position recorded and the entry marked pending the chef partner.

**Needs agreement from:** the chef partner; both seated founders.

### 4.1.28 Decide who holds an event in the room

### 4.1.29 Decide where the overnight cleaning crew sits in the structure

### 4.1.30 Decide the temporary structures Sŏn allows

**The question:** what are Sŏn's forms of the book's project and working group, and how does each end?

**Openers:** the group that drafts the late-night charter: who is in it, and on what day does it stop existing?

**Narrowing questions:** the process change (one owner, a date, closed when versioned) as the project? The charter group (leads and partners, dissolved when ruled) as the working group? What happens when either runs past its date? Any third form for pre-opening workstreams (2.3.5)?

### 4.1.31 Decide how a live existential risk is run

**The question:** when a risk on the register goes live, what changes, and what deliberately does not?

**Openers:** the food-safety sensor platform fails its audit two weeks before opening. Who owns that today, what is written every evening, and who reads it first tomorrow?

**Narrowing questions:** a founder owner, a written closing condition, a daily state, read first at the leads' review: yes? Each of the Code Yellow's powers (shielding, trade-off resourcing, dedicated space, convening leadership, longer hours, all-hands updates, a cap, approval to trigger): keep or refuse, one by one. Never into service hours?

**What the book says:** the Code Yellow, pp. 268 to 270. **White-paper default:** bandwidth is health, burnout is a system failure, the stack is deterministic, no special powers (WP pp. 12, 16 to 17, 21). **How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Owner, exit condition, time cap are the three that survive everywhere; the powers vary. **Options:** discipline without powers; the Code Yellow adapted; no mode. Commitments: cons. 3. **Watch for:** "longer hours" arriving as dedication. **A finished answer:** trigger, owner class, record, reader, closing rule, and each power's fate. **Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.1.33 Decide when the structure is reexamined

**The question:** on what occasions is the whole structure read again, and what is read?

**Openers:** the book says at least yearly (p. 265). Between dinner opening and late night opening, how many times will the structure have changed?

**Narrowing questions:** every gate and every reset, yearly after? What is read: the inventory, the line list, the load reading, the variety test's last pass? What sentence do candidates hear about it?

**What the book says:** yearly; too often destabilizes, too rarely drifts (p. 265). **White-paper default:** the structure changes most at period openings, which are gated (WP pp. 35 to 36). **How others have handled it:** nothing useful beyond the book's own caution. **Options:** gates and resets; yearly with gates as exceptions; entries only. Commitments: cons. 2. **Watch for:** a reexamination that is a reorganization by another name. **A finished answer:** the occasions, the reading list, and the candidate's sentence. **Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.1.36 Decide how a lead's load is relieved, and when a leadership line is added

**The question:** when a lead's check-in load or live load exceeds what the seat can hold, what gives first, and what would ever justify a new line?

**Why it matters now:** the Maitre d's seat, if it holds both strands, is the one the earlier work flagged; the book's answer is a layer, which the model refuses; without an order of relief, the answer on the night will be a manager by feel.

**Openers:**
- The Maitre d has twenty-two check-ins on a fortnight and three nights holding the room herself. What do you take off her first?
- Sideways: the peer room had fourteen leadership positions around one dinner (WP p. 22). Pick the one that got added last and guess why.

**Narrowing questions:**
- The order: stack, designations, longer check-in interval after training, then a line beside. Right order? Anything missing?
- A new line only when 2.2.36 shows a class resolving to nothing or to a founder. Would you ever add one on load alone?
- Beside, never above: is that absolute?
- If a line is added, do you run the book's method (sketch, narrative, people, one-to-one, monitoring)?

**What the book says:** avoid "I" structures and overload alike; add a layer when needed, by strategy, narrative, people, communication (pp. 280 to 281).

**White-paper default:** reach not altitude; bandwidth is the measure; the stack absorbs integration; node overload is fixed by restoring the separation (WP pp. 09, 12, 14, 21).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. The first manager added to a thin restaurant is almost always added on a bad month rather than on a finding; a written trigger is the only known counter.

**Options:** (a) ordered relief, a line only on the test, beside. (b) the book's layer at a stated ceiling. (c) split the domain instead. Commitments: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** a check-in interval lengthened until the check-in is a formality; a "line beside" that everyone treats as above.

**A finished answer:** the order of relief, the trigger for a line, the beside rule, and whether the book's method is kept for that case.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
