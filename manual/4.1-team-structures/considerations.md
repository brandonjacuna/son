# 4.1 Team structures: considerations

What the white paper holds, what the earlier work found worth keeping, the options and what each commits Sŏn to, and where Sŏn may diverge from the book. Topics follow the book's order (pp. 261 to 281). "WP p. N" is the white paper; "cons. N" is a section of this file. Nothing here is a decision.

Vocabulary used throughout, in plain words: a **seat** is a position in the structure; a **domain** is a set of seats with one lead; a **strand** is the outward-facing or inward-facing half of a domain (WP pp. 10 to 11); a **leadership line** is a seat that owns a domain or a program across every open service period; a **designation** is a live function assigned to a person for one service (WP p. 10); the **stack** is the software.

## 1. What counts as a team

**Book.** Most groups called teams are individuals working in parallel; a team is a group with a shared mission of at least a year, persistent jobs, and lasting norms and culture, and the milestone is working as one entity (pp. 262, 265 to 266). Team development is a set of habits kept up over time, and a team that has developed can absorb a new person without an overhaul (pp. 262 to 263).

**White paper.** One restaurant, one brand, "met at different times of day," not several concepts sharing an address (WP p. 33). A great night is "distributed intelligence," every element in relationship with every other (WP p. 08). The three core periods are early morning, dinner, and late night; dinner opens first and runs alone for at least a quarter; lunch and brunch are held in reserve (WP pp. 35 to 36). A peer's added periods "stayed flat against dinner" because the restaurant underneath was built for one hour (WP p. 02). Silent on what "team" means internally.

**From the earlier work.** Nothing smaller than the house meets the book's definition. A domain is a lead's seats, not a group with its own culture; a strand is a kind of intelligence and output; a service period has a charter and goals but no people of its own; a roster is who is on tonight and dissolves at close. The house's word "team" therefore lands three ways: the house (the collective), the domain ("my team" when a lead says it), and the roster ("tonight's team" at the brief). Whether the periods are one team or several was the earlier work's central question, and it proposed a test rather than an assertion: a period is the same restaurant when its charter names what it hands to and draws from the other periods and something actually consumes what it provides. A charter that fails the test is not opened. Consequences if the periods are one team: one schedule from one labor pool, cross-training as a fluency marker rather than a transfer, one uniform arc, one register that loosens across the day rather than changes.

**Options.**
- (a) The house is the one team; domains and rosters are not teams. Commits Sŏn to building norms and culture once (4.5), to one labor pool across periods, and to the interface test at every period's gate.
- (b) Each domain is a team, the house is the company. Commits Sŏn to domain-level norms and charters (2.1), a cross-domain seam that must be managed deliberately, and the risk the book names for teams with many dependencies (p. 266).
- (c) Each service period is a team. Commits Sŏn to separate rosters, separate culture, and the peer's failure the white paper opens with (WP p. 02).

**Divergence.** The book expects several teams inside a company; Sŏn may have exactly one in the book's sense, with the book's team-building chapters (4.2 to 4.8) applied to the house rather than to departments.

## 2. Structure follows strategy, and stays flexible

**Book.** Structure exists to arrange resources for the goals; start from the plan (Chapter 2). Preserve optionality; tell people up front that the structure will change and that they will shift portfolios; reexamine at least once a year, since too often destabilizes and too rarely lets the structure drift from the business or the talent (pp. 264 to 265).

**White paper.** The structure changes most when a period opens, and the opening is phased and gated: dinner alone for at least a quarter, then the other core periods "once dinner is steady," lunch not revisited for a year and decided equally by all three partners (WP pp. 35 to 36). "Authority lives in the domain" (WP p. 07); the failure mode when the structure blurs is node overload, and "the fix is restoring the Operations Lead and Maitre d separation" (WP p. 14).

**From the earlier work.** The structure gets its flexibility from two places the book does not have: per-service designations (who holds the room, the door, the sequence changes nightly) and the period gates. Between gates, the structure changes only by a versioned entry with its reason stated first (cons. 9). Reexamination happens at every gate and at each mechanism reset (2.3.30), which is more often than yearly while periods are opening and yearly after.

**Options for the cadence (4.1.33).**
- (a) At every gate and every reset. Commits the leads' review and the reset to carrying the inventory, the line list, and the load reading as standing items.
- (b) Yearly, per the book, with gates as exceptions. Commits Sŏn to one heavy annual pass and lighter gates.
- (c) Continuous, by entry only, no scheduled pass. Commits Sŏn to trusting the entry process to surface drift, which the book warns against.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Restaurants that add a service period commonly find the structure that ran dinner does not run the morning, and either add a manager per period or let the morning run thin; the white paper's own peer case is the first pattern (WP pp. 33 to 34).

## 3. Teams, projects, working groups, and the Code Yellow

**Book.** Three constructs: a team (a year or more, persistent jobs), a project (weeks to months, a subgroup inside a team, ends when done), a working group (under three months, cross-team, needs a directly responsible individual, a scope, and a spin-down because it has no permanent owner). Before creating one, ask the objective, the skills, and the duration; the worst outcome is a temporary structure that persists, under-resourced and confusing the permanent teams (pp. 265 to 267). The Code Yellow is Stripe's special mode for an urgent risk: a shielded working group, resourced by painful trade-offs, daily standups, dedicated space, power to convene leadership, longer hours, all-hands updates, a cap around ten weeks, leadership approval to trigger (pp. 268 to 270).

**White paper.** Temporary work appears as process change ("the process that allowed the failure gets updated," WP p. 13) and as the opening of each period. Bandwidth is the measure of health, burnout is a system failure not a character flaw (WP pp. 12, 16 to 17), and the stack is deterministic so that nothing the service depends on rests on a person or on the AI layer (WP p. 21). No special powers exist for anyone.

**From the earlier work.** The book's project becomes a process change with a single owner and a date, closed when versioned. The working group becomes the charter-drafting group for a new period (the leads and the partners), dissolved when the charter is ruled. Refused: any standing team smaller than the house, a group that outlives its purpose, a project that quietly becomes a seat. For the Code Yellow: keep the discipline, refuse the powers. A risk from the register (2.2.26) gets a founder owner, a written closing condition, and a daily state, read first at the leads' review until closed. Against the powers: no decision-rights entry changes because a risk is live; pulling a lead onto a risk full-time is itself node overload; longer hours and weekends conflict with the labor commitments (WP pp. 16 to 17); there is no all-hands to update; a live risk does not cross into service hours.

**Options for temporary structures (4.1.30).**
- (a) Two forms only, the process change and the charter group, each with an owner, a life, and an end. Commits Sŏn to naming an owner and a date for every piece of temporary work.
- (b) Allow ad hoc working groups for pre-opening workstreams too (2.3.5 assigns owning partners). Commits Sŏn to the book's full working-group discipline, including spin-down, for the build.

**Options for a live risk (4.1.31).**
- (a) Discipline without powers, as above. Commits a founder to a daily written state and the leads' review to reading it first.
- (b) The Code Yellow as written, adapted. Commits Sŏn to a trigger with partner approval, a cap, a shielded owner, and the trade-offs the book describes.
- (c) No formal mode; the register and the house review suffice. Commits Sŏn to catching a live risk at the review's cadence only.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Incident and "war room" modes in operating companies tend to survive on three things: a named owner, a written exit condition, and a hard time cap. The powers vary; the cap is what keeps the mode from becoming the structure.

## 4. Functional or business-line, vertical or horizontal

**Book.** Early companies organize by function; vertical teams own a region or product area, horizontal teams cut across verticals (analytics, ops platform). A true business unit has its own product, customer, and independence, which is rare; where a company wants one leader accountable per product while centralizing distribution and support, it designs a hybrid and places the hinge, the point where focus turns from the product to the customer, usually product marketing (pp. 270 to 273).

**White paper.** Two axes exist: domains (the floor, the bar, the kitchen, the operating architecture) and service periods, with "different first customers at different times" (WP p. 35). Neither axis has a manager at its intersection. The management layer is two parallel leads, "co-leads of different systems serving the same center," and bundling them into a general manager is "the load-bearing model's final failure mode" (WP p. 10). The customer-facing pivot is a strand inside every domain (WP pp. 10 to 11).

**From the earlier work.** The domains are the functional axis, the periods are the business-line axis, and the structure is neither, because a period is a charter rather than a unit with a leader. The business unit is refused outright: a period that "can operate independently" is the separate-restaurant failure the interface test exists to catch (cons. 1). The hinge team is refused; the pivot toward the customer is the outward-facing strand in every domain, and the white paper's own hinge is a room, not a team (the bar at the dinner-to-late-night transition). The business lead's surviving functions map to the periods' joint goal-setting by the Maitre d, the Operations Lead, and the chef partner (2.2.1); forums are the brief and the close; headcount and dollar accounting touch figures and belong in Dominic's domain, not this manual.

**Where the seam actually lives.** Because no one manages the intersection, three seams need an owner in service: the floor's side of the kitchen pass (the earlier work's "sequence" designation on a runner seat), the room when the Maitre d is absent (the "room" designation), and the one call that must cover both the floor and the kitchen at once (4.1.22). The earlier work's rule for the last: each owner calls their own side; when one call must cover both, the room's owner decides because "dinner is what everything protects" (WP p. 33), the kitchen's owner is told immediately, the Operations Lead is not called mid-service, and founders are reached after service. This needs the chef partner's agreement.

**Options for the mid-service call (4.1.22).**
- (a) The room's owner decides, the kitchen's owner is told. Commits the room designation to carrying this on nights the Maitre d is absent, and the kitchen to accepting a floor-side call in the moment.
- (b) The kitchen's owner decides when the failure originates in the kitchen, the room's owner otherwise. Commits both sides to agreeing, in the moment, where a failure originated.
- (c) Whoever holds emergency authority for the service (2.2.7) decides. Commits 2.2.7 to naming one person, not one per side.

**Divergence.** The book's hybrid places authority in a team between product and customer; Sŏn places it in a strand within every domain and in per-service designations at the seams. The book's business lead has a ladder and a budget; Sŏn's periods have a charter and joint goals.

## 5. Reporting lines, leadership lines, and the two open seats

**Book.** Solid and dotted lines are instruments of strategy; Stripe's dual reporting gives a person a primary and a secondary manager, both recorded, both doing goals and reviews, with separate 1:1s (pp. 265, 275 to 276). Business leads are evaluated on their business's outcomes and held to the same people-manager bar (pp. 277 to 278).

**White paper.** "Authority lives in the domain. The person who owns the domain is accountable to its outcomes" (WP pp. 07 to 08). The two leads are parallel, "neither reports to the other" (WP p. 10). The candidate sheet names "their direct lead" (WP p. 18). The Head of Beverage is described three ways: a per-shift designation (p. 10), the owner of the program across two strands (p. 11), and the seat a wine manager and a bar manager collapse into (p. 22). The floor's inward-facing strand has a described intelligence but no named lead (pp. 10 to 11). The Operations Lead's outcomes (labor efficiency, cost, compliance, vendors, facility) contain no people-development function; the Maitre d's do (team development, training, team health) (WP p. 10).

**From the earlier work.** Refuse solid and dotted lines: every seat has exactly one lead who holds the check-in, opens the seat, and decides the hire; a second lead whose judgment must reach the work reads a record and never holds a check-in or a decision about the person. Reasoning: any second line with review or assignment power reintroduces a person standing above another. The Head of Beverage is read as a seat and a line, the bar's domain lead, because a nightly designation cannot open seats or hold check-ins; its check-in held by the Maitre d, whose outcomes the program serves, with the Operations Lead reading its costing and purchasing as a record. The floor's inward strand is read as the Maitre d's, because the floor is one domain with two strands, the ruling already made the Maitre d master of the house, and a separate strand lead is a floor-manager equivalent the leadership count has no room for (WP p. 22). The leadership-line list that results, without inventing a count: the Operations Lead, the Maitre d, the chef partner's seat, the Head of Beverage, with Station Leads pending the chef partner. Two peer positions in the white paper's arithmetic (an events team, overnight cleaning) collapse into nothing it names (cons. 8).

**Options for the reporting rule (4.1.3).**
- (a) One lead per seat; a second lead reads a record only. Commits Sŏn to routing every cross-domain need for a person's work through a record and the leads' review, never a second check-in.
- (b) One primary lead, with a named secondary who may set a goal but not review. Commits Sŏn to a lighter form of the book's dual reporting and to recording it.
- (c) The book's dual reporting where a seat's output serves two domains. Commits Sŏn to two check-ins for those seats and to the HR-system recording the book describes.

**Options for the inward strand's lead (4.1.4).**
- (a) The Maitre d leads the whole floor. Commits that seat to the largest check-in load in the house and makes the room, door, and sequence designations load-bearing on every service (cons. 7).
- (b) The Operations Lead leads the inward strand. Commits a seat with no people-development outcomes to holding check-ins, and splits the floor into two domains with a seam between servers and runners.
- (c) A separate strand-lead seat. Commits Sŏn to a line the white paper's count does not include and to explaining why it is not a floor manager.

**Options for the Head of Beverage (4.1.5).**
- (a) A seat and a line. Commits Sŏn to a salaried bar lead from the first bar hire and a fourth check-in holder question (2.2.5).
- (b) A seat inside the floor's domain, led by the Maitre d, with program ownership. Commits the Maitre d to the bar's check-ins and leaves the program's execution designation intact.
- (c) A designation only, as WP p. 10 literally reads. Commits the bar's seats to another lead and the program to no standing owner.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Restaurants at this level almost always separate the beverage director (program, buying, list) from the bar manager (the bar's shifts and people); the white paper's claim is that software collapses the buying half so one seat can hold both (WP p. 22). Whether one person can also hold the bar's people work every open period is the question the option leaves to the load reading (cons. 7).

## 6. Levels, ladders, entry, and salaried seats

**Book.** Levels, titles, and layers are the book's ordinary instruments; adding a layer is "often necessary" in growth; consider who is "scaling to the call" (pp. 280 to 281).

**White paper.** "The web never acquires altitude, only reach. New people always enter at the outside edge" (WP p. 09), written without an exception for leads. "Progression develops connections, not altitude"; every position has documented competency requirements visible to anyone; the absence of a clear path is "where politics begin" (WP p. 11). Each person is "at the center of their own knowledge map" (WP p. 11). The kitchen has two title tiers (WP pp. 11, 22). "Leadership and salaried roles" are named together and never listed (WP p. 18). Cross-strand fluency is "the real development marker," recognized in pay, with the pay architecture an open item (WP pp. 11, 15). Advancement has two accounts: the practical "auto-schedules with the appropriate domain lead" and no one's advancement "waits on a manager's attention or judgment" (WP p. 11); the person "schedules a competency conversation with the appropriate lead and are signed off" (WP p. 19).

**From the earlier work.**
- No levels, no ladders. The five things a ladder does (show the next step, mark seniority, price progression, confer authority, answer "how do I get promoted") are carried by a visible next step on each seat's sheet, connections on the person's page, pay moves tied to recorded unlocks (5.5), a designation as the only elevation and only for a shift, and a written "outward, not upward" answer in onboarding. The caution behind it: a single house cannot keep the promise a ladder implies.
- Two webs. A lead's seat sits at the center of the house's web because a seat's position is the seat's, not the person's; the lead personally starts at zero on their own map. This reconciles "everyone enters at the edge" with hiring a lead, but the white paper's sentence has no exception, so it is Brandon's to accept or amend.
- Salaried test. A seat is salaried when it owns a domain or a program across every open period rather than being scheduled shift by shift. Under it: the two leads, the chef partner's seat, the Head of Beverage; Station Leads pending the chef partner; no edge seat; no designation. Pricing is 5.5's and financials are not a source here. Counsel should confirm the test squares with the rules that govern salaried status where Sŏn operates.
- Fluency, defined so it can be demonstrated: certified on both strands (a passed readiness assessment on a seat in each strand, scored by that strand's own assessor), exercised on both within a recent window, and "held at once" evidenced by shift-close records of a coordination failure absorbed without visible disruption; cross-period fluency the same way for a second period. Recorded as dated connections on the person's page, never a score or a title, and never a placement tool. Cross-period fluency is the leading indicator of who is nearest when a new period's seats open.
- Advancement as three roles, until the founders choose between the white paper's two sentences: the system schedules when the training window elapses; a trained assessor from a pool, never the person's own mentor, scores; the domain lead records the form at the next check-in and may not override either way. Across domains, the receiving domain's lead records on its own assessors' scoring, and the original lead reads the outcome. This mirrors the separation of assessor from decider the white paper uses in hiring (WP p. 18).

**Options for levels (4.1.7).**
- (a) None; the five functions carried as above. Commits Sŏn to keeping the seat sheets, the person page, and the pay-unlock table (5.5) current, because they are doing the ladder's work.
- (b) Two tiers everywhere, matching the kitchen (owner and execution). Commits Sŏn to a title tier on the floor and the bar and to explaining why it is not altitude.
- (c) The book's levels, kept small. Commits Sŏn to a promise of upward movement a single house may not be able to keep.

**Options for the advancement roles (4.1.11).**
- (a) System schedules, assessor scores, lead records, no override. Commits Sŏn to assessor pools in every domain before anyone can advance, and to leads accepting a result they did not produce.
- (b) The lead assesses and signs off (WP p. 19 as written). Commits advancement to a lead's attention, which WP p. 11 says it never waits on.
- (c) Assessor scores, lead may hold a "ready" for a stated structural reason (no open seat, a flag) but never reverse it. Commits Sŏn to writing the reasons a hold is allowed.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Skill-based progression without titles is common in kitchens (station by station) and in some cooperative and open-book restaurants, where pay follows certified stations rather than rank; the recurring weakness is that the certification lapses when no one owns the record. That is the argument for the currency window and the person page.

**Divergence.** The book assumes levels and treats layering as normal growth. Sŏn may refuse both, which means the book's "scaling to the call" becomes designations held more often and a place in the ready-now pool (WP p. 11), not a new title.

## 7. Spans, load, and layering

**Book.** Avoid "I" structures (one leader with one or two reports) and avoid so many reports that no bandwidth is left; land "adequately flat, but with appropriate bandwidth"; when a layer is needed, sketch the structure without names, write the narrative first, consider who is scaling to the call, message each affected person one-to-one with a monitoring plan, and remind everyone that further evolution is likely (pp. 280 to 281).

**White paper.** Bandwidth is the metabolic rate and the first health measure (WP p. 12). Node overload is "one domain owner absorbing load that belongs to adjacent domains," fixed by restoring the separation (WP p. 14). The stack absorbs the integration work a person would otherwise hold (WP p. 21). "Roughly eight leadership lines running the full span of the day" (WP p. 22) cannot mean one seat present every open hour. The white paper does not say what "management" means in the plural when it describes managers pouring tableside or reviewing incidents (WP pp. 13, 26).

**From the earlier work.** Nothing reports to anything, so span is read as check-in load per lead: count times interval, with the Maitre d's rotation the one load that can exceed its budget once the inward strand is theirs (cons. 5). The count is unknown until period charters set seat counts; read it at the first reset. Layering is refused (growth is reach): relief comes first from the stack, then from designations, then from lengthening the check-in interval after training (2.3.10), then from a line added beside the existing ones, never above; a line is added only when the variety test (2.2.36) shows a disturbance class resolving to nothing or to a founder by default. The load test on the Maitre d's seat found three functions that go unheld in the Maitre d's absent hours, each resolved by a designation rather than a second seat: the room (coverage, escalation, the transition trigger, emergency authority only), the door (a host seat holding the threshold per service, with the Maitre d there in person when free), and the sequence (a runner seat owning the floor's side of the pass). Reading the threshold as a register rather than a fixed post departs from the white paper's literal picture of the maitre d at the door and its customer-facing wording belongs to the naming session (WP p. 15). A fourth finding is a system one: a stack outage mid-service has no fallback and lands on the Maitre d's memory, hence a printed degraded-mode set and one training service run with the stack off.

**Options for the room and the threshold (4.1.21).**
- (a) Room, door, and sequence as three designations. Commits every brief to naming three people from eligible seats and the rotation floor to being real.
- (b) Room only; the door and the sequence stay with the room's holder. Commits the room's holder to the threshold and the pass at once, which is the load the ruling was meant to relieve.
- (c) A second standing floor seat for the Maitre d's absent hours. Commits Sŏn to the floor-manager line the ruling removed.

**Options for relief and adding a line (4.1.36).**
- (a) The ordered relief above; a new line only on the variety test, beside not above. Commits Sŏn to running 2.2.36 honestly and to accepting a thin period rather than adding a manager by feel.
- (b) The book's layering when a lead's load exceeds a stated ceiling. Commits Sŏn to a number for the ceiling and to a layer, which the white paper's model refuses.
- (c) Relief by splitting a domain (two floor domains) rather than adding a layer. Commits Sŏn to a seam through the floor and to deciding which lead holds the room.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Restaurants that run more than one service period with one salaried floor lead almost always name a shift lead per service; the difference between that and a designation is whether the assignment carries pay, title, and permanence. The old work's rotation floor exists because the informal version (the same person, every time) is how a designation becomes a title without anyone deciding it.

## 8. What the structure needs that the book does not cover

**The seat inventory.** Three sources disagreed on what seats exist; no seat can open, be hired for, or be trained against without a row, and the candidate sheet needs a direct lead per seat (WP p. 18). The earlier work's reconciled floor and bar rows: Operations Lead, Maitre d, Head of Beverage, server, host, runner, bartender, plus the chef partner's seat and the Steward ("not a support role," WP p. 11). "Lead Host" is a designation on the host seat (WP p. 10). An early-morning role has no row until that charter is ruled. Beyond the basics, a row carries the designations the seat may hold, assessor and mentor pool marks per strand, and a salaried mark; pools are marks on rows and entries on the person's page, never seats. Ownership: the founders' review owns it, the Operations Lead holds the record, each lead proposes rows; contract roles in the build need naming so they do not default to Brandon by omission. Pre-opening specialist contract work is a real class of seat the inventory should not ignore.

**The kitchen's spine.** Everything below a shared spine is the chef partner's. The spine the earlier work proposed: one kitchen domain, one lead; two tiers; the Steward a full seat with its own materials; a sheet before any kitchen seat opens; kitchen emergency authority in service (2.2.7); a pass owner named every service; kitchen goals in one shared set with the floor. Fourteen items are collected for the chef partner's own packet (4.1.17). Nothing in this chunk should read as pre-deciding them.

**Two unowned functions.** The white paper's leadership arithmetic collapses a peer's events team without naming who inherits events, while space activation and hosting events are named as ways cultural equity and customer relationships are built (WP pp. 22, 28, 31). The earlier work's reading: an event is a service under an existing period's charter with its own brief, held by that service's room-holder. Options: the room-holder; a standing designation for events; a seat, once volume warrants, added through 4.1.12. Separately, an overnight cleaning crew is contracted "at the same cost" so servers can do relationship work at close (WP p. 26), but has no owner, range, or lead, while facility integrity (Operations Lead) and the kitchen environment (Steward) are owned. Options: a seat class in the inventory; a vendor with a written range and a named reader; the Steward's domain extended. The crew sits outside the team's compensation pool, which 5.5 should weigh; no figure belongs here.

**The web rendering.** The structure "maps as a web" and its rendering "as a designed organizational artifact rather than a chart" is an open item (WP pp. 09, 15); the candidate sheet carries "the org chart" (WP p. 18), which under this model must not be a chart. This chunk supplies the content list; the visual design and its owner are outside this manual. Until it exists, a list will serve the candidate sheet.

**The structure narrative.** The book's step two, the narrative before the announcement (p. 281), is the one piece of the book's method the earlier work never produced. The white paper's account is written for an investor. A one-page team-facing version, in plain words, is where a line cook learns why there is no general manager and how they advance, and where the founders find the gaps before a candidate does.

## 9. Changing the structure

**Book.** Sketch without names, narrate, consider people, message each affected person one-to-one (acknowledge the loss, explain the strategy, say how it will be monitored), then communicate broadly; remind everyone that evolution is normal (pp. 280 to 281). The workbook's unblocking process escalates a disagreement up two chains to a shared manager.

**White paper.** Knowledge lives in the system; the process is fixed, not the person blamed; post-mortems are private and process-focused (WP pp. 07 to 08, 17). No manager stands above the two leads (WP p. 10).

**From the earlier work.** Every structure change is one versioned entry with its reason stated first, in structural terms; proposed through the leads' review, finalized by the partners; reaching people through the affected person's check-in, a new version of the seat's sheet, the next brief, and the team home, never an announcement. A reduction's reason is never a person; a held seat is never retired as a retirement but handled as a departure or a reduction (5.10). The book's escalation up two chains is refused, since there is no shared manager, but its form survives as the hold's half-page (2.2.3): the problem, the options, and the trade-offs written jointly, no founder hearing one side until both have written, and a time window.

**Options (4.1.12).**
- (a) The entry process as above, with the book's one-to-one message kept for any change that moves a person. Commits Sŏn to writing before changing and to four channels per change.
- (b) The book's process as written, with the partners as the leader. Commits Sŏn to announcement as the broad step, which the operating system (2.2.11) may or may not allow.
- (c) Entries for seats and lines only; designation and pool changes by the lead's note. Commits Sŏn to two classes of change and a line between them.

## 10. Where Sŏn may diverge from the book, in one place

- One team, the house, rather than teams per function (cons. 1).
- No solid or dotted lines; one lead per seat, records for the rest (cons. 5).
- No hinge team, no business units, no business leads; strands and designations at the seams (cons. 4).
- No levels, ladders, or layers; designations as the only elevation, for one shift (cons. 6, 7).
- Reexamination at gates and resets rather than yearly (cons. 2).
- The Code Yellow's discipline without its powers (cons. 3).
- The book's change process kept in substance, delivered through the operating system's channels rather than an announcement (cons. 9).
