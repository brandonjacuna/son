# 5.4 The formal review process: working session

## 1. Orientation

This chunk decides Sŏn's formal review: what it is for, what it reads, who holds it, what it writes, and what, if anything, it feeds. The book (pp. 399 to 413) wants a written review on a rhythm, a way to compare assessments across managers, and a link from performance to pay. The white paper has already committed Sŏn to a review every three to six months that is about "development, growth, and the path forward rather than a verdict," never contains new information, and runs in both directions (WP p. 19). It says nothing about a rating, a reviewer, calibration, or a link to pay.

Upstream, a lot is already fixed. The check-in and its intervals (2.3.9, 2.3.10) carry the running conversation. The person page (2.3.11) holds no rating and no pay figure. Who holds each lead's check-in and review is 2.2.5's. Every hire is read on a fixed list (3.1.5, 3.2.2). There are no levels or ladders (4.1.7), and advancement has its own three seats (4.1.11). The ninety-day plan (3.3.10) and the career conversation (4.4.3, 4.6.17) come before the first review. 5.1 decided whether a coaching hypothesis is written anywhere; 5.2 whether a hard conversation leaves a record; 5.3 where a concern about one's own lead goes.

Who else must agree: both seated founders on every policy decision; Dominic in particular on 5.4.5 (what the review feeds), since pay is his domain; the chef partner on the kitchen's holder and placement (5.4.21), once seated. Nothing here is a founder's personal document, so nothing in this chunk is Brandon's alone.

Why it sits here: the book puts the formal review after coaching, hard feedback, and informal feedback, because the review should only formalize what those already carried. At Sŏn the review is also the seam to 5.5: whether the review touches pay is decided here, and 5.5 builds against the answer.

## 2. Agenda

A default order. Start wherever he wants.

1. Purpose and shape (5.4.2, 5.4.3, 5.4.4): what the review is for, what it reads people on, and whether there is a rating.
2. What it feeds (5.4.5): advancement, pay, a separation record, or nothing. The seam with 5.5.
3. When and who (5.4.6, 5.4.7): the interval and clock; the holder; a lead's review.
4. Sources and voices (5.4.8, 5.4.9, 5.4.10, 5.4.11): what it reads, the person's half, peers, the holder's part.
5. No surprises and the upward half (5.4.12, 5.4.13).
6. Calibration and potential (5.4.14, 5.4.15).
7. The block, the conversation, the record, the first review, load (5.4.16 to 5.4.20).
8. The kitchen (5.4.21): what to hand the chef partner.
9. Deliverables and the kit (section 4 and 5).

## 3. Briefs

### 5.4.2 Decide what the review is for at Sŏn, in one sentence

**The question:** when a person sits down for their review, what is the room for?

**Why it matters now:** every later decision follows this sentence. If the review identifies top talent (p. 402), it needs a way to differentiate (5.4.4). If it is only the developmental moment the white paper describes, differentiation lives in the progression record and the review can refuse a verdict. The sentence also has to survive a candidate reading it (2.1.19).

**Openers:**
- A server, eight months in, walks out of their first review. What do you want them to say to the person on the next station about what just happened?
- Sideways: at the rooms you have worked in, what did the word "review" mean to the floor? What did people do the week before one?
- The book says a review system needs six things (cons. 1). Go down the list. For each: built here, built somewhere else at Sŏn, or not built on purpose?
- The white paper says systems "develop people rather than judge them." Is that a description of the review, or a rule for it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Does the review have any job other than the person's own development? Name it if so.
- Who is the review for: the person, the holder, the house, or a future decision?
- Could a lead run this review well and still not know who their strongest person is? Is that fine?

**What the book says:** a written review summarizes ongoing feedback and outlines development; it also catches the gaps where managers were not giving continuous feedback, and helps identify top talent as the company scales (pp. 399, 402). People trust the system only when they know how they are measured, that it is applied fairly, and how results lead to recognition (pp. 399 to 400).

**White-paper default:** "development, growth, and the path forward rather than a verdict"; no surprises; both directions (WP p. 19). Silent on talent identification and on any link to reward.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies that dropped ratings kept a rhythm of written conversations and moved the differentiation decision elsewhere; the decision did not disappear (cons. 13).

**Options:** (a) the review is the developmental moment only; talent is read from the progression record and the ready-now pool. Commits Sŏn to building those records well and to answering "who is strongest" without the review. (b) the review is developmental and also the house's formal read of who is furthest along. Commits Sŏn to some form of differentiation on the page (5.4.4). (c) the book's review: check-in against the role's expectations, feeding designation, promotion, and pay. Commits Sŏn to calibration and to rewriting the white paper's sentence. Depth: cons. 1.

**Watch for:** a sentence that says "develop" and a design that quietly judges; a sentence Brandon likes that a candidate would read as a promise the review cannot keep.

**A finished answer:** one sentence, said aloud, with the six-item list sorted and the talent question answered either way.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.4.3 Decide the review's dimensions: the four hiring reads, or something else

**The question:** what does the review read a person on?

**Why it matters now:** if hiring and review read different things, the review becomes the door through which a new expectation enters unannounced, which breaks no surprises at the design level. 3.1.5 and 3.2.2 fixed the hiring reads and said the review "will use" the same list; this is where that is confirmed or changed.

**Openers:**
- Say the four hiring reads out loud (cons. 3). Is there anything you would want to say to a person at six months that none of them lets you say?
- Sideways: think of someone you have seen get a surprising review. Was the surprise the judgment, or was it the category, a thing they did not know they were being read on?
- The white paper uses different words for hiring and for review (cons. 3). Are they the same list in different clothes, or two lists?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Unchanged, one named addition, or a different list?
- If an addition: why can review read it when hiring could not?
- What is the sentence that keeps a meeting behavior, a designation held, or a recognition from becoming an unwritten fifth dimension?
- Do the kitchen's dimensions match, or does the chef partner set them?

**What the book says:** reviews are check-ins against the role charter and the ladder's expectations (p. 402); the rubric should be the one people were told about (p. 400).

**White-paper default:** hiring reads competencies, cultural alignment, growth potential, and whether the building can supply what the person needs (WP p. 18); reviews focus on development and the path forward (WP p. 19). Not stated to be one list.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most structured hiring systems that also run reviews use the same competency framework for both; drift between them is a common audit finding.

**Options:** cons. 3 (a), (b), (c).

**Watch for:** an addition that is really a work-style label (1.1.4) or a team-environment behavior by another name.

**A finished answer:** the list in plain words, the hiring-to-review mapping accepted or reworded, the fifth-dimension sentence said, the kitchen placed.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen's list.

### 5.4.4 Decide whether the review carries a performance designation, and on what scale

**The question:** does a person leave their review with a word or a number that ranks them?

**Why it matters now:** this is the largest single divergence from the book in the chunk. It decides whether calibration is needed (5.4.14), whether the review can feed pay (5.4.5), and what the review page may say. It also decides how the house ever says "you are exceptional" in a formal setting.

**Openers:**
- Picture the person in the middle of your floor, solid, not a star. What do they hear at their review? Say it in one sentence with no rating word. Now say it with one.
- Sideways: the white paper says there is "no hierarchy of importance" between the pre-shift brief and the signature dish. Does that principle reach people, or only tasks?
- The book's test for the middle: "would you enthusiastically rehire this person for the role?" (p. 412). Would you ever want a lead asking that question about someone on their team? Where would the answer go?
- If there is no rating, how does the house know who is furthest along? Say where that is read.

**Narrowing questions:**
- Five, three, or none with each dimension as a dated state?
- If a scale: what does it move? If it moves nothing, why does it exist?
- If none: how is "you have real talent" said formally, and by whom?
- Is the word "rating" on the review page or off it?

**What the book says:** five designations or three; the tradeoff is nuance for identifying top talent against simplicity; either way potential deserves a separate read (pp. 404 to 405). Past about thirty people, expect a normal curve (p. 412).

**White-paper default:** "rather than a verdict" (WP p. 19); no hierarchy of importance (WP p. 20). No scale named.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Firms that removed ratings often found managers still needed a shared word for pay decisions and reintroduced a simple one; firms that kept ratings mostly kept three to five bands and spent most of their calibration time on the lines between them (cons. 13).

**Options:** cons. 4 (a), (b), (c), each with what it commits Sŏn to.

**Watch for:** "states" that are ratings in disguise (a state called "strong"); a scale kept "just for information" that leads start using to explain pay anyway.

**A finished answer:** the choice, the middle-of-the-room sentence said aloud, the talent moment placed, the word decided.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.4.5 Decide what the review may feed: advancement, pay, a separation record, or nothing outside itself

**The question:** does anything decided elsewhere read this review?

**Why it matters now:** 5.5 cannot set what moves a person's points until this is answered. 4.1.11 already gave advancement its own three seats. 5.2.4 and 5.10 build the separation record. If the review feeds any of them, the no-surprises rule collides with it: a raise or its absence is news.

**Openers:**
- A person hears their number before anything else in the room. Say the first thirty seconds of a review that also carries a pay change. Now say it without one.
- Sideways: the book says a compensation conversation "can be a helpful tool" to make a managing-out concrete (p. 418). Is there a version of Sŏn where a lead uses pay as a lever? What does that do to the review page?
- The white paper says no one's advancement waits on a manager's judgment (WP p. 11). Does that sentence stop at advancement, or does it reach pay?
- Trust, per the book, comes from knowing how results lead to reward (p. 400). If the review moves nothing, where does that trust come from instead?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Advancement: input, or 4.1.11 alone?
- Pay: input by any route, or none? If some, what exactly, and 5.5.8 gets told.
- Separation: does a review ever become part of the record 5.2.4 says a separation needs?
- Does the review page and the mechanics page say the same sentence about this?

**What the book says:** a mechanism for designation and promotion should be transparent, not informal (p. 399); performance and promotion should feed compensation (p. 400); never promise a reward before the process concludes (p. 401).

**White-paper default:** advancement by demonstrated competency, system-scheduled (WP pp. 11, 19); pay by a point system weighted by days (WP p. 20). The review is connected to neither in the text.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most companies with formal reviews connect them to pay; the ones that separated them (some technology firms in the last decade) typically ran pay on level and market data with a manager's discretionary component, and reported that the review conversation got easier while the pay conversation got harder.

**Options:** (a) nothing outside itself: advancement through 4.1.11, pay through records only (5.5.8), separation through its own record. Commits Sŏn to pay that is legible without judgment and to a separate path for performance that is not holding (5.8). (b) advancement only: the review carries a recommendation that 4.1.11's assessor reads. Commits Sŏn to a lead's opinion entering an assessment designed to exclude it. (c) the book's link: the review feeds designation, promotion, and pay. Commits Sŏn to 5.4.4c and calibration. Depth: cons. 1, 12.

**Watch for:** "nothing" on paper while a lead can still influence a point weight or a schedule; the reverse, a link kept "for later" that makes every review a pay conversation now.

**A finished answer:** three yes-or-no answers with the route for any yes, the same sentence for both pages, the counsel question logged.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; Dominic's agreement on the pay half in particular.

### 5.4.6 Decide the review's interval and whose clock it runs on

**The question:** how often, and on whose calendar?

**Why it matters now:** the white paper gave a range and no point. The interval sets the load on every holder (5.4.20), the first review's timing (5.4.19), and whether the house has a review season.

**Openers:**
- The check-in is monthly after the plan (2.3.10). At three months, what would a review say that the third check-in did not?
- Sideways: a house-wide review season means the floor knows it is "review month." Have you seen what that does to a room? Is that what you want, or the opposite?
- The two leads were hired months before opening. When is their first review, and is it before the doors open?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Three, six, or somewhere between, as a parameter?
- The person's clock or a house season? If a season, where against the service calendar and the reset?
- What read would change the interval, and in which direction?

**What the book says:** most often annual, with review-style conversations at least quarterly or twice a year so the formal one only formalizes (pp. 402 to 403); calibration on the same cadence (pp. 407 to 408).

**White-paper default:** "every three to six months" (WP p. 19). No owner.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies with a season run it once or twice a year and accept the workload spike; companies on the person's anniversary avoid the spike but lose the cross-team comparison the season enables.

**Options:** (a) six months on the person's clock, shortening toward three only on a read that check-ins are failing. Commits Sŏn to no season and to per-person scheduling on the surface. (b) three months on the person's clock. Commits every holder to twice the blocks. (c) a house season twice a year. Commits Sŏn to a review month and makes a light calibration read possible. Depth: cons. 10.

**Watch for:** an interval set by what the stack can schedule rather than what the person needs; the leads' first review forgotten because it falls before opening.

**A finished answer:** the interval as a parameter with its reason, the clock chosen, the change condition named, the leads placed.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.4.7 Decide who holds the review: the check-in holder or a separate reviewer, and who holds a lead's

**The question:** who is across the table?

**Why it matters now:** the review opens on the check-in's lines. A different reader is reading a relationship they were not in. 2.2.5 named who holds each lead's check-in and review; this confirms the floor and asks what happens when the person cannot say something to the holder.

**Openers:**
- Your Maitre d holds every floor check-in. Who else could hold the review, and what would they be reading?
- Sideways: at Alinea or Coqodaq, who reviewed you? Did they know your work, or a report of it?
- A person has a problem with the person holding their review. Where do they go, and does that place exist yet (5.3.4)?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The check-in holder, always? Any exception (a person who asks for another)?
- A second person in the room: never, on request, or an interpreter only?
- Who holds a lead's review, per 2.2.5, and is the form the same?

**What the book says:** the manager is central; if they managed well the review holds no surprises (p. 401); the manager writes it after reading the inputs (pp. 402 to 403).

**White-paper default:** the Maitre d owns team development (WP p. 10); the appropriate lead holds the competency conversation (WP p. 19). No reviewer named.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. The direct manager holds the review almost everywhere; skip-level reviews are rare and usually reserved for the manager's own review.

**Options:** (a) the check-in holder, no exceptions, interpreter excepted. (b) the check-in holder with a person's right to ask for a partner to sit in. (c) a separate reviewer for a stated seat class. Each commits Sŏn to a different answer on where a concern about the holder goes.

**Watch for:** a founder holding floor reviews "for now" with no end date; a lead's review held by whichever partner is free.

**A finished answer:** a holder by role for every seat class, the presence rule, and the destination for a concern about the holder named or parked.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.4.8 Decide what the review reads, and what is never a source

**The question:** what is on the table, and what is kept off it?

**Why it matters now:** no surprises is only as strong as the source list. Every earlier chunk built something the review might read (check-in lines, the progression record, captures, the discretionary-range count) or fenced something it must not (the readiness form, coaching records, the pulse, recognition). This is where the lists close.

**Openers:**
- Walk the person page (2.3.11). For each thing on it: does the review read it? Now walk everything not on it that a lead knows about the person. Does any of it come in?
- Sideways: the readiness form said "not yet" on a row at week eight. It is month six. Should that row exist anywhere the review can see?
- If a peer told the Maitre d something about a person last week, and it is not on any record, what happens to it at the review?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The closed lists as drafted (cons. 5), with one holder-proposed source, or the book's open sources?
- Are development goals (2.2.14) a source? Does that depend on who set them?
- Where does the hand-written training record go, and the reference note in a lead's hiring packet?
- Is the list closed by the system (no free-text field) or by a rule a holder follows?

**What the book says:** peer input, a self-assessment, the manager's assessment, and reports' feedback for a manager (pp. 402 to 405); the review documents conversations already had (p. 401).

**White-paper default:** never new information (WP p. 19); every competency requirement visible to anyone (WP p. 11).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Formal review tools almost always include a free-text manager field; a closed source list is unusual and would be a Sŏn-specific build.

**Options:** cons. 5 (a), (b), (c).

**Watch for:** a "never a source" list that is aspirational because the holder can see the record anyway; a source list that requires records the stack cannot write by month six.

**A finished answer:** two closed lists with records named, the closure mechanism stated, the three edge items placed.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.4.9 Decide the person's own half: what they write, when, and on whose time

**The question:** what does the person write, and when?

**Why it matters now:** the book's self-assessment is homework. The white paper says costs are not shifted to employees. Whether writing happens before or in the block changes the block's length and what can be sent ahead (5.4.17).

**Openers:**
- A line cook is asked to write their self-assessment. Where and when do they do it, and are they paid for that hour?
- Sideways: what did you write in your own self-assessments, and did anyone read it before the meeting?
- Someone does not want to write anything. What happens?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The prompts, in plain words: what did you hold, what next, what must the building supply?
- Written in the block, or ahead on paid time?
- The rule for a person who will not write: the holder writes it in their words and reads it back, or the half stays empty?

**What the book says:** the self-assessment covers contributions, a capability excelled at, a development area with support wanted, submitted with peer input (pp. 403 to 404).

**White-paper default:** feedback runs both directions (WP p. 19); nothing owed off the clock (WP p. 19).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Self-assessments are homework nearly everywhere in office work; in hourly settings they are usually skipped or done in the meeting.

**Options:** (a) in the block, prompts translated, holder writes for a person who prefers not to. (b) ahead, on paid time, so the record can be sent a day before. (c) the book's form. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** a half that becomes the person's case for themselves rather than their account of the period.

**A finished answer:** the prompts, the timing, the pay, the no-write rule, the language.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.4.10 Decide whether peers contribute to the review

**The question:** do coworkers have a voice in a person's review?

**Why it matters now:** 4.8.9 and 5.3.7 gave peers a recognition mechanism that is never a review input. Adding peer input here creates a second channel that reaches the record, and 5.3.6's bounds on peer feedback would apply to it.

**Openers:**
- Someone picks three peers for their review. Who do they pick, and what does the fourth person think?
- Sideways: in a kitchen, what does a peer review look like the week after? Has it ever made a line better?
- Recognition is already peer feedback in the moment. What would a written peer review add that recognition does not?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The book's form, one named peer example per dimension chosen by the person, or none?
- If any: who chooses, what is asked, does the person see it, and how do 5.3.6's bounds apply?

**What the book says:** three to five peers, chosen by the person and agreed by the manager; prompts on closeness, a strength, one improvement, one principle (pp. 403 to 404).

**White-paper default:** peer recognition in the moment, which "lands differently than management praise" (WP p. 19). No peer role in reviews.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Peer input is standard in office reviews and rare in restaurants; where restaurants use it, it is usually informal and verbal.

**Options:** cons. 6.

**Watch for:** peer input that becomes the informal network the code-bearing structure is built to remove (WP p. 11).

**A finished answer:** yes or no, and the form if yes.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.4.11 Decide what the holder writes in their own voice

**The question:** does the review hold anything the holder wrote about the person?

**Why it matters now:** 4.4.2 decided what a lead may write about a person. The book's manager review is a narrative with a recommendation and a designation. If the holder writes nothing, the "you have real talent" moment (p. 384) has to be carried another way.

**Openers:**
- Write one sentence about a person on your team in your own voice, the kind that would go on a review. Now imagine them reading it a year later. Still true? Still yours to have written?
- Sideways: the book's best moment is telling someone, formally, that they have real talent. How does that get said if the holder cannot write an assessment?
- If a holder can write nothing, what do they do for the half hour?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The book's sections, a short note written in the person's presence and read back, or nothing beyond confirming the assembled record?
- In what voice, and does the person read it before it is saved?
- Is this consistent with 4.4.2?

**What the book says:** summary, capabilities used, a development area, a promotion recommendation, a designation (pp. 404 to 405); the template's impact and strengths sections (pp. 468 to 471).

**White-paper default:** not a verdict (WP p. 19); blame the process (WP p. 17). No lead-authored assessment anywhere.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. A manager narrative is near-universal in formal reviews; the argument against it is that it outlives its context and is read by people who were not there.

**Options:** cons. 6.

**Watch for:** "nothing in the holder's voice" while the holder chooses which records to read aloud and in what tone; that is a voice too.

**A finished answer:** what the holder may write, in what voice, read before saved, and how the talent moment is carried.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.4.12 Decide how "no surprises" is made a mechanism, and what a surprise becomes

**The question:** how does the rule become something a holder cannot break by accident?

**Why it matters now:** the rule is the white paper's, stated outright. The earlier work built it in three parts (cons. 7). The candor count is a supervisory instrument on leads and needs to be named as one.

**Openers:**
- A holder notices something about a person the week before the review, and it is not on any record. Say what they do with it, step by step.
- Sideways: the last time you were surprised in a review, what would have had to be true a month earlier for it not to be a surprise?
- End of the review: "was anything said today that you could not already have read?" Would you ask that question? What does a "yes" do?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Pre-assembly with nothing added: yes or no?
- The undisclosed observation goes to the next check-in, once, after the person's account: yes or no?
- The closing question: asked or not; where the answer goes; whether it is a count on the holder.
- A strength not yet named: same rule as a concern?

**What the book says:** if the manager managed well there are no surprises; the review documents conversations already had (p. 401); send it a day ahead; redirect pushback to next time (pp. 405 to 407).

**White-paper default:** "a formal review never contains new information. If something is wrong, the person knows before the review. If something is excellent, they know before it too" (WP p. 19).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most companies state the rule and enforce it by training; a structural version (closed sources, a closing question, a count) is unusual.

**Options:** (a) the three-part mechanism with the count. (b) the mechanism without the count: the question is asked and answered, nothing tracked. (c) the book's procedural form only. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** a count that reaches a founder about a lead without 5.9 knowing it exists; a closing question a person feels unable to answer honestly with the holder in the room.

**A finished answer:** the steps a holder follows, the destination of an undisclosed observation, the question's fate, the strength rule.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.4.13 Decide the upward half: what the person says about their lead, the workplace, and the building, and where it goes

**The question:** what does the person get to say about everything that is not them, and does it go anywhere?

**Why it matters now:** the white paper promises it (WP p. 19). 5.3.4 decided where a concern about one's own lead goes when the check-in is not safe. This decides the review's own upward moment and how a lead's team's answers reach the lead's review without names.

**Openers:**
- "What would you change?" A person says: the schedule, the way my lead runs the brief, and the walk-in door. Where does each of those go the next morning?
- Sideways: the white paper says the earliest warnings come from the people doing the work. Where in the current design does a warning said at a review end up?
- A person has something to say about the holder and the holder is in the room. What do they do?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The three targets (the holder, the mechanisms, the building) or fewer?
- Written only if the person wants it?
- Destinations: the channel, the infrastructure line, the leads' review, 5.3.4's destination.
- How a lead's team's upward halves roll up to the lead's review without names.

**What the book says:** a manager's review includes their reports' feedback (pp. 402 to 403); the conversation is a chance for the manager to receive open feedback (p. 406).

**White-paper default:** "Team members weigh in on management effectiveness, operational improvements, and culture" (WP p. 19).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Upward feedback is usually gathered anonymously in a survey rather than in the person's own review; the reason is the holder in the room.

**Options:** (a) the extended "what's in the way" question with destinations named. (b) the same, with the holder half moved out of the room to 5.3.4's destination. (c) none in the review; the pulse and the channel carry it. Depth: cons. 10.

**Watch for:** an upward answer "explained away" in the room; an item written into the person's record that is really about the building.

**A finished answer:** the questions, each destination, the write rule, the roll-up rule.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.4.14 Decide whether Sŏn runs calibration, and what carries its functions if not

**The question:** how does the house know two holders are holding the same bar?

**Why it matters now:** the book treats calibration as one of three fundamentals (p. 402). If 5.4.4 kept no designation there is nothing to calibrate in the book's sense, but two of calibration's functions (a cross-holder read of record quality; a bias check) still need a carrier. 4.7.18 wants to attach equity checks to "the calibration record."

**Openers:**
- The Maitre d writes generous reviews and the Head of Beverage writes thin ones. With no ratings, how would you ever know?
- Sideways: the book's failure mode is managers performing their justifications to a room (p. 409). Have you sat in that room? What did it produce?
- 4.7 wants equity checks on the review. What record do they read if there is no calibration record?

**Narrowing questions:**
- None with carriers named, a sample read at the reset, or the book's roll-up?
- If a read runs: who runs it (a founder, per p. 412), what it reads, what it may produce (a process act only).
- Where 4.7.9's checks attach.
