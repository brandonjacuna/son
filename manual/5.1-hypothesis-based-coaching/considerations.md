# 5.1 Hypothesis-based coaching: considerations

What the white paper holds, what the old work argued that is worth keeping, the options, and where Sŏn may diverge from the book. Topics follow the book's order (pp. 382 to 391). "WP p. N" cites the September 2026 white paper; "cons. N" cites a section here.

## 1. Where coaching sits at Sŏn

**White paper.** Team development, training, and the "invisible advocate" belong to the Maitre d (WP p. 10). The ninety-day plan runs on "regular check-ins and course correction" (WP p. 18). Reviews every three to six months "focus on development, growth, and the path forward rather than a verdict," and the governing rule is no surprises: "continuous communication means a formal review never contains new information" (WP p. 19). Feedback runs both directions (WP p. 19). Silent on how a lead coaches, on what they read, and on whether they may write anything about a person.

**From the earlier work.** The check-in (2.3.9) is the coaching surface: on the clock, in the pre-service window, the person's three standing questions plus an upward question, one line in the person's words, read back. It refuses verdicts, task assignment, pay, and discipline. What a lead does inside those minutes to coach was left to this chunk (86akh67y5).

**The book's continuum.** The extreme coach over-involves; the forgot-to coach names the result and not the path; most managers wait until a review (p. 382). The white paper's no-surprises rule puts Sŏn on the side of coaching before the review, but does not say how often.

**Options for 5.1.2.**
- (a) Every check-in carries one observation from the lead. Commits every lead to forming a hypothesis every interval, for every person, which is heavy for a lead holding a whole floor's check-ins and risks manufactured observations.
- (b) A lead brings an observation when one has formed; the check-in stands complete without one. Commits Sŏn to trusting leads to notice when they are drifting toward the forgot-to end, and to reading the drift at the reset.
- (c) Between the two: a lead is expected to have said something about strengths and something about gaps to each person in every review period, on their own timing. Commits Sŏn to a per-period expectation without a per-check-in one.

## 2. Gathering data: what a lead may read

**White paper.** The shift close captures each incident, the lead reviews, and the process is updated (WP p. 13). Progression is documented from the start with milestones, skill assessments, and feedback (WP p. 18). Every position's competency requirements are visible to anyone (WP p. 11). Peer recognition runs on a platform (WP p. 19). The diagnostic reference is for reading operational problems before intervening at the people level (WP p. 14). Silent on a list of sources and on any exclusion.

**From the earlier work.** The strongest sources are the ones the person can already read: their own check-in lines, the ninety-day plan's states, readiness rows, the incident captures they wrote, the record of how they used their decision-rights range, schedule facts, and demonstrated skills. The weakest is the lead's own unwritten observation from working a service, because the person cannot read it back. The earlier work refused as coaching data: a peer's account of a peer (an informal grapevine), a mentor's account (3.3.14 keeps the mentor out of any lead's channel), a hiring scorecard (3.2 keeps hiring assessments from carrying forward), a work-style placement (1.1.4), a peer recognition (a peer's act, not a read of a person), a diagnostic finding (4.2.7 decides whether the evidence line names anyone; the earlier work's answer was never), and a customer rating.

**The book pulls the other way.** Her Sonya example counts three reports' feedback, the leader's own history with those three, and the situation Sonya was in, and arrives at ten or fifteen data points (pp. 387 to 388). Refusing peer and mentor accounts costs a lead most of that richness.

**Options for 5.1.3.**
- (a) Readable records only, plus the lead's own observation as the weakest tier, corroborated before it carries a hypothesis alone. Commits Sŏn to slower hypotheses and to a heavier shift close and person page, since those become the only memory.
- (b) The book's breadth: anything a lead has seen or heard, including what peers and the mentor say, weighed by the lead. Commits Sŏn to a lead holding accounts the person cannot see, which cuts against no surprises and against 3.3.14's mentor rule.
- (c) Readable records plus what a person's peers say about the work, never about the person: an incident capture that names a table and a sequence is a source; "she's slow" is not. Commits Sŏn to teaching that line in the module and to leads rewriting captures that cross it (5.3.15).

**The diagnostic hand-off** (86akhptpd, 86akhpvkk). 4.2 reads the house by failure mode; a finding such as one role's load spilling onto its neighbors may implicate one person's seat. The earlier work's constraint: the finding reaches coaching only as dated facts about the role's load, never as a description of the person, never with the word "disengaged," and never as a reason for anything but a conversation. Note that the white paper itself uses "disengaged" of a role under "tension slack" (WP p. 14). 5.1.3 decides whether that word may ever be said to a person.

**Observing directly.** The book has a manager join a report's meetings (p. 390). At Sŏn the equivalent is a lead working a service beside someone. Whether that is announced, and whether the lead may do it for the purpose of observing, is part of 5.1.3.

## 3. The principles as a reference

**White paper.** Orientation covers "the mission, the values, and the behavioral standard" before operational training (WP p. 18). Silent on whether the principles are what feedback measures against.

**From the earlier work** (86akh3wxv). A principle nothing references in practice is decoration. A candidate principle can serve as a feedback reference only if it names a behavior observable in one service, not an inferred state. The earlier work ran seven candidates through that test; two ("pay for what matters," "advancement requires preparation, not permission") read as commitments the company makes, not behaviors a person shows, and were proposed for reclassification rather than removal. The principles themselves are 2.1.5's; that list may differ from the seven.

**Options for 5.1.4.** (a) A hypothesis may reference a principle and is expected to when one fits; the test gates which principles may be cited, and 5.4 inherits the same list. (b) A hypothesis never references a principle; principles are taught in orientation and left out of feedback, so a person is never told they broke a value. (c) Principles are referenced only for strengths. Each commits 2.1 to accepting or refusing the deliverability test on its list.

## 4. Forming a hypothesis: its shape

**White paper.** Performance systems "develop people rather than judge them" (WP p. 19). Silent on the form of an observation.

**The book's shape** (pp. 389 to 390): a theory about a specific behavior and its effect, with an example, offered as the manager's perception, followed by "did you sense that?" and "what might you do differently? How can I help?" The counter-example is a trait ("a bad communicator"). Her formative review named strengths explicitly (pp. 384 to 385). She also allows motivation questions: is this new, episodic, or persistent; could something outside work be a factor (p. 286).

**From the earlier work.** One shape only: a specific behavior in a specific service and its effect on the work. Never a trait, a verdict, a placement on the skill-will grid, a state word, or a label such as "self-awareness gap." The motivation questions were refused, on the ground that a question about someone's personal life is not a lead's to ask.

**Where this may diverge from the book.** The book asks about personal life gently and as a possibility. Refusing it entirely is a real divergence: it protects privacy and keeps a lead from diagnosing, at the cost of a lead sometimes missing the obvious cause. 4.4.6 closes with counsel what a lead may ask about a person's background; the motivation question sits next to that.

**Options for 5.1.5.** (a) The earlier work's single shape and full refusal list, strengths included. (b) The book's shape with the motivation questions allowed once, framed as "is anything in your way that I can help with," which the check-in already asks (2.3.9). (c) Shape as in (a), with the skill-will placement allowed only if 4.2.5 keeps the matrix and only in the lead's own head.

## 5. Where a hypothesis lives

**White paper.** Silent on a lead's private notes. The person page is written only at the check-in and read back (2.3.11); 4.4.2 decides whether a lead may keep any written read outside it.

**The book.** File it away and revisit it (p. 386). Over three to six months, count how many proved right (p. 389). Both assume a memory that lasts, which for most managers means a note.

**From the earlier work.** A hypothesis is never written: it exists in the lead's head between the records that produced it and the one check-in where it is tested. The release valve for a hypothesis that cannot wait is the early check-in (cons. 7), not a note. The design principle: nothing written means nothing exists for any later process to cite.

**The cost.** A lead holding thirty people's check-ins cannot carry thirty hypotheses in their head across a month, and cannot run the book's three-to-six-month accuracy check without a record. The earlier work never resolved that.

**Options for 5.1.6.** (a) Nothing written, ever; accuracy is gauged from the person page's lines and the lead's memory at the reset. (b) A lead may keep a private note of an untested hypothesis, destroyed once said; the note is the lead's, not the house's, and counsel is asked whether such notes are discoverable (5.2.4). (c) A lead may keep a note the person may read on request, which makes it a readable record and moves it toward the person page.

## 6. Testing a hypothesis

**White paper.** No surprises (WP p. 19). Check-ins carry course correction (WP p. 18).

**The book.** Say it, framed as a theory, then ask whether it feels right; sometimes you are wrong, which is data (pp. 386, 389 to 390). Common understanding can take rounds (pp. 394 to 395). When self-perception and observation stay apart, gather more detail, observe more directly (p. 390).

**From the earlier work.** The hypothesis enters after the person has answered their own questions, never before, so the person's account is on record first. One per check-in. Three outcomes: the person agrees and adds their own read; the person disagrees and the records are thin, so it is dropped or held; the person disagrees and the records are not thin, so the lead points to the dated records and asks what the person sees. The "self-awareness gap" becomes a pattern rather than a label: the same hypothesis raised at consecutive check-ins, records unchanged after any process fix, no acknowledgment. That pattern, and only that, is what 5.8 receives.

**Options for 5.1.7 and 5.1.9.** For testing: (a) after the person's answers, one per check-in, outcome recorded nowhere but the person's own line; (b) the same, but the lead may note the outcome per 5.1.6's answer. For the repeat: (a) a fixed count of consecutive unacknowledged raisings, with the process-fix check first, hands the records to 5.8; (b) no count, the lead judges when coaching has stopped working and says so to the person before anything moves; (c) the book's route, more data and closer observation before any escalation, with 5.8 reached only through the formal review (5.4).

## 7. The early check-in

**White paper.** Silent. 2.3.10 sets intervals as parameters; 2.3's mapping left "a lead may place a check-in ahead of the interval" as an option on 2.3.9.

**From the earlier work.** The person's own check-in holder may bring one forward once per interval, on the schedule, paid, no reason field, and the per-lead count is read at the leads' review as a load reading on the lead's seat. If a lead is placing many, the reading is that the seat's roster or interval is wrong, not that the people are.

**Options for 5.1.8.** (a) As above. (b) No early check-in; a lead who cannot wait says a brief in-person heads-up that a conversation is coming, and holds it at the scheduled time. (c) Early check-ins allowed without limit but counted. Each commits the scheduling surface (3.3.32) to a second trigger, or the partners' page to a manual step.

## 8. Testing through a duty

**White paper.** Designations mark accountability in the moment and "are not permanent elevations above anyone" (WP p. 10). Advancement waits on no manager's judgment: competencies, a practical, a ready-now pool (WP p. 11).

**The book.** Before promoting, have the person perform some of the role's duties; a stretch project as a test (pp. 390 to 391).

**Where Sŏn diverges.** The white paper has already replaced the manager's judgment about readiness with a documented assessment. A lead giving a designation "to see how they do" reintroduces the judgment the white paper removed, and the earlier work (86akhcz71) held that holding a duty is never a reward or a sanction and the count of times held is read only in aggregate. 5.1.10 decides whether any room remains for the book's practice, for example an assignment the person asks for.

## 9. The career conversation as a source

**White paper.** The person sits at the center of their own knowledge map (WP p. 11). No career conversation is described.

**From the earlier work.** The one-time career conversation (4.4.3 to 4.4.5, guide 4.4.13, kit 4.4.15) is not a coaching surface and never involves reading the person. Its record is the person's own stated path, in their words, dated and versioned, on the person page (86akhpv2v, built here as 5.1.14). Note that 4.6.17, 4.6.22, and 4.6.23 also name a career conversation guide and kit; 4.4 is the owner named for this chunk, and the duplication is parked for the controller.

**The book** treats a report's self-named tendency as something to keep and raise later (pp. 386 to 387). Whether that is welcome or feels like a trap depends on the person knowing it may be used. 5.1.11 decides.

## 10. Coaching the founders

**White paper.** Describes each founder's domain (WP pp. 3 to 5); silent on who coaches them. 1.1 and 1.2 set the partners' mutual awareness and the rule for raising hard things; 3.3.23 considers an outside coach for a lead's first months; 4.2.12 decides whether the founders' seats are read by the diagnostic.

**The book.** She went years without a review; the one that named her strengths changed her practice (pp. 384 to 385).

**Options for 5.1.12.** (a) The partners test hypotheses about each other's work at the weekly review (2.3.4), under the same shape and refusals. (b) An outside coach for each founder, with the partners' rule for hard things covering the rest. (c) The leads' upward question is the founders' main source, read at the leads' review.

## 11. How others have handled it

General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- The Situation-Behavior-Impact form from the Center for Creative Leadership is the most widely taught shape for an observation and matches the book's model example: the moment, what the person did, the effect.
- Kim Scott's *Radical Candor* pairs "challenge directly" with "care personally" and warns against the manager who withholds to be kind, which is the book's forgot-to coach.
- Netflix, as described in *No Rules Rules*, asks feedback to be actionable and offered to assist, and expects the receiver to appreciate it and then accept or discard it; the "accept or discard" half is the book's point that acting is the report's decision.
- Fine-dining rooms commonly separate the live call during service from any conversation about a person, holding the second for after service; the pattern is old and the book's "praise publicly, criticize privately" (p. 396) is its written form.

## 12. Counsel questions raised here

- Whether a lead's private note of an untested hypothesis, if 5.1.6 allows one, is a personnel record under Texas law and discoverable in a dispute (with 5.2.4).
- Whether a lead may ask about a factor outside work when a pattern appears, and how to ask without creating an obligation (with 4.4.6).
