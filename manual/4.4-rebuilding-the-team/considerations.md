# 4.4 (Re)building the team: considerations

What the white paper holds, what the earlier work found worth keeping, the options and what each commits Sŏn to, and where Sŏn may diverge from the book. Topics follow the book's order (pp. 294 to 303, with the exercise at pp. 364 to 367). "WP p. N" is the white paper; "cons. N" is a section of this file. Nothing here is a decision.

Vocabulary, in plain words: a **career conversation** is the book's one-off conversation about a person's career and what they want next. An **assignment** is work outside a seat's ordinary scope, handed to a person for a bounded time. A **trapdoor** is a decision that is hard to reverse. A **named owner** is the one person accountable for an assignment getting done; the book's word is DRI. The decision-rights register is 2.2.4's; the check-in and person page are 2.3's; the ready-now pool is the white paper's (WP p. 11).

## 1. The bedrock is the people: how a lead knows them

**Book.** Whether the team is new, inherited, or restructured, its bedrock is the people; evaluate each person's skill and will, form a view of contribution and potential, and take time to understand each person individually and collectively (p. 294).

**White paper.** An organization that holds knowledge in the system gets smarter as it ages; one that holds it in people resets with every departure (WP p. 08). Every position's requirements are visible without asking; the system is the advocate (WP p. 11). No surprises: a review never contains new information (WP p. 19). Silent on whether a lead may hold a private view of a person.

**From the earlier work.** "Rebuilding" was read as something the system does, through documented processes and the pool, rather than something a lead does by re-evaluating people; after a departure there is no new team, only a complement of seats to re-establish. A lead's personal impression was declined as a mechanism; "understanding a person" was placed on four things where the person speaks in their own words: the person page (2.3.11), the check-in with its standing "what's in your way," a working-with-me document for leads (1.1, 3.3.24), and the career conversation (cons. 2). None permits a lead's read of anyone's skill or will to be written anywhere.

**Options.**
- (a) The four person-authored records only; a lead keeps no written read of a person outside them. Commits Sŏn to leads carrying their impressions unrecorded, and to Chapter 5's records (5.4) being the only place a lead's assessment is written, with the person seeing it.
- (b) The four records plus a lead's private working notes, kept short and discarded at the review. Commits Sŏn to a category of record the person does not see, which "no surprises" tolerates only if nothing in it is news at the review.
- (c) The book's approach: the lead's evaluation is the primary record, held by the lead. Commits Sŏn to the manager-as-instrument model 4.2 may have declined.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most managers keep private notes; most employment lawyers advise that anything written about a person be something the manager would be comfortable having the person read, which is the white paper's rule stated as caution.

## 2. The career conversation

**Book.** A scheduled, dedicated conversation with each person about their career and development, held after a few months (enough trust) and before any formal review. Not an interview; no five-year-plan question, which shuts people down. Walk the person's history and choices to surface motivations. Three purposes: show you care about them and their career; understand their narrative arc; begin a longer-term direction you both return to (pp. 294 to 296). The exercise: an hour, shorter for someone straight out of school; an advance note to the team and a day-before reminder so no one over-prepares; questions on where they grew up, school, first job and why, favorite and least favorite jobs, what work they picture in five years without a title; listen, take notes, ask why; close by recapping and writing two or three development goals to track together (pp. 364 to 367).

**White paper.** Reviews every three to six months focus on development and the path forward (WP p. 19). The path is explicit before a person starts it; advancement requires preparation, not permission (WP pp. 11, 14). Cross-training is career development (WP p. 19). The candidate sheet already shows the advancement path at the first interview (WP p. 18). Hiring reads competencies through documented questions (WP p. 18). Silent on a career conversation, and on what a lead may ask a person about their life.

**From the earlier work.** A tension: a conversation whose purpose is for a manager to learn a person's ambitions in order to decide what they should focus on cuts against a system built to remove the manager's attention from that judgment. The proposal: one conversation, held once, after the ninety-day plan closes and before the first formal review, paid, roughly half an hour as a starting parameter, between the person and their direct lead; opening on the person's own earlier notes about what they want next and on documents they already have (their seat's advancement criteria, the visible tracks, their cross-training record), adding nothing they have not been told. Outputs: a re-confirmed or re-written statement in the person's words of what they want next; one named track; a note of what the house must supply, which becomes a training-infrastructure line (3.3.28) if unmet. Declined: the five-year projection; a title, level, or promise of a seat; pay; a date beyond the tracks; questions about life or history before joining (hiring never asked; asking after hire risks the overreach the people principles exist to prevent); goals in the lead's words; and any use of the conversation's content in a later assignment or placement. The book's three purposes translated: "showing you care" declined as an explicit purpose (care is meant to be structural); the "narrative arc" became the person's stated near-term interest; the "shared reference point" became the person page, revisited at ordinary check-ins. The history question was flagged as close to an HR and legal judgment, to confirm with counsel. A carryover asked that recurring training-infrastructure complaints be read as diagnostic signals; the earlier work read them as node overload or sequence corruption by pattern.

**Options.**
- (a) The book's conversation, as written, once per person in the window. Commits Sŏn to leads asking about childhood and family, to counsel's view on that, and to the two or three goals living beside 2.2.14's goals.
- (b) The narrowed conversation: the person's own notes and the visible tracks as the opening, no history walk, outputs in the person's words. Commits Sŏn to a shorter conversation that may miss what the book values most (the why behind past choices), and to the person page carrying the outputs.
- (c) A mix: the book's job-history questions (favorite and least favorite jobs, why they chose roles) kept; the childhood, family, and five-year questions dropped; outputs as in (b). Commits Sŏn to a conversation that asks about work history the hiring loop may already have covered (3.2), which is fine if it asks why rather than what.
- (d) No separate conversation; the check-in's standing questions and the review (5.4) carry it. Commits Sŏn to the book's warning that without a dedicated moment, the longer view never gets discussed.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Russ Laraway's version, popularized through Radical Candor, runs three conversations (life story, dreams, an eighteen-month plan); it shares the book's history walk and is widely used in technology companies. Restaurants rarely hold anything like it, which is part of the white paper's argument about turnover (WP p. 16).

**Divergence.** The book's conversation is an instrument of the manager's understanding. Sŏn may make it an instrument of the person's own statement, which changes who it is for.

## 3. Named owners

**Book.** A DRI is the one person accountable for a goal or ship date; may be a lead or an individual contributor; owns getting the decision made even when not the decider; DRIs nest; the term works when taught in onboarding and used consistently (p. 298).

**White paper.** Authority lives in the domain; the domain owner is accountable to its outcomes (WP p. 07). Designations mark accountability in the moment (WP p. 10). Silent on ownership of one-off work.

**From the earlier work.** A DRI was declined as redundant: a decision-owner with a stated range carries more accountability than a DRI, and a DRI without decision authority is the "responsible but not empowered" pattern the white paper forbids. Against that: 4.1.30 may have created project and working-group forms, and a project needs someone to be accountable for it finishing; the register names who decides, not who carries a piece of work to its end.

**Options.**
- (a) Every assignment and project has one named owner, in plain words ("owner"), recorded on the project form or the brief; the owner has the decision range the register gives the work, or an escalation path if not. Commits Sŏn to teaching the term in onboarding and to never naming an owner without a range or a path.
- (b) The domain lead owns every assignment in their domain; no separate owner. Commits Sŏn to leads carrying every project personally, which reads as node overload at any volume.
- (c) The book's DRI, term and all. Commits Sŏn to a borrowed word and its connotations.

## 4. Delegating: the rule

**Book.** Two axes, impact and reversibility; delegate everything that is not high impact and a trapdoor. Sometimes delegate a trapdoor to grow a trusted high performer, with an explicit expectation of close reporting. Sometimes keep adjustable work: to model it (then let people practice, "see one, do one, teach one"); for genuine urgency not yet set up to delegate, then fix the gap; under a resource constraint, which is itself a signal (pp. 299 to 302). Delegate most to high skill, high will; more selectively to high will, low skill; discomfort delegating to someone is a signal to examine (p. 302).

**White paper.** The server resolves at the table within "their authorized judgment and generosity range" (WP p. 13). Every server carries a bottle to gift as they see fit (WP p. 26). Systems are designed for the people who use them (WP p. 17). Silent on reversibility as a test.

**From the earlier work.** Manager-held delegation was declined as a concept: authority is pre-placed with the closest competent seat through the decision-rights register (2.2.2, 2.2.4), which states what a seat decides alone, what triggers escalation, and to whom, set one level broader than the seat strictly needs. Only the underlying test of the book's matrix was kept: a high-impact, hard-to-reverse outcome is built into the seat's escalation triggers so it is never inside an ordinary range by default. The three "do it yourself" cases were translated: modeling into paired services and shadowing (3.3.8); urgency into the incident-and-fix record; a resource constraint into a node-overload finding (4.1.36), never personal overwork.

**Options.**
- (a) The register carries it: the trapdoor test is an escalation trigger on every entry; a founder keeps only the partner-level decisions (2.1.13); no case-by-case delegation. Commits Sŏn to the register being complete enough to cover real work before the leads arrive, and to leads not being able to hand down a decision the register keeps.
- (b) The register as the floor, the book's rule of thumb above it: a lead may delegate anything not a trapdoor, case by case, recorded on the assignment note. Commits Sŏn to two layers and to the note being kept.
- (c) The book's approach, unwritten: leads delegate by judgment. Commits Sŏn to the under- and over-delegating failure modes the book lists (cons. 6).

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Bezos's one-way and two-way doors are the book's own reference; the useful practice is that the reversibility question is asked out loud before the decision is placed, not after. David Marquet's "I intend to" practice on a submarine pushed decisions to the person closest to the work with a stated intent and a check, which is close to a register entry with an escalation trigger.

## 5. The founders' handover

**Book.** An under-delegating manager: decisions need you personally, things fall apart when you are away, you are too buried to do strategic work (pp. 298 to 299).

**White paper.** "The mark of these founders is a company built to run without them" (WP p. 06). CPU failure is a general manager resolving scheduling conflicts (WP p. 13). Node overload is a manager doing operational work because the parallel structure blurred (WP p. 14).

**From the earlier work.** A pre-opening signal named as node overload at the center: once domain leads are in the building a set number of weeks before opening, any seat still opened or hiring packet still decided by the pre-opening hiring lead in that domain is node overload, and the first pass (4.2.19) records the date load handed over. 3.1.4 decided who is hiring manager before and after the leads exist; 1.1.12 built the founders' knowledge-transfer list; 2.3.5 assigned owning partners to pre-opening workstreams. What is missing is the schedule: which function passes to which lead, by which week.

**Options.**
- (a) A dated list per lead, each function with a week and a record that shows it passed; a founder holding a function past its date is a line on the first pass. Commits Sŏn to the founders letting go on a schedule, not when it feels safe, and to leads taking on functions before they feel ready.
- (b) A list without dates, passed "when the lead is ready" as judged in the lead's onboarding plan (3.3.45). Commits Sŏn to the founders' judgment of readiness, which is the book's under-delegating risk in plain sight.
- (c) The leads take everything on arrival; the founders step back to the partners' page (2.3.6). Commits Sŏn to a hard transfer that the leads' onboarding may not support.

This section is about the seats and the schedule, not about either founder's temperament; that belongs to 1.1 and 6.1.

## 6. The signs, and the three cases

**Book.** Under-delegating signs: problems without solutions, decisions need you, things fall apart when you are away, firefighting. Over-delegating signs: consistently low-quality output, projects off the rails before you notice, reports overwhelmed, you cannot describe your team's work in detail (pp. 298 to 299). Delegating is inefficient up front and efficient later (p. 299).

**White paper.** The failure modes (WP p. 14).

**From the earlier work.** Each sign read as a mode from records rather than as a habit to adjust: a bottleneck as a range too narrow or unstated; falling apart in absence as node overload; low output as a readiness gap; late problems as sequence corruption; overwhelm as cooling failure. None treated as something fixed by "delegating more or less." The three do-it-yourself cases translated as in cons. 4.

**Options.**
- (a) Signs read as modes only. Commits Sŏn to a record for each and to no self-check in the lead's monthly conversation.
- (b) Signs as a self-check in the lead's monthly conversation (2.3.14), and as modes at the reset. Commits Sŏn to the lead's own reading and the record's reading sitting side by side, and to the founders reading the same signs on themselves (4.2.12).
- (c) The book's: a lead reads themselves and adjusts. Commits Sŏn to the self-read alone.

For the three cases, the live question is what a lead working a seat for a service is recorded as: a designation (4.1.20), an overload line (4.2.7), or nothing. Each commits the seat load reading (4.1.37) to seeing it or not.

## 7. The assignment conversation

**Book.** Seven steps: bottom-line the assignment; why it fits this person; clear deliverables, co-created with more senior people; timeline against their other work; buy-in; next steps; how you stay informed. Can double as a shared document for someone earlier in their career (pp. 302 to 303).

**White paper.** Employees are the users (WP p. 17). Silent on assignments.

**From the earlier work.** Mostly absorbed into the format for logging a process change (the change in one line, its owner, the date, what it displaces). Two steps declined: "why it fits this person" (assignments go to seats by domain, not to people for development) and "buy-in" (accepting a seat is accepting its range). Against that: the check-in refuses task assignment (2.3.9), so an assignment has to be given somewhere, and the book's two declined steps are the ones that make an assignment development rather than dispatch; the career conversation's outputs (cons. 2) are useless for development if they may never inform an assignment.

**Options.**
- (a) A short separate conversation with a half-page note carrying all seven steps in Sŏn's words; the career conversation's outputs may inform "why this person." Commits Sŏn to assignments being development moves, and to a note per assignment.
- (b) The note carries five steps; "why this person" and "buy-in" are declined; assignments go to seats. Commits Sŏn to development happening only through the tracks (WP p. 11), never through assignments.
- (c) The brief carries service-length assignments as designations; anything longer uses the project form (4.1.30) with an owner. Commits Sŏn to two forms by duration.

## 8. Where Sŏn may diverge from the book, in one place

- The book's lead evaluates each person; Sŏn may let each person state themselves (cons. 1).
- The book's career conversation walks a life; Sŏn may walk the tracks (cons. 2).
- The book's DRI; Sŏn may use a plain word or the register alone (cons. 3).
- The book's manager delegates by judgment; Sŏn may pre-place authority in the register with a trapdoor trigger (cons. 4).
- The book's three do-it-yourself cases; Sŏn may route each to a mechanism (cons. 6).
