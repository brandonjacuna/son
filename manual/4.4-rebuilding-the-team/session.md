# 4.4 (Re)building the team: working session

## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** What a lead does inside a domain once its shape is settled: how they come to know each person, the career conversation, who owns one-off work, what gets delegated and what a founder or lead keeps, how the founders hand their pre-opening load to the leads, and the signs of delegating too little or too much. The book's section (pp. 294 to 303) says the bedrock is the people, describes the career conversation, the DRI, and a framework for delegating by impact and reversibility. Sŏn's decision-rights register (2.2) already pre-places much of what the book's manager delegates by hand, so the chunk is partly about what is left for a lead to hand down, and partly about the biggest handover of all: the founders' own load.

**Already decided upstream.** The decision-rights rule and register (2.2.2, 2.2.4, 2.2.8); how development goals are set (2.2.14); the check-in and what it refuses, and the person page (2.3.9, 2.3.11); which partner holds each lead's monthly conversation (2.3.14); who is hiring manager before and after the leads exist (3.1.4); the pool-first rule (3.1.38); the ninety-day plan and mentoring (3.3.10 to 3.3.14); the lead onboarding plan (3.3.45); the project and working-group forms (4.1.29); the founders' knowledge-transfer list (1.1.12). From 4.2 if decided: whether the skill-will matrix is used (4.2.5) and whether the founders' seats are read (4.2.12).

**Who else must agree.** Dominic on every founder-level rule, and especially on the handover (4.4.9). The chef partner on the kitchen's career conversations and their own handover list. Counsel on what a lead may ask about a person's background (4.4.6).

**Why it sits here.** 4.2 read the house; 4.3 changed it; this chunk is how a lead builds inside it. 5.1 (coaching) and 5.4 (the formal review) take the career conversation's outputs forward; 5.9 holds managing the leads themselves; 6.1 holds the founders' own time, which the handover frees or fails to.

## 3. Briefs

### 4.4.2 Decide how a lead comes to know each person, and what a lead may write about a person

**The question:** where does a lead's knowledge of a person live, and may any of it be written where the person cannot see it?

**Why it matters now:** the person page's rule (nothing the person has not heard) is fixed; the leads will arrive with notebooks. The answer shapes the career conversation (4.4.3) and the formal review (5.4).

**Openers:**
- The Maitre d, month three, has a strong sense that one server is coasting and another is about to bloom. Where does that go? A notebook, the person page, nowhere, or the review?
- Sideways: what did the best manager you ever had know about you, and how did they know it? Did they write it down?
- The book says take time to deeply understand each person. The white paper says the system holds what people know. Are those in conflict, or is one about the person and the other about the work?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The four person-authored records only; those plus a lead's short private notes discarded at review; or the book's lead-held evaluation?
- If private notes exist: what kind, how long kept, and would the lead be comfortable if the person read them?
- How does this square with working-with-me documents for leads (1.1, 3.3.24) and the question of whether hourly hires write one (3.3.26)?

**What the book says:** the bedrock is the people; evaluate each person's skill and will; form a view of contribution and potential; understand each person deeply (p. 294).

**White-paper default:** no surprises (WP p. 19); the person page is written only at the check-in, read back (2.3.11); the system is the advocate (WP p. 11).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most managers keep private notes; most employment counsel advise writing nothing about a person you would not be comfortable having them read.

**Options:** (a) person-authored records only; (b) plus short private notes, discarded at review; (c) the book's lead-held evaluation. Depth: cons. 1.

**Watch for:** "no private notes" in a house where leads plainly will keep them, unrecorded and unregulated; a rule that leaves the formal review (5.4) with nothing to draw on.

**A finished answer:** where knowledge lives, the private-notes rule with its limits, and the working-with-me question reconciled.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.4.3 Decide whether Sŏn holds career conversations, when, and with whom

**The question:** is there a dedicated, scheduled conversation about what a person wants next, and if so, when in their first year and with whom?

**Why it matters now:** the two leads arrive months before opening; their window (a few months in, before the first formal review) will fall before the doors open. The general rule has to exist by then, and 5.4's review timing depends on it. The book asks the question twice, once in this chapter's section on rebuilding (pp. 294 to 296) and once as a chapter exercise, a sixty-minute walk through a person's history ending in a five-year picture (pp. 364 to 367); 4.6 carried the exercise's version and the cross-chunk pass merged it here. Its terms (voluntary, in the person's language, possibly leads only) are in the narrowing questions as things to decide, not as answers. It also seeds a kit (4.4.15).

**Openers:**
- The Operations Lead is four months in. The partner who holds their monthly conversation sits down and says: this one is different, it is about you. What do they ask first?
- The best manager you ever had: did they know what you wanted to be doing in five years? How did they find out?
- Sideways: nobody in a restaurant ever asked you where you wanted to be. If someone had, at Alinea, what would have changed?
- Sideways: the white paper says the longest measure of the culture is how people grow beyond the building (WP p. 23). Who in the building is supposed to know where a person is trying to grow to?
- The white paper says the path is visible without asking (WP p. 11). If that is true, what is this conversation for?
- The book's exercise starts at childhood. Where would you start?
- Once, or every year?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Held at all? If so, in which form: the book's exercise as written, shortened to start at the first job, or the section's lighter version?
- Window: after the ninety-day plan closes and before the first formal review (5.4)?
- For whom: every person, or leads first and then everyone?
- Holder: the direct lead; a partner for each lead (2.3.14); the chef partner in the kitchen?
- Paid working time; length as a parameter; held in the person's language?
- Voluntary, or part of everyone's first year?
- Once, or repeated at an interval?
- Where the two or three goals go is 4.4.5's question; note it and carry it there.

**What the book says:** a scheduled conversation with each person, after a few months and before any formal review; not an interview; three purposes: show you care, understand the arc, begin a direction to return to (pp. 294 to 296). The exercise version: introduce it in advance, remind the day before, listen and ask why, end in a five-year picture without a title and two or three development goals (pp. 364 to 367).

**White-paper default:** reviews every three to six months focus on the path forward (WP p. 19); advancement requires preparation, not permission (WP p. 14); the candidate sheet shows the advancement path (WP p. 18). Silent on the conversation itself.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Russ Laraway's three-conversation version (life story, dreams, an eighteen-month plan) is widely used in technology companies; restaurants rarely hold anything like it.

**Options:** (a) the book's section version, once per person in the window. Commits every lead to one hour per person in their first year. (b) The book's exercise version, voluntary, leads first, then everyone. Commits Sŏn to a question list counsel has cleared (4.4.6) and to a conversation some people will decline. (c) A shortened form from the first job onward. Commits Sŏn to writing its own version of the exercise. (d) None; the check-in and review carry it. Commits Sŏn to the path being visible without anyone asking. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** a conversation the leads have no time to hold for forty people; a window that collides with the first review; "the path is visible" used to skip the one conversation about the person; a question counsel would not allow; the conversation used as a read on the person.

**Capture for the kit (4.4.15):** when a partner holds the first one with a lead, the questions that drew good answers, the order, where it stalled, and what the lead wished they had been told beforehand.

**A finished answer:** yes or no; the form, the window, for whom, the holder, the pay and length, the language, voluntary or not, once or repeated.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner holds the kitchen's, once seated.

### 4.4.4 Decide what the career conversation asks, and what it never asks

**The question:** which of the book's questions does a lead at Sŏn ask, and which are off the table?

**Why it matters now:** the book's script asks about childhood, family, and school; the hiring loop never did (3.1.5). A lead asking after hire is a different act, and counsel should see the list before the first conversation.

**Openers:**
- Read the book's day-before reminder aloud (p. 364). Which sentence would you cut before sending it to a nineteen-year-old runner?
- Sideways: the book says "tell me about where you grew up" and that the author does not pry on family but listens. Picture the Maitre d asking that of someone who left home at sixteen. Is the question kind or is it a trap?
- The book drops the five-year-plan question because it shuts people down. What question opens them?
- "Tell me about your least favorite job and why." Keep it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The book's history walk; a narrowed version opening on the person's own notes and the visible tracks; or a mix that keeps the job-history questions and drops the life ones?
- The never-asked list: a title or level; pay; a date beyond the tracks; a five-year plan; family or life history before the company. Which stay on it, and why in one line each?
- Counsel queued (4.4.6)?

**What the book says:** where they grew up; school and why; first job and why; favorite and least favorite jobs; what work they picture in five years without a title; keep asking why (pp. 364 to 367).

**White-paper default:** hiring reads competencies through documented questions (WP p. 18); the path is explicit before they start it (WP p. 11). Silent on what a lead may ask about a life.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. The life-story opening is standard in the technology versions of this conversation and is usually framed as optional; the person chooses how far back to go.

**Options:** (a) the book's questions; (b) the narrowed set; (c) the mix. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** a list so narrow the conversation is a form; a list so open it becomes the interview the person already had; pay slipping in because the person raises it (the answer is where pay is discussed, 5.5, not silence).

**A finished answer:** the questions, the never-asked list with a reason each, and the counsel question logged.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.4.5 Decide what the career conversation produces and where it lives

**The question:** what is written at the end, in whose words, where, and what may it later be used for?

**Why it matters now:** 2.2.14 decided how development goals are set and 2.3.11 fixed the person page; this either adds to the page or creates a second record. Whether the outputs may inform an assignment (4.4.10) decides whether the conversation is development or ceremony.

**Openers:**
- End of the conversation. The person says: I want to learn the bar. What gets written, by whom, and who sees it next week?
- Sideways: the white paper says a host who wants to learn everything can become the Maitre d without waiting on a manager's attention (WP p. 19). What does that sentence need this conversation to produce, if anything?
- If the outputs can never be used to decide who gets an assignment, what are they for?
- A former mentor, after the plan ends: any role here?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Outputs: a statement in the person's words of what they want next; one named track; what the house must supply (a 3.3.28 line if unmet); the book's two or three goals?
- Whose words?
- On the person page beside the plan's goals? Is anything from it ever written anywhere the person has not agreed to (a rule 4.6 carried and the cross-chunk pass moved here)?
- May inform an assignment or placement, or never?
- What the house does with the two or three goals: 2.2.14's development goals, or a separate thing?
- The former mentor: nothing beyond what the person asks (3.3.14), or a stated role?

**What the book says:** recap, share notes, write two or three development goals to track together; a reference point for later choices about projects, delegation, and roles (pp. 296, 367).

**White-paper default:** cross-training is career development (WP p. 19); development goals per 2.2.14; the person page per 2.3.11.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Where these conversations work, the notes are shared with the person and revisited; where they fail, the manager keeps them and nothing follows.

**Options:** (a) the person's statement, one track, the house's obligation, on the page, usable for assignments; (b) the same, never used for assignments; (c) the book's goals in the lead's words. Depth: cons. 2 and 7.

**Watch for:** a "track named" that is a promise of a seat; a house obligation nobody logs; outputs on the page that the review (5.4) then grades; notes kept where the person cannot see them.

**A finished answer:** the outputs, the author, the location, the use rule, and the mentor answer.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.4.7 Decide whether Sŏn names an owner for one-off work, and what the word is

**The question:** when work exists that no seat owns, does one person own it by name, and what are they called?

**Why it matters now:** 4.1.29 may have created project and working-group forms; a project with no owner is the book's example of a decision nobody gets made. The term, if any, has to be taught in onboarding (3.3).

**Openers:**
- The patio build-out, the first wine list, the thank-you note template: three pieces of work before opening that no seat owns yet. Who owns each, and would you want a word for that?
- Sideways: Apple's DRI came from hardware programs with ship dates. What is Sŏn's equivalent of a ship date?
- An owner who is not the decider: the book says that still works, because they own getting the decision made. Does the register (2.2.4) allow that shape?

**Narrowing questions:**
- One named owner per assignment or project, distinct from the domain lead?
- The word: the book's, or a plain one?
- Owner without decision authority: allowed, with an escalation path; or never?
- Recorded where: the project form, the brief, the register?

**What the book says:** the DRI is accountable for a goal or ship date; may be a lead or an individual contributor; owns getting the decision made; nests; works when taught and used consistently (p. 298).

**White-paper default:** authority lives in the domain and the owner is accountable to its outcomes (WP p. 07); designations mark accountability in the moment (WP p. 10). Silent on one-off work.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Apple's and Stripe's use of the term is the book's own account; many companies use "owner" and get the same effect provided the owner's range is stated.

**Options:** (a) one named owner in plain words with a range or a path; (b) the domain lead owns everything in the domain; (c) the book's DRI, term and all. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** an owner with responsibility and no authority, which the white paper's structure argument forbids; a word nobody uses after month two.

**A finished answer:** owner or not; the word; the no-authority rule; the record.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.4.8 Decide the delegation rule: what a seat decides alone, what is a trapdoor, and what a founder or lead keeps

**The question:** is the book's test, impact against reversibility, built into the register, applied case by case, or not used, and what does a founder keep by rule?

**Why it matters now:** the register (2.2.4) exists in version one; adding a trapdoor trigger is cheap now. The founders' handover (4.4.9) needs to know what a founder keeps on principle rather than on nerves.

**Openers:**
- Four decisions: a ten-year lease, the website copy for opening, the pre-shift agenda, a test message to a hundred customers. Which is the trapdoor? (The book's own examples, pp. 300 to 301.) Now four from Sŏn.
- Sideways: the server's generosity range (WP p. 13) is delegation the white paper already made. What is the equivalent range for a lead, and is any of it a trapdoor?
- Would you ever hand a trapdoor to a lead to grow them? What would you need to hear from them weekly?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The trapdoor test as an escalation trigger on every register entry; case by case by the lead; or not used?
- What a founder keeps: the trapdoors of 2.1 and 2.2, the partner-level decisions, nothing else?
- A trapdoor delegated to grow someone: under what reporting?
- What the register entry kit (2.2.8) gains.

**What the book says:** delegate everything that is not high impact and a trapdoor; sometimes delegate a trapdoor to grow someone with close reporting; sometimes keep adjustable work to model it, for urgency, or under a constraint (pp. 299 to 302).

**White-paper default:** the authorized judgment range (WP p. 13); the register (2.2.2, 2.2.4). Silent on reversibility.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Bezos's one-way and two-way doors are the book's reference; the practice that works is asking the reversibility question out loud before placing a decision. Marquet's "I intend to" pushed decisions down with a stated intent and a check.

**Options:** (a) the register carries it with a trapdoor trigger, founders keep only the partner-level decisions; (b) the register as the floor, the book's rule above it case by case; (c) the book's judgment-based delegation. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** a register that keeps too much at the founders "for now"; a trapdoor list that grows every time something goes wrong; the growth case used to avoid writing the range.

**A finished answer:** the test's place, the founders' keep list, the growth rule, and the kit's new question.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.4.9 Decide what the founders hand to each lead on arrival, and by when

**The question:** for every function a founder holds before the leads exist, which lead does it pass to, and by which week after that lead starts?

**Why it matters now:** this is the largest single change before opening, and the white paper's promise is a company built to run without the founders (WP p. 06). Without dates, the handover happens when it feels safe, which the book says is never.

**Openers:**
- List what you personally do this week that a Maitre d would do. Now put a week number on each.
- Sideways: at Coqodaq's opening, what did the founders still hold on opening night that they should have handed over a month earlier? What did it cost the room?
- The white paper's example of CPU failure is a general manager resolving scheduling conflicts (WP p. 13). Who resolves scheduling conflicts at Sŏn three weeks before opening, under your plan?
- What will you still be holding on opening night, on purpose?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Per function (hiring in the domain, scheduling, vendor calls, training delivery, the brief): the lead, the week.
- What a founder keeps and why.
- A function held past its date is a node-overload line on the first pass (4.2.19)?
- The chef partner's arrival: same rule, pending?

**What the book says:** under-delegating signs: decisions need you, things fall apart when you are away, you are buried (pp. 298 to 299); ordinary work should already be covered in 1:1s (p. 297).

**White-paper default:** built to run without them (WP p. 06); CPU failure (WP p. 13); node overload (WP p. 14); 3.1.4, 1.1.12, and 2.3.5 already hold pieces.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Openings that go well usually have the general manager and chef in the building well before opening with a written list of what transfers to them and when; the ones that go badly have the owner still approving the schedule in month three.

**Options:** (a) a dated list per lead with a record per function; (b) an undated list, passed when ready; (c) everything on arrival. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** anything in the record about either founder's temperament rather than the seat's functions; a date list that ignores the lead onboarding plan (3.3.45); the documented build's camera (3.1.16) as a reason a founder keeps a function.

**A finished answer:** the list per lead with weeks, the keep list with reasons, the overload rule, and the chef partner marked pending.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for their own list.

### 4.4.10 Decide where an assignment is given and recorded, since the check-in refuses task assignment

**The question:** when a lead hands someone work outside their seat, where does that conversation happen, what is written, and may the person say no?

**Why it matters now:** 2.3.9 kept task assignment out of the check-in; the book's delegation conversation needs a home, and the two steps the earlier work declined ("why this person," "buy-in") are the ones that make an assignment development.

**Openers:**
- The Maitre d wants a server to build the thank-you note templates over three weeks. Where is that asked, and what is written?
- Sideways: the book says explain why the assignment fits this person. The earlier work said assignments go to seats, not people. Which house would you rather work in?
- Can a server say no to an assignment? What happens next?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Given where: a separate short conversation; the project form; the brief for a service-length one?
- Which of the seven steps the note carries; which Sŏn declines?
- Decline allowed; what follows?
- How the lead stays informed without the check-in carrying it?

**What the book says:** the seven steps, doubling as a shared document (pp. 302 to 303).

**White-paper default:** the check-in refuses a task assignment (2.3.9); employees are the users (WP p. 17). Silent on assignments.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. A one-page brief per assignment (goal, owner, done-when, check-ins) is standard in project work and rare on a restaurant floor; the ones that exist are usually the brief's side notes.

**Options:** (a) a separate conversation and a half-page note with all seven steps, the career conversation's outputs may inform it; (b) five steps, "why this person" and "buy-in" declined; (c) two forms by duration, the brief for a service, the project form for longer. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** an assignment given at the check-in anyway because there is nowhere else; a note nobody writes; "assignments go to seats" leaving no path for someone to grow through work.

**A finished answer:** the where, the steps kept and declined, the decline rule, and the stay-informed rhythm.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.4.11 Decide how the under- and over-delegating signs are read: as a lead's habit or as a mode

**The question:** when the book's eight signs show up, does the house read them as something about the lead or something about the structure, and where?

**Why it matters now:** the signs will show first on the founders themselves during the handover; the monthly conversation with each lead (2.3.14) and the reset (2.3.30) are the two places the read could live, and both are being built.

**Openers:**
- Things fall apart when the Maitre d takes a night off. Is that a delegation habit or node overload? What would tell you which?
- Sideways: the book says a manager should be able to describe the team's recent, current, and upcoming work in detail to a peer. Could you describe Dominic's? Could he describe yours?
- Which of the eight signs do you expect to see on yourself first?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Each sign: read as a mode from records; a self-check in the lead's monthly conversation; both?
- The founders read the same signs on themselves at the reset (4.2.12)?

**What the book says:** the four under-delegating signs and the four over-delegating signs (pp. 298 to 299).

**White-paper default:** node overload and sequence corruption (WP p. 14).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. The signs are most often used as a manager's self-assessment; reading them from records is rarer and needs the records to exist.

**Options:** (a) modes only; (b) self-check monthly, modes at the reset; (c) the book's self-read alone. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** a mode reading that removes any sense that a lead can change how they work; a self-check with no consequence.

**A finished answer:** the eight signs each placed, and the founders' answer.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.4.12 Decide the three cases a lead does the work themselves: modeling, urgency, short staffing

**The question:** when a lead steps onto a station or takes a task back, which of the book's three reasons is it, what is Sŏn's form of each, and what is written?

**Why it matters now:** a lead working a seat is the most common sight in a restaurant and the white paper's clearest example of the structure blurring (WP p. 14); the seat load reading (4.1.36) can only see it if something records it.

**Openers:**
- Saturday, a server calls out, the Maitre d takes a section. Modeling, urgency, or short staffing? What gets written, if anything?
- Sideways: "see one, do one, teach one" comes from surgical training. Sŏn already has paired services (3.3.8). Is there anything left for a lead to model in person?
- The book says a resource constraint is itself a signal the team is not scaled. In Sŏn's terms, that is a line for 4.1.35. Do you want that line written every time?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Modeling: paired services and the mentor roster, or the lead on the station?
- Urgency: the incident-and-fix record with the gap logged?
- Short staffing: a node-overload line under 4.1.35, never the lead's overtime?
- A lead working a seat for a service is recorded as: a designation, an overload line, nothing?

**What the book says:** model it, then let them practice; urgency, then fix the gap; a constraint, which is a signal (pp. 301 to 302).

**White-paper default:** paired services (3.3.8); the incident captured and the process updated (WP p. 13); node overload's fix is restoring the separation (WP p. 14).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. In most restaurants the manager on the floor covering a section is invisible in any record; where it is logged (some hospitals log a charge nurse taking a patient load), it becomes the strongest staffing signal they have.

**Options:** (a) each case routed to its mechanism and every lead-on-a-seat service recorded; (b) routed, recorded only when it repeats; (c) the book's, at the lead's discretion. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** a recording rule that makes a lead hesitate to help on a bad night; "modeling" as the permanent reason a lead never leaves the floor.

**A finished answer:** the three forms and the recording rule.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
