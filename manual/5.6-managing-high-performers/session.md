# 5.6 Managing high performers: working session

## 1. Orientation

This chunk covers what a lead does for the people who pull more than their weight: reading them, coaching the two patterns the book names, protecting them from their own yes, telling them the truth about the path, and letting them go well. It sits after coaching (5.1), hard feedback (5.2), informal feedback (5.3), the formal review (5.4), and compensation (5.5), because the book's high-performer moves are mostly uses of those tools. At Sŏn the white paper has already taken the manager out of the advancement chain (WP p. 11), so the chunk is smaller than the book's and mostly about the gaps the system leaves: the pool wait, the load pile, the ceiling, and the goodbye.

Upstream, already decided or in progress: whether Sŏn has ladders (4.1.7), cross-strand fluency (4.1.10), who runs the advancement assessment (4.1.11), what a lead may write about a person (4.4.2), the career conversation (4.4.3, 4.4.3), whether stretch assignments test hypotheses (5.1.10), internal succession (3.1.38), whether the structure is ever changed for one person (4.3.6), the held-designation read (4.3.13), the departure read (4.3.8), the exit conversation (4.8.14), and the compensation architecture (5.5, which 5.6.4 waits on).

Who else must agree: both seated founders on every decision here. The chef partner on anything that reaches the kitchen's pools and designations once the seat is filled. The two leads should read the guide (5.6.12) before it is final.

## 3. Briefs

### 5.6.2 Decide whether Sŏn names high performers, and what a lead may do with the read

**The question:** does the house ever say, anywhere, that a person is one of its best, and what may a lead do with knowing it?

**Why it matters now:** every later decision in this chunk assumes an answer. The stay question (5.6.10) needs a "whom." The load rule (5.6.6) needs a source. And the candidate sheet (5.6.3) says what the house reads, so a candidate is relying on this at the first interview.

**Openers:**
- It is the end of a Saturday dinner. You know, without looking at anything, who carried the room tonight. Where does that knowledge go on Sunday?
- Sideways: at Coqodaq, who did everyone know was the best server, and how did everyone know? Was it ever written down? Did it need to be?
- The white paper says the mentor is "a proven cultural steward rather than simply the strongest performer" (WP p. 18). Who decided who the strongest performer was, in that sentence?
- A new lead asks you, "who are my best people?" What do you hand them?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Is there any surface at Sŏn where a person's standing relative to others should be visible: a list, a field, a room, a lead's head?
- What may a lead read to know who is carrying more? What is never a source (a customer rating, a recognition count, a designation count, a hiring scorecard)?
- Once a lead knows, what may they do with it that does not touch pay, sequence, or the schedule?

**What the book says:** top performers pull more than their weight and demand more energy; the manager's starting point is understanding what drives each one (p. 419). Great employees have disproportionate impact, positive or negative; the manager nurtures the best version while keeping their effect on the team positive (pp. 420 to 421).

**White-paper default:** advancement reads documented competencies "anyone can see" and "favors the people who want it most" (WP p. 11); reviews are "the path forward rather than a verdict" (WP p. 19). No definition of a high performer anywhere.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Calibrated talent rankings are the norm in large companies and rare in independent restaurants, where the "best server" is usually common knowledge and never a record. Restaurants that tie section assignments to a ranking tend to find the ranking becomes the culture.

**Options:** (a) no named category; the lead reads the record and the career conversation, writes nothing. (b) a lead's private read, never written, bounded by 4.4.2. (c) a named class for one purpose (the stay conversation). Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** a "no category" answer that leaves the lead with a read they act on invisibly through sections and shifts; a "yes" that becomes a tier by another name.

**A finished answer:** a sentence saying whether the word exists at Sŏn and where; a list of what a lead may read and what is never a source; a list of what the lead may do with it.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.6.3 Decide what the candidate sheet and the team home say about how far the path goes

**The question:** how does Sŏn tell a candidate, at the first interview, that the path is real and also short?

**Why it matters now:** the candidate sheet already shows the advancement path (WP p. 18). With roughly eight leadership lines across the day (WP p. 22) and an intent to fill them from within, most people who become ready will find no seat. Said now, it is honesty. Discovered later, it is the broken promise the book warns about (pp. 428 to 429).

**Openers:**
- A 24-year-old server, ambitious, asks at the first interview: "where can I be in three years here?" Answer them out loud, and count how many sentences are true.
- Sideways: the book's example is a business development team where opportunity caps out and she says be explicit (p. 429). Sŏn's whole leadership layer is that team.
- Which sentence is harder to say to a candidate: "there are eight leadership seats" or "growth here mostly is not a seat"?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Does the sheet state the number of leadership lines?
- Does it say readiness and a seat are different things, and what the pool is?
- Does it name the growth that is not a seat (the adjacent strand, another service period, teaching, assessing, mentoring)?
- Does it say that leaving to grow is a normal outcome, or is that too much for a first interview?

**What the book says:** be explicit about the trade-off on small specialized teams; promotions get rarer as people get senior, so set expectations and find other ways to recognize (pp. 428 to 431).

**White-paper default:** the sheet holds the advancement path (WP p. 18); growth is outward not upward (WP p. 9); the building rarely hires a manager from outside (WP p. 19).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Small firms with flat structures that say so in hiring (some cooperatives and professional partnerships) report fewer exits over "no room to grow" than firms that imply a ladder they do not have. The mechanism is the same as the book's: no surprises.

**Options:** (a) the sheet says the number, the difference between readiness and a seat, and the non-seat growth. (b) the sheet shows the path and leaves the odds for a check-in. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** wording that promises non-seat growth the house has not built yet (the teaching platform, the assessor pool), which WP p. 36's "never assert the unproven" applies to.

**A finished answer:** the sentences for the sheet, said aloud once as if to a candidate, and the same sentences for the team home.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.6.4 Decide the retention levers Sŏn keeps or refuses

**The question:** when a person the house wants to keep has an offer elsewhere, what may anyone at Sŏn do?

**Why it matters now:** compensation is being decided in 5.5, and a candidate reads the compensation mechanics on the sheet (WP p. 18). If a counter-offer can exist, the mechanics are not what the sheet says.

**Openers:**
- Your best line cook gets an offer from the room across town. Dominic asks what it would take to keep them. What is your first sentence?
- Sideways: the book's manager "promotes them and gives raises" (p. 422). Which of those two verbs exists at Sŏn?
- If one exception is made for a lead, what does the sheet say the next morning?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Is a counter-offer ever made? For which seats? Decided by whom?
- Does any pay exist outside 5.5's architecture to keep someone?
- May a title or a designation be used to keep someone, outside the rotation floor (4.1.19)?
- If none of those, what is the house's answer to an outside offer?

**What the book says:** reward high standards with promotion and raises (pp. 421 to 422); if you cannot find real opportunity, do not hold on too long (p. 426).

**White-paper default:** compensation built into menu price, point system weighted by days worked (WP p. 20); fluency pay is an open item (WP p. 15). Silent on counter-offers.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Formula-pay houses commonly refuse counter-offers on the grounds that one exception makes the formula a fiction; negotiated-pay houses commonly make them. Cons. 4.

**Options:** (a) none; the what-next conversation and a clean goodbye. (b) leads only, both partners deciding. (c) the book's practice within a band, reopening 5.5. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** a "none" answer given before 5.5 is final; a "leads only" answer that has not been said to the leads.

**A finished answer:** yes or no on each lever, the deciders named, and the sentence said to a person with an outside offer.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders, with 5.5 complete.

### 5.6.5 Decide whether "pusher" and "puller" are words the leads use, and how each is coached

**The question:** do the leads learn the book's two patterns, and what do they do with each?

**Why it matters now:** the coaching module (5.1.15) is being written; either the vocabulary goes in now or the leads meet the patterns without names.

**Openers:**
- Think of the person you have worked with who told you, every week, what was broken. Were they right? What did their manager do with it?
- Think of the one who never said no and then quit. When did you know?
- Sideways: the white paper says the feedback channel exists because "the earliest warnings come from the people doing the work" (WP p. 19). The pusher is that channel with a face.

**Narrowing questions:**
- Do the words go in the coaching module, the lead's guide, or nowhere?
- If they are used, are they a pattern named at a check-in, never a type written on a person (5.1.5)?
- What does a lead do with a pusher's critique: route it to the channel and the process update, hand them the fix, coach the effect on others?
- The puller's load rule is 5.6.6; here, what does a lead say?

**What the book says:** pushers are ambitious and critical and push their manager too; pullers say yes and burn out silently; each has a best and worst and a coaching response (pp. 420 to 423).

**White-paper default:** burnout is a system failure, not a character flaw (WP p. 17). No vocabulary for the pusher.

**How others have handled it:** Nothing useful beyond the book's own frame.

**Options:** (a) the words enter the module and guide as patterns. (b) the behaviors are coached without the labels. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** a label that ends up on the person page or in a room.

**A finished answer:** where the words live, the rule that they describe behavior not a person, and the two coaching moves.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.6.6 Decide the load rule for a person who takes on more than their seat, and who may say stop

**The question:** at a house where teaching is paid, unlocks are self-paced, and designations rotate, who notices when one willing person is carrying too much, and who may stop it?

**Why it matters now:** the puller problem at Sŏn has no manager in the loop to catch it. The surfaces that assign extras (the learning platform, the mentor and assessor pools, the rotation) are being configured now. A ceiling is cheap to build in and expensive to add later.

**Openers:**
- A server authored three modules last month, mentors two new hires, holds the room designation twice a week, and picked up four extra shifts. They are earning more than anyone. Is that a success story?
- Sideways: the white paper calls it cooling failure: running people at full capacity "until they stop, and calls it dedication until it becomes turnover" (WP p. 13). The book calls it a puller. Same person.
- Who, tonight, would have seen that pile? Would anyone?

**Narrowing questions:**
- What does the house read to see one person carrying too much: designations held, modules in a period, active pairings, shifts worked, the person's own line?
- Is there a ceiling, as a reset parameter? On what?
- May a lead decline work on a person's behalf, or only ask the three questions with them?
- A goal met by one person's overload: 2.2.23 already scores it. Is anything added here?

**What the book says:** before a puller takes on work, three questions: most qualified, most important, what they would give up; coach them to say no (p. 423).

**White-paper default:** "the more modules completed, the more earned" (WP p. 19); cooling failure (WP p. 13). Silent on who notices.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Houses that pay for extras (training, opening shifts, events) commonly cap them per period to protect the schedule, not the person; the cap does the person's protection as a side effect.

**Options:** (a) a hard ceiling per person, enforced by the surfaces. (b) no ceiling; the lead asks the three questions when the record shows a pile. (c) a soft threshold that surfaces, and a hard ceiling above it. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** a ceiling that also caps the earnings the white paper promised; a lead's discretion to "decline on their behalf" becoming the manager's judgment the design removed.

**A finished answer:** the list of what is read, the ceiling if any as a number for the reset, who may say stop and how.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen's version.

### 5.6.7 Decide how a lead's time is spent: evenly, by ask, or by potential

**The question:** beyond the fixed check-in cadence, where does a lead's attention go?

**Why it matters now:** the book's advice (spend the most time on the highest return) is a manager's judgment about who is worth more. Sŏn designed that judgment out of advancement. Whether it survives in a lead's calendar is a real question.

**Openers:**
- You have two hours this week that are not check-ins or service. Who gets them: the person who asked, the person who is quietest, or the person you think could be the next lead?
- Sideways: the book's Gmail story (p. 427): the unsolicited idea that became the model. Where at Sŏn does that idea go if the lead thinks it is wrong?
- Would you pay for one person's course at another room? Who decides?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Discretionary time: by ask, spread evenly, or by the lead's read?
- Outside learning for one person: does the house pay, and who decides?
- An idea from an unusual angle: must a lead route it to 5.3.10's test-first step or a working group, or may they judge it?

**What the book says:** spend the most time on the highest long-term return; unlock "interesting" talent with projects, courses, apprenticeship; the unusual angle sometimes delivers the breakthrough (pp. 426 to 427).

**White-paper default:** the documented system "favors the people who want it most" (WP p. 11); the feedback channel closes the loop (WP p. 22).

**How others have handled it:** Nothing useful beyond the book.

**Options:** (a) by ask. (b) evenly. (c) by potential, said aloud. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** "by potential" with nothing said to the person; "evenly" that makes a lead unable to lean in when someone is close.

**A finished answer:** one rule for discretionary time, one for outside investment with a decider, one for the unusual idea.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.6.8 Decide what the house offers a person the pool holds with no seat open, and when the wait is named

**The question:** the system has certified a person ready and there is no seat. What happens next, and how long before someone says so out loud?

**Why it matters now:** this is where the white paper's advocate goes quiet. The book's stalled high performer had a manager to blame; Sŏn's has a pool. If the house has no answer, the pool is where its best people learn to leave.

**Openers:**
- Picture the first person to pass the assessment for a lead seat with both leads still in place and happy. Month one in the pool. Month four. Month nine. What did each month feel like, and who said what?
- Sideways: the book's line is "I'm worried we won't have enough interesting projects for you in six months" (p. 424). At Sŏn, who says it, and is six months the number?
- The book says there is more opportunity than you think: hiring, running the business review, architecture (pp. 424 to 425). Name Sŏn's versions without inventing anything.
- Is the pool something a person is proud to be in, or something they hide?

**Narrowing questions:**
- How long in the pool before the wait is named aloud at a check-in? As a parameter.
- What is offered meanwhile: the adjacent strand, another service period, module authorship, the assessor or mentor pool, a working group, leading a fix they surfaced?
- Do the two write a what-next note together, in the person's words? Where does it live?
- The structure is not reshaped for one person unless 4.3.6 says so. Confirm that holds here.
- When the honest answer is "your next seat is not in this building," who says it, and when?

**What the book says:** anticipate boredom and say it early; brainstorm what is next together, including outside; there is more opportunity than you think; reshape the structure; consider succession (pp. 423 to 425).

**White-paper default:** with no position open the person enters a ready-now pool; the pool surfaces who is furthest along when a key person leaves (WP pp. 11, 14). Silent on the wait itself.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Restaurant groups that grow by opening new rooms use the next opening as the answer to the pool. A single building has to answer with breadth (the other strand, the other period) or with honesty.

**Options:** (a) the wait is named at a fixed parameter, the offers are the list of existing mechanisms, and a what-next note is written in the person's words. (b) the same without the note; the check-in line carries it. (c) the pool is simply the pool; nothing is added until a seat opens. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** offers that do not exist yet (an assessor pool with no assessors); a note that drifts into a lead's development plan for the person; a parameter set by hope rather than by what a person will tolerate.

**A finished answer:** the parameter, the list of offers, yes or no on the note and where it lives, and the sentence for when the seat is elsewhere.

**Capture for the kit:** which questions drew out what the person actually wanted; whether the order (what they are ready for, the pool's order, what meanwhile, when we look again) worked; what inputs the person needed beforehand.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.6.9 Decide how "not yet" is told

**The question:** when a person's assessment rows are not met, or a lead's praise gave them a different picture than the rows did, who says what?

**Why it matters now:** the assessment (4.1.11) is Sŏn's promotion decision. The first one will happen in training services before opening. The words need to exist before the first person hears them.

**Openers:**
- Someone you have told for three months that they are excellent fails the assessment. They look at you, not the assessor. What do you say?
- Sideways: the book's own story is the individual contributor who had not learned to scale through her reports; six months of specific work got her there (p. 430). The specificity was the gift. Where are Sŏn's specifics: the rows?
- "Will I pass next time?" What is the honest answer that is not a prediction?

**Narrowing questions:**
- What does the assessor say: the rows, the retake window, nothing else?
- Is the coaching lead in the room?
- What does the lead say at the next check-in if their praise and the rows disagreed?
- Does the house ever predict a retake's outcome?

**What the book says:** gut-check whether the person can sustain the next level; own the decision; name specifically what is missing; own your own praise gap (pp. 429 to 431).

**White-paper default:** the assessment is scheduled by the system and signed off by the domain lead (WP p. 11); no surprises (WP p. 19).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Trades with practical certification (culinary and sommelier examinations among them) separate examiner from teacher and report by criterion; the teacher debriefs afterwards. It is the pattern 3.2.35 already uses for the hiring practical.

**Options:** (a) assessor reads rows and window, lead takes the praise gap to the check-in. (b) lead delivers both. Depth: cons. 8.

**Watch for:** an assessor who softens the rows into a prediction; a lead who owns the praise gap by blaming the rows.

**A finished answer:** who is in the room, the assessor's words, the lead's words at the check-in, and the rule on prediction.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen's assessments.

### 5.6.10 Decide whether the house asks "what would make you leave," of whom, and how

**The question:** does anyone at Sŏn ask people, before they leave, what would make them?

**Why it matters now:** the pulse (2.3.15) and the career conversation guide (4.4.13) are being written. The question either goes in now or waits for the first surprise resignation.

**Openers:**
- The last time someone good quit on you, what did they say in the exit conversation that you could have heard six months earlier if you had asked?
- Sideways: the book asks only the top 10 to 20 percent (p. 427). At a forty-person house, who is not asked, and what do they conclude?
- Would you rather hear it in a number on the pulse or in a sentence at a check-in?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Asked at all?
- Of whom: everyone in the pulse, everyone at the career conversation, each lead by a partner, or the book's top group (which needs 5.6.2 option c)?
- Where do answers go: channel items with owners, the leads' review in aggregate?
- The departure retro is 4.3.8 and 4.8.14. Anything to add there, such as asking colleagues, which prior work argued against?

**What the book says:** interview the top group once a year; three to five conversations surface pain points; retro every departure with the person and close colleagues (pp. 427 to 428).

**White-paper default:** feedback runs both directions (WP p. 19); employee NPS tracked regularly (WP p. 22); exit interviews read for what could have been better (WP p. 23). No group singled out.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Stay interviews asked of whole teams are common in high-turnover sectors; the answers are acted on visibly or the practice breeds cynicism. Cons. 9.

**Options:** (a) everyone, through the pulse or the career conversation. (b) each lead by a partner, everyone else through the pulse. (c) the book's top group. Depth: cons. 9.

**Watch for:** a question asked with no owner for the answer.

**A finished answer:** yes or no; whom; by whom and where; where the answer goes.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.6.11 Decide the leaving-to-grow rule

**The question:** when the house helps a person leave because their next step is elsewhere, is that a loss on the books, and what does the house do for them?

**Why it matters now:** the retention definition (2.2.25) and the departure read (4.3.17) are being written. The white paper names both retention and growth beyond the building as measures (WP p. 23); without a rule they contradict each other.

**Openers:**
- A cook you trained leaves to open their own place. In the leads' review, is that the same line as the cook who left because the schedule was wrong?
- Sideways: the white paper's longest measure is "how people grow beyond the building" (WP p. 23). Say that to Dominic beside a retention target.
- A lead who has outgrown the building: who tells them, and when? Does the same apply to a founding seat?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Does the departure read carry a "left to grow" reading in the person's words, counted separately in retention?
- What does the house do for the person: an introduction, a reference, a standing invitation back, first read on a seat in a later property?
- Who tells a lead the truth that they have outgrown the building?
- Is a founding seat's succession on the same footing (2.1.8, 2.1.9), or parked?

**What the book says:** do not hold on too long; land them elsewhere or open your network; you earn loyalty either way (p. 426); play the person's path first (pp. 424 to 425).

**White-paper default:** growth beyond the building is the longest measure (WP p. 23); retention is evidence the commitments are real (WP p. 23); regulars may follow a server to another property (WP p. 25).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Kitchens with a reputation as training grounds treat alumni as an asset and say so publicly; their retention numbers read worse and their hiring is easier. Both effects are real.

**Options:** (a) a separate reading and count. (b) no distinction. Depth: cons. 10.

**Watch for:** a category that any departure gets dressed in; a promise of a seat in a later property, which WP p. 36 says not to assert.

**A finished answer:** the reading and how it is counted, the list of what the house does, who tells a lead, and whether the founders' seats are in or parked.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
