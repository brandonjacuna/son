# 5.6 Managing high performers: considerations

Context for the working session. Topics follow the book's order (pp. 419 to 431). White paper cited as WP p. N. The prior extraction's positions appear as options with their reasoning, not as rules.

## 1. What the white paper holds, and where it is silent

The white paper's whole design assumes the manager is not the one who decides who rises. "No one's advancement waits on a manager's attention or judgment. The system is the advocate" (WP p. 11). Every position has documented competencies anyone can see; the next skill set unlocks after a set period; the practical assessment auto-schedules with the domain lead; with no seat open, the person enters a ready-now pool (WP p. 11). Growth is outward, not upward: a longer-tenured person "does not move toward the center," their node develops more threads (WP p. 11). Cross-strand fluency is "the real development marker," recognized in compensation (WP pp. 10 to 11, 15).

It also holds: a mentor is "a proven cultural steward rather than simply the strongest performer" (WP p. 18); any qualified team member can author training modules and is paid for teaching (WP p. 19); peer recognition runs through a platform (WP p. 19); reviews focus on "the path forward rather than a verdict" (WP p. 19); the goal is that "the building rarely hires a manager from outside at all" (WP p. 19); retention and internal promotion rate are "direct evidence the development commitments are real" (WP p. 23); "how people grow beyond the building" is the longest culture measure (WP p. 23); a server who moves to another property in the group may bring their regulars (WP p. 25); the building runs roughly eight leadership lines across the whole day (WP p. 22).

Silent on: what a high performer is or how one would be read; what the pool is like to wait in; who notices when one person is doing too much; counter-offers, retention pay, titles as rewards; whether any group is asked what would make them leave; what a lead does for a person whose next seat is outside the building.

## 2. Whether the house names high performers (5.6.2)

The book treats "top performer" as a known category and asks the manager to understand what drives each one (p. 419). The white paper has no such category, and prior work argued that any list, field, or count that sorts people functions as a rating, which it had closed off from pay, reviews, and the person's file (digest).

Options:
- (a) No named category. A lead reads what the record already holds (unlocks, cross-strand markers, modules authored) and what the person says at the career conversation. Commits Sŏn to a lead who may know who is carrying more but has nowhere to write it, and to 5.6.10's stay question being asked of everyone or no one.
- (b) A lead's private read, never written. The book's understanding of "what drives them" lives in the lead's head and the check-in. Commits Sŏn to 4.4.2's rule on what a lead may write about a person and to trusting the lead's read stays out of scheduling and designations.
- (c) A named class for a defined purpose (the book's top 10 to 20 percent for the stay conversation). Commits Sŏn to a list someone maintains and a criterion someone defends, and to explaining to the rest why they are not on it.

What is never a source, whichever option: a customer rating, a recognition tally, a designation count (86akhcz7b), a hiring scorecard (17tn048qc2e).

## 3. The ceiling, told plainly (5.6.3)

The book: on small specialized teams where opportunity caps out, be explicit rather than let people find out the hard way; promotions get rarer with seniority, so keep setting expectations (pp. 428 to 431). Sŏn's ceiling is structural and low by design: roughly eight leadership lines (WP p. 22), no altitude (WP p. 9), and a stated intent to fill leads from within (WP p. 19). Most people who become ready will find no seat open. The candidate sheet already shows the advancement path at the first interview (WP p. 18), which is why this is phased before the first hire.

Options:
- (a) The sheet and the team home say the number of leadership lines, that readiness and a seat are different things, and what growth is available that is not a seat. Commits Sŏn to saying it aloud to every candidate and to meaning the non-seat growth.
- (b) The sheet shows the path and stays quiet on the odds. Commits Sŏn to the conversation happening later, at a check-in, with a person who may feel misled.

Divergence from the book: the book's "other ways to recognize" (p. 431) means things a manager gives. At Sŏn most of them are things the system opens (the adjacent strand, teaching, assessing) and one the rotation floor governs (designations, 4.1.20).

## 4. Retention levers (5.6.4)

The book rewards high standards with promotion and raises (pp. 421 to 422) and treats a counter-offer as ordinary. The white paper's compensation is built into menu price and distributed on a point system weighted by days worked (WP p. 20); how cross-strand fluency is paid is an open item (WP p. 15). 5.5 owns the architecture. Prior work argued that pay with a fixed, narrow set of inputs leaves no figure a manager could offer, so counter-offers and retention bonuses cannot exist without breaking the architecture (digest).

Options:
- (a) No counter-offers, no retention pay, no title outside the structure; the response to an outside offer is the what-next conversation and, if the answer is still outside, a clean goodbye with the network opened. Commits Sŏn to losing some people it could have kept, and to 5.5's architecture staying whole.
- (b) A counter-offer permitted for the lead seats only, decided by both partners together. Commits Sŏn to a two-tier truth: the architecture holds for everyone but two people.
- (c) The book's practice: raises and titles as retention tools within a band. Commits Sŏn to reopening 5.5 and to a lead's judgment entering pay, which WP p. 11 designs out.

General practice, not Sŏn-specific: hospitality groups with a transparent, formula-driven pay model commonly refuse counter-offers on the grounds that one exception makes the formula a fiction; groups with negotiated pay commonly make them. Neither is unusual.

## 5. Pushers and pullers (5.6.5, 5.6.6)

The book's two patterns (pp. 420 to 423) describe real behavior and a coaching response each. The pusher's critique is a resource if routed; the puller's yes is a slow burnout. At Sŏn the puller problem has a structural twin the book does not have: paid teaching ("the more modules completed, the more earned," WP p. 19), self-paced unlocks, mentor pairings, assessor duty, and per-service designations all reward saying yes, and none of them is assigned by a manager who might notice the pile. The white paper's own frame calls this cooling failure: running people at full capacity "until they stop, and calls it dedication until it becomes turnover" (WP p. 13). Prior work argued that a goal met at a workload cost is a system failure, never a result to reward (86akht39y, 2.2.23).

Considerations:
- Vocabulary: "pusher" and "puller" are useful at a check-in as a description of a pattern the person can recognize. Written on a person, they become a type. 5.1.5 already decides what a hypothesis may name.
- What the house can read without a manager's impression: designations held (4.3.13 already reads concentration), modules authored per period, active pairings, shifts worked, the person's own check-in line.
- Who says stop: the book coaches the puller to say no themselves (p. 423). A ceiling as a reset parameter says it for them and removes the discretion. A lead who may decline work on a person's behalf reintroduces the manager's judgment the white paper removes, which prior work flagged (digest).

Options for the load rule:
- (a) A ceiling per person on concurrent extras (designations, modules in progress, pairings), set at the reset, enforced by the surfaces that assign them. Commits Sŏn to a number that will sometimes stop a willing person.
- (b) No ceiling; the lead asks the book's three questions at the check-in when the record shows a pile. Commits Sŏn to the lead noticing, and to the puller who suffers silently being missed.
- (c) Both: a soft threshold that surfaces to the lead and the person, and a hard ceiling above it.

## 6. A lead's time and potential (5.6.7)

The book: spend the most time on the highest long-term return; unlock "interesting" talent with projects, courses, apprenticeship; hear the unusual angle (pp. 426 to 427). At Sŏn the check-in cadence is fixed and equal (2.3.10), so this is about the discretionary remainder. The white paper's line that a documented system "favors the people who want it most" (WP p. 11) puts the initiative with the person.

Options:
- (a) Discretionary time follows the ask: whoever brings something to a check-in gets the time. Commits Sŏn to the quiet person getting less, which the puller pattern predicts.
- (b) Spread evenly by rule. Commits Sŏn to a lead who cannot lean in.
- (c) The lead's read of potential governs, said aloud to the person. Commits Sŏn to a manager's judgment about who is worth more time, and to 4.4.2 limiting what is written about it.

Outside investment (a course, a stage in another room): the white paper's platform is internal (WP p. 19). Whether the house ever pays for one person's outside learning, and who decides, is undecided. The unusual angle: 5.3.10's test-first step and 4.1.30's working group already exist as places to try an idea; the decision is whether a lead must route an idea there rather than judge it.

## 7. The pool wait (5.6.8)

This is the chunk's center for Sŏn. The book's stalled high performer is someone whose manager has no project for them; the fix is the manager finding or inventing one, reshaping the team, or letting go (pp. 423 to 426). Sŏn's stalled high performer is a person the system has certified ready and placed in a pool with nothing open (WP p. 11). The system that was supposed to be the advocate has nothing to say next. Prior work named nine existing mechanisms that already carry people forward without a manager (self-paced unlocks, cross-training markers, paid teaching, career conversations, rotation designations, the internal pool, among them) and argued they are enough if named (17tn048qfmx).

Considerations:
- The book's "more opportunity than you think" (pp. 424 to 425) maps onto things that exist at Sŏn: module authorship, the assessor pool (4.1.14), the mentor pool (3.3.13), a working group (4.1.30), leading a fix the person surfaced through the channel, the adjacent strand (4.1.10), another service period (3.3.29).
- The book's "reshape the structure" is decided at 4.3.6 (whether the structure is ever changed for one person) and 4.1.36 (when a leadership line is added). This chunk does not reopen those.
- The book's six-month warning ("I'm worried we won't have enough interesting projects for you") becomes a parameter here: how long in the pool before the wait is named aloud.
- The what-next note (5.6.13) is the book's "brainstorm together" written down in the person's words. Prior work would have refused any document about a person in a lead's voice; a note in the person's own words, kept where they agree, is a different thing, and whether it exists at all is Brandon's call.

Divergence: the book's manager creates opportunity. Sŏn's lead can only open what exists and say plainly when nothing does. That honesty is the white paper's own rule ("never assert the unproven," WP p. 36, applied to a person's path).

## 8. Telling someone not yet (5.6.9)

The book: gut-check readiness, own the decision, name what is missing, and own your own praise gap if you have one (pp. 429 to 431). At Sŏn the readiness call is an assessment against rows (4.1.11), signed off by the domain lead, so "not yet" has an objective source the book's manager lacks. The remaining questions are human: what the assessor says, whether the coaching lead is present, and what a lead does when their own praise painted a different picture than the rows.

Options:
- (a) The assessor reads the rows and the retake window and says nothing else; the lead takes the praise gap to the next check-in. Commits Sŏn to two conversations and a clean separation of assessor and coach (3.2.37's pattern).
- (b) The lead delivers both together. Commits Sŏn to the coach also being the judge in the room, which the assessor design avoids.

No prediction: prior work argued the house never answers "will I pass next time" (digest). The book agrees for the low-performer case (p. 439); here the question is the same.

## 9. The stay question (5.6.10)

The book: interview the top group once a year on what would make them leave; three to five conversations surface pain points (pp. 427 to 428). Prior work argued against a top-talent list and against interviewing a departing person's colleagues, on the grounds of unequal treatment and secondhand accounts (digest). The white paper already runs a regular pulse (WP p. 22) and reads exit interviews (WP p. 23). 4.3.8 and 4.8.13 own the departure read and the exit conversation.

Options:
- (a) The question goes to everyone, in the pulse or at the career conversation, and the answers become channel items with owners. Commits Sŏn to hearing it from people the book would not have asked, and to acting on it.
- (b) A partner asks each lead once a year. Commits Sŏn to the two people with the most options being heard directly, and everyone else through the pulse.
- (c) The book's practice, which needs 5.6.2 option (c).

General practice, not Sŏn-specific: "stay interviews" asked of whole teams, not a top group, are a common retention practice in sectors with high turnover; the question set is short and the answers are acted on visibly or the practice breeds cynicism (which WP p. 22 also says of feedback generally).

## 10. Leaving to grow (5.6.11)

The book: do not hold a high performer too long; land them elsewhere in the company or open your network; you earn their loyalty either way (p. 426). The white paper names growth beyond the building as the longest measure of the environment (WP p. 23) and, in the group's future, a server whose regulars follow them to another property (WP p. 25). The tension: retention rate is also named as evidence the commitments are real (WP p. 23). A departure the house helped with counts against one measure and for the other unless the definition separates them.

Options:
- (a) The departure read (4.3.17) carries a "left to grow" reading in the person's words and the retention definition counts it separately. Commits Sŏn to a category someone could game and to reading two numbers.
- (b) No distinction; retention is retention. Commits Sŏn to a metric that punishes the outcome the white paper calls its longest measure.

What the house does for the person: a founder's introduction, a reference, a standing invitation back, first read on a seat in a later property (4.8.13 decides contact with former employees). The founders' own seats: the book's "moving yourself out of your own job" (p. 425) is a founder's question; whether a founding seat's succession is planned is 2.1.8's and 2.1.9's, and this chunk only asks whether a lead who has outgrown the building is told so, and by whom.

## 11. Counsel

Nothing in this chunk needs counsel. A reference given for a departing person and what may be said about them is 4.3.14's.

## 12. Where Sŏn diverges from the book, in sum

- The manager does not decide who rises; the record and the assessment do (WP p. 11). Most of the book's manager moves become things the system opens or the rotation floor governs.
- The ceiling is structural and stated at the first interview, not discovered.
- The puller problem is a design problem first (paid teaching, self-paced everything), so the load rule is a parameter before it is a coaching conversation.
- Leaving to grow is a measured outcome, not a retention failure, if 5.6.11 keeps the distinction.
