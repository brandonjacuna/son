# 6.3 Consider your career: considerations

Context for the working session. The book's last section (pp. 498 to 503) is the most personal in it: your own career, kept in your own document, reread on your own rhythm, with your own counsel. White paper cited as WP p. N. This file frames those questions for each founder and adds the house's side, which the book never needed: what reads a founder when no one stands above, what absorbs a founder's failure, and what the house does when a founder leaves. Nothing here says where any founder's path leads, what they are good at, what they enjoy, or what they are paid.

## 1. What the white paper holds, and where it is silent

The white paper reads Brandon's career as a shape, not a title: "into each segment, the best version of it he could find, to learn what worked and what did not, then out, carrying the piece worth keeping" (WP p. 05). That is a fact about how the white paper describes him, quoted because it is the one place the record and the book's "envision the shape, not a title" (p. 500) meet. It is not a statement about where he goes next.

The house is built to run without its founders (WP pp. 05 to 06). When a key person leaves, "the tension that node held redistributes, which costs something, the web is weaker until the node is re-established, but the knowledge does not leave" (WP p. 14), and the departure is "bounded, temporary, calculable." The business "does not rest on the survival of any one person in it" (WP p. 37). The horizon is "gated on the proof, never a promise" (WP p. 38).

For the team, the path is explicit before anyone starts it (WP p. 11); "advancement requires preparation, not permission" (WP p. 14); internal succession is the goal "to the point where the building rarely hires a manager from outside at all" (WP p. 19); and the longest measure of the environment is "how people grow beyond the building" (WP p. 23). Reviews develop rather than judge, and feedback runs both directions (WP p. 19). Management absorbs blame from staff (WP p. 17).

Silent on: where either founder's path leads; any founder's own reflection or its rhythm; a review of a founder; who absorbs a founder's failure; a founder leaving; the operating agreement; founder pay, which this manual never records either.

## 2. A founder's path (6.3.2)

The book: you are your own career coach; no one else knows what is in your head, heart, and gut; careers wind and make sense in hindsight; every five years envision the shape of what you want to be doing, not a title, and check your ambitions against your demonstrated impact; above all do not adopt someone else's path (pp. 498 to 502).

The earlier work offered a structural definition, so the house could have something to read without reading the founder: in a house built to run without its founders, a founder's path is the sequence of hand-overs by which a seat's domain moves from being held by the founder to being held by the record and the leads, freeing the founder for the next thing only that seat can do. It is readable directly off the handover list (4.4.18), the knowledge-transfer list (1.1.12), and the seat inventory if 6.1.5 kept one. Where the path leads, the definition does not say and the house does not ask. Its one convergence with 6.1: a founder stuck holding by-default loads past their dates is a founder whose path has stopped, and 6.1.4's read sees that as overload before anyone sees it as a career.

Options:
- (a) The structural definition, and that is all the house reads. Commits Sŏn to the handover list being honest, and to the house knowing nothing about a founder's path except that the seat is emptying on schedule.
- (b) Entirely private; the house reads nothing, not even the hand-over sequence as a path. Commits Sŏn to 4.4.18 being read only as delegation, which it already is.
- (c) Each founder states one line of direction for the coming year to the other at the annual block, unrecorded beyond that. Commits the partners to the book's twelve-month question asked between them, with no record.

The white paper's shape for the company (the restaurant as proof, the layer as the asset, the horizon earned, WP pp. 34, 38) is the same shape at the person scale. Whether that is a coincidence or a design is the founder's to say, and not here.

## 3. The personal career document (6.3.3)

The book's instrument: a document, started early, with a few bullets per role on six questions (were you good at it and how do you know; did you enjoy it and why; what it revealed about the work you want; where the gaps were; what you had to acquire and how hard it was; whether you wanted to keep learning), reread every 6, 12, or 18 months; for an aspiring leader it surfaces the complements to hire rather than duplicate (pp. 499 to 500).

The earlier work kept this document and the working-with-me document as separate instruments: one private, reread on the founder's own rhythm, about what you are good at and enjoy; the other exchanged, dated, versioned, revised on 1.1.2's rule, about how to work with you. It refused to merge them. It also noted that the working-with-me document's "what I don't know yet" prompt exists so a founder can write "I don't know yet" instead of a plan.

Options:
- (a) Kept privately, separate, never held or read by the house. Commits Sŏn to nothing, and each founder to a second document.
- (b) Its questions folded into the working-with-me document's private prompts (the strengths line, the learning list), with no second document. Commits Sŏn to 1.1.2's document carrying reflection it was not designed for, and to the risk that private answers reach the issued version.
- (c) Not kept. Commits the founder to the book's warning: careers make sense only in hindsight, and the document is how the hindsight is kept (pp. 498 to 499).

The kit question: the book recommends this document to everyone, early. Whether the kit (6.3.12) is offered to leads and to anyone at the career conversation (4.4.3) is a house decision; the rule that the house never reads one is the same either way.

## 4. The rhythm of stepping back (6.3.4)

The book's cadence: every six months, is this the right role to learn what I want to learn next, and from whom; every twelve, should I be seeking a change to stay on the five-year path, with two or three directions in mind; every eighteen, is the five-year aspiration still right, consulting the document (pp. 500 to 502). The earlier work refused it as a house rhythm: the house's rhythms are the reset and the annual block (2.3.30), and a founder's stepping back is not the house's to schedule.

The house does hold two natural points: the reset after dinner's first quarter and the annual block after the first year (WP p. 36; 2.3.30), the latter with the founders' working-with-me documents already on the table. The learning half of the six-month question is 6.1.10's.

Options:
- (a) The founders' own, unscheduled by the house. Commits Sŏn to nothing, and the founder to remembering.
- (b) The annual block holds a closed hour with no record, in which each founder asks the book's questions of themselves, with the other partner present or not as each chooses. Commits Sŏn to protecting an hour it cannot read, which is the point.
- (c) The working-with-me document's revision rule (1.1.2) is the only rhythm the house holds, and the founder's own questions ride on it. Commits Sŏn to the revision being the moment, and to the private questions staying out of the revision.

## 5. The founders' own review (6.3.5)

The book: nobody taps you on the shoulder; outside advice is welcome (p. 498). Elsewhere she describes going years without a review and the one that named her strengths as formative (pp. 384 to 385). The white paper's review is developmental, every three to six months, with feedback running both directions (WP p. 19). 5.4.7 named the review holder "for each founder, if any"; that "if any" is this decision.

The earlier work's finding: a person's review at Sŏn needs a check-in holder, a seat sheet, and competency rows; a founder has none, so there is no review, and none should be built under another name. What partially reads a founder anyway: the domain read (what the domain produced, monthly, in writing, 2.3.4); the load line (what the building failed to supply the seat, 4.2.12, 6.1.4); the inclusiveness observation (4.7.12); the leads' upward halves (5.4.13); a founder's domain failure written up, if 1.2.3 chose that. Conduct is read exactly as for anyone (6.2.9). None of it carries a rating, none has an interval of its own, and none reaches pay, since a founder's pay is the operating agreement's and outside this program entirely. It left one question open for the founders: whether the domain read should formally use the review's dimensions (5.4.3) or stay as readiness rows and goal pages.

Options:
- (a) No review under any name. The four reads above are what reads a founder. Commits Sŏn to a founder never hearing, in one place, how their seat is doing, and to the book's "nobody taps you on the shoulder" being true here by design.
- (b) The monthly domain read uses 5.4.3's dimensions on the seat's outcomes, in writing, with no rating and no interval beyond the read's own. Commits Sŏn to the partners reading each other on the same dimensions the leads are read on, which is either fairness or a review by another name.
- (c) A mutual partner review once a year at the annual block, in the check-in's form, on the person page's form, held by the other partner. Commits Sŏn to a founder having a person page, which the earlier work refused, and to the two partners reviewing each other while tied in every reserved decision.

Whatever is chosen: nothing about a founder's performance reaches pay through this program; the founder's own account is on the page if any read exists (the book's "the manager writes after reading the self input," 5.4.9); a lead's impression of a founder is never a source (5.1.3, extended in reverse).

How others have handled it: General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Where there is a board, the board reviews the chief executive, usually annually, often through a 360. Where there is not, founder-led companies commonly have no review of the founders at all, and some substitute a coach (6.2.10) or a mutual co-founder review. Sŏn's earlier position is the "no review" case with the reads that exist anyway made explicit.

## 6. What absorbs a founder's failure (6.3.6)

The book: in a fast-growing company most mistakes are quickly forgotten because everything is changing; reframe and move on (p. 489); be the manager your own manager can lean on (p. 502). The white paper: management absorbs blame from staff; post-mortems are private and process-focused; the process is fixed so it does not happen again, to anyone (WP p. 17). Nothing in that design is above management, so nothing absorbs a founder's failure by structure.

The earlier work's answer: three things absorb it, none of them a person below. First, the same accountability principle run on the founders' own domains first, at the partners' review, with an evidence note written by the other partner naming no person. Second, the record itself: a founder's mechanism failure becomes a process update and a version, and the version is the absorption. Third, the reserved decision classes held at the status quo (2.1.13), so neither partner can unilaterally turn the other's failure into an unagreed change. What does not absorb it: a lead (that would reverse the load-bearing structure the whole system exists to avoid), the team, or the public. It read the white paper's own public admission, "Sŏn has done it too, which is how the team knows the trap" (WP p. 17), as the model: a founder's failures are absorbed by being written into the record, because the record is the only thing above the founders the house actually holds. 1.2.3 decided whether a founder's domain failure gets the house's post-mortem and who reads it; 5.2.10 decided how management absorbs blame in practice.

Options:
- (a) The record absorbs it, as above. Commits Sŏn to a founder's failures being versioned rather than forgotten, and to the other partner writing the evidence note.
- (b) The other partner absorbs it in front of the leads, as a lead does for their team. Commits Sŏn to a partner standing between the leads and the other partner's failure, which the partners may or may not want.
- (c) The founder absorbs their own, said to the leads at the leads' review. Commits Sŏn to the book's candor (p. 52, by cross-reference) and to a founder in a room the leads chair (2.3.13).

Where Sŏn diverges from the book: the book forgives because the company forgets. Sŏn's record does not forget. Whether that is heavier or lighter for a founder is the founder's to say; the design only decides who writes it down.

## 7. A founder's departure (6.3.8)

The book does not treat a founder leaving; its closest line is that careers wind and pivotal moments are not always visible at the time (pp. 498 to 499). The white paper never describes a founder's departure; the operating agreement governs first, and this program does not read or interpret it (2.1.1 noted what it holds). 4.3.8 and 4.3.10 built the departure read for anyone and said a founder's departure is referred to 2.1 and runs on the same records.

The earlier work confined the house's part to continuity: the same three-pass departure read used for anyone, applied to the founding seat as the node (what the seat held per the inventory; a memory-failure pass on the knowledge-transfer list); a versioned structure-change record with the departure as its stated reason (4.1.34); the seat's inventory rows moving to the remaining partner as a flagged, dated overload item; a scheduled hand-over conversation running against the records in the final period; and the leads learning of the change through the normal structure-change notification path (4.1.12), which carries coverage information only, never anything about the person. What is explicitly not the house's to design: the reason, the vote, terms, pay, equity, notice, restrictive covenants, incapacity and death provisions, and whether founders are employees for any purpose. All of that is the operating agreement's and counsel's.

Options:
- (a) Continuity only, as above. Commits Sŏn to running a founder's departure on the records anyone's runs on, and to the remaining partner carrying dated overload rather than an undated inheritance.
- (b) In addition, a scheduled hand-over conversation in the final period run against the records. Commits Sŏn to a conversation the agreement may not provide for, and that the circumstances may not permit.
- (c) Nothing designed until it happens. Commits Sŏn to the white paper's claim about departures (WP p. 14) being untested for the seats where it matters most.

How others have handled it: General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Operating agreements commonly carry buy-sell, incapacity, and deadlock provisions; what they rarely carry is an operational continuity plan, which is the gap the house's note (6.3.13) fills without touching the legal instrument.

## 8. What the founders owe the team's path (6.3.9)

The book closes the career section by naming the privilege of having the position to choose, and suggests using some of it to help others who lack the same choices (p. 500). At Sŏn that has a concrete form: the functions the founders hold by default before the leads exist (3.1.4, 3.2.6, 3.3.6, 2.3.5) are exactly the first seats and designations the internal pool is meant to fill (WP p. 19). The earlier work stated it as dated commitments already made elsewhere: competency requirements and unlock periods exist before the cohort arrives; the pool is read before any outside hire (3.1.40); career conversations happen once per person (4.4.3); and a founder keeping a by-default load past its date has closed a path someone in the pool was on.

Options:
- (a) Every by-default founder function has a date on the handover list (4.4.18) and the pool is read first when it opens (3.1.40); nothing more is written. Commits Sŏn to what it has already committed to.
- (b) In addition, the founder-held functions that could become a seat or a designation are shown on the opportunities page (4.7.17) with a rough horizon, so the team can prepare. Commits Sŏn to a public statement about founder hand-overs, which the team will hold the founders to.
- (c) No commitment beyond 4.4.18. Commits Sŏn to the same as (a) without saying so.

## 9. The closing read (6.3.14)

The book ends by returning to fundamentals: understand your energy, your abilities, and the boundaries you need; if you are not feeling stable, take stock, delegate, reframe, and ask for help; be the manager your own manager can lean on and the colleague others want to be near; when you feel less strong, return to your starting point, yourself (p. 502). Its stated intent is confidence, self-awareness, the ability to say the hard thing, and eventually the reader's own operating system (pp. 502 to 503).

The earlier work read six founder-facing principles from the start of the program against everything built since, and made one substitution in the book's closing move: at Sŏn, the thing a founder returns to under strain is not the self but the record, since the fundamentals are written where the house can read them without asking a founder to remember. The one place "return to yourself" survives whole is the working-with-me document and its three prerequisite exercises (1.1.6), the one house document about a founder that the founder writes. The six principles' marks and status table are dropped as machinery; what 6.3.14 does instead is list every founder-facing rule the manual now holds, with its home and status, against the book's closing list, and name what is still open.

## 10. Counsel

Collected in 6.3.7, from this chunk and from 6.1.9, 6.2.4, 6.2.9, 2.2.5, and 5.5.7: the operating agreement's departure, incapacity, deadlock, and removal provisions; whether the founders are employees for any purpose; how a founder as the subject of a conduct concern is handled beyond what the house designs, and what record may be kept; how the founders' place in or out of the pool bears on its legal character; what records and lawful interim holders apply to a founder's departure; whether the founders' working documents, the seat inventory, and any load line are records for any legal purpose; whether a founder's outside commitments raise any duty. None is answered in this manual.

## 11. Where Sŏn may diverge from the book

- The book's career section is entirely personal. Sŏn keeps it that way for the path, the document, and the rhythm (6.3.2 to 6.3.4), and adds a house side the book never needed (6.3.5 to 6.3.9), because the founders have no manager, no board, and no one to tap them on the shoulder.
- The book's manager is reviewed by someone. Sŏn's founders may choose no review under any name (6.3.5), with the reads that exist anyway made explicit.
- The book forgives mistakes because the company forgets. Sŏn's record versions them, and the record is what absorbs a founder's failure (6.3.6).
- The book does not treat a founder leaving. Sŏn designs continuity in advance and leaves everything else to the operating agreement (6.3.8).
- The book's "return to yourself" becomes, at Sŏn, "return to the record," with the founder's own document the one place the self survives whole (6.3.14).
