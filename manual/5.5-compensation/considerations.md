# 5.5 Compensation: considerations

What the white paper holds, what the earlier work argued, the options, and where Sŏn may diverge from the book. Numbered so `session.md` can point at a section. No financial figure appears here; where one would be needed, financials are not a source for this work and the value is set in Dominic's domain. Nothing here is a decision.

## 1. The philosophy

**White paper.** Labor is "an asset to invest in," not a cost to minimize (WP p. 16). "If it matters to the experience, Sŏn pays for it. Costs are not shifted to employees" (WP p. 19). The culinary team is brought "into the upside it is usually walled out of" (WP p. 20). Cross-strand fluency is "recognized in compensation as meaningful" (WP p. 11). Teaching is paid, and "the more modules completed, the more earned, a direct connection between personal growth and financial reward" (WP p. 19). The white paper never says "pay for performance."

**The book.** At minimum a company needs a core philosophy; her example is market-competitive pay with higher reward for higher performance, "pay for performance" (pp. 413 to 414). Everything else in her chapter (fixed and variable elements, levels, bands, market data, the conversation) follows from it.

**Earlier work.** A philosophy tested against three principles said to transfer from the book: pay must be legible, defensible, and connected to a review people trust. The first two were kept: a person can compute their own pay from records they can read (seat, horizon, markers, days), and the same inputs always produce the same pay. The third was deliberately not kept: pay is disconnected from the review, because the review produces development and path-forward language, never a verdict, and no feedback act touches pay. "Pay for performance" is carried instead by the unlock system (pay for an assessed competency rather than an impression), the fluency markers (pay for demonstrated, current versatility), and the pool's day weighting (pay for nights actually worked, kitchen included). Deliberately not carried: any differential reward for doing the same seat better than someone else in it.

**What the session should hold.** The philosophy is the sentence a candidate hears and a line cook repeats. It has to be consistent with 5.4.5. If the review feeds pay, "pay for performance" is honest and the earlier design is mostly undone; if it does not, the sentence has to say what pay does reward, in words that do not sound like a rating. A related finding: the task material that generated the earlier work carried a specific percentage and an outside data source; neither was used, since the white paper itself says the percentage is still being finalized (WP pp. 20, 22) and financials are not a source here.

## 2. Two layers: base pay and the pool

**White paper.** Only the pool is described: built into the menu price, never a service charge, "set at a defined percentage that is still being finalized," distributed to the whole team "on a point system weighted by the days each person works," "calculated daily rather than quarterly and paid on a reasonable rhythm" (WP p. 20). Base pay is assumed throughout ("a paid practical," "the training plan with pay dates," WP p. 18) and never named.

**The book.** Decide what is fixed and what is variable; salary is fixed, a bonus variable (pp. 413 to 414).

**Earlier work.** Name both layers. Base pay is an hourly wage for most seats and a salary for a few, one rule per seat, published like the point weight, never stated in an offer as contingent on the pool. The pool is the second layer. Every wage, hour, overtime, tip-law, and pay-transparency question the two-layer structure raises belongs to counsel (section 11).

**Options.** (a) Two named layers, both published per seat. Commits Sŏn to a public wage per seat and to explaining both on one page. (b) Base pay per seat published, the pool described but its weights visible only to the seat's candidates. (c) A single blended presentation, which the white paper's "never presented as a service charge" and the customer research behind it (WP p. 20) make hard to sustain internally without also blurring it externally.

## 3. Seat weights, the salaried seat, entry pay, fluency, unlocks, designations

**White paper.** "Every position has documented competency requirements visible to anyone" (WP p. 11); "the web never acquires altitude, only reach" (WP p. 9); designations "are not permanent elevations above anyone" (WP p. 10); "leadership and salaried roles" named together, never listed (WP p. 18); "new people always enter at the outside edge" (WP p. 9).

**The book.** Levels are the basis for pricing; bands and targets are set per role and level from market data (p. 414).

**Earlier work.**
- *Weights.* Each seat carries a published point weight at each horizon; it moves on the date the domain lead records a completed unlock (4.1.11), never on the lead's initiative. A competency for a seat not held adds a connection and possibly a marker but moves no pay until the seat is held.
- *Salaried.* 4.1.9's test: a seat that owns a domain or program across every open period rather than a set of scheduled services. Option: salary plus pool points for every open day in the pay period, by rule, since the seat's work spans every open day; no conflict arises because no seat holder can move anyone's points. Alternative: salary outside the pool, simpler and free of any appearance of a leader drawing from the same pool as the people whose reviews they hold, but a full night is then worth nothing extra to the seats that hold it.
- *A lead's entry.* The seat's own published salary and weight at its entry horizon, no added rank, consistent with 4.1.8's two webs.
- *Fluency.* Cross-strand fluency as an on-or-off marker while connections stay current; cross-period fluency the same way, because a day counts once however many periods were worked and the second register would otherwise earn nothing. Alternative: no pay recognition, fluency visible only in succession ordering.
- *Designations.* Never pay, a marker, or day credit; a designation held every service is a staffing question (4.3.13).

**What the session should weigh.** The white paper's one sentence on fluency pay is the strongest pay commitment it makes about an individual; the session should decide whether cross-period fluency inherits it or is a Sŏn addition. The salaried-in-the-pool option creates an appearance question the book would flag (a manager benefiting from the pool their team fills); the counter is that the manager cannot move anyone's points.

## 4. Market data and refresh

**White paper.** "Austin's restaurant labor market is tight ... Sŏn has to be worth choosing" (WP p. 16). No source, no refresh.

**The book.** Work with a consultant or a market-data source; early on, enough to price offers; tell employees when data is refreshed, at least annually (p. 414).

**Earlier work.** Silent beyond placing hourly wages and salaries on the register as rows set against wage law and the market. The book's refresh rule is the useful addition: a stated rhythm at which each seat's published rule is re-read, moving the rule rather than any one person's pay. The source and the values are Dominic's.

## 5. The pool's base, one pool or several, who is in it

**Base.** A defined percentage of a defined revenue base, computed once per closed day. The one rule the earlier work proposed now: a comped item counts at full menu price, so the pool never shrinks when a server uses the generosity range (WP p. 26) and the team never pays for its own generosity. Which other classes count (early-morning sales, private events, retail, tastings) is open, and some cannot be decided until that service period exists.

**One pool per day.** The earlier work's reasoning: separate pools per period make periods compete for the same people, pay a slower period's team only from its own revenue, and cut against "one restaurant ... met at different times of day" (WP p. 33); one pool also makes a cross-period marker meaningful. A per-period pool is the alternative. A practical question for the first quarter: with dinner alone open (WP p. 36), one pool and one period are the same thing; the decision matters when the early morning opens.

**Who is in.** Every working seat with a shift that day, across every domain and strand; a person in training from the first paid hour; a candidate on an unpaid trial not (3.1.37); contracted labor such as the overnight cleaning crew (WP p. 26; 4.1.29) outside by not being employed team. Whether the founders and the chef partner hold points is a separate question gated on counsel (section 11) and the operating agreement (2.1.1). Nothing about any founder's own pay is recorded in this repo.

## 6. What sets points, what never does, and what a day is

**Four sources.** The published weight for the seat and horizon held; active markers; days worked; the salaried day rule. Each is a record the person can read.

**The never-list.** Any review, check-in, rating, designation, recognition, a lead's judgment, a lagging indicator beyond its role in the base, a customer rating, sales attribution, hours as distinct from days, which period was worked, tenure, negotiation. No one, founders included, moves points by hand. The reasoning: WP p. 11's "no one's advancement waits on a manager's attention or judgment" read as extending downstream to pay; and "one rule for everyone by construction" as the defense against pay disputes.

**A day.** Any calendar day on which the person worked a scheduled shift of any length in any period, counted once. Any paid block (a check-in, a review, training, a paired service, a module hour, orientation) is a worked shift, consistent with costs not shifted to employees. A day scheduled but not worked does not count. An unexpectedly closed day is a counsel question with its pay rule as a register row.

**Options on the never-list.** (a) As drafted, with no hand adjustment anywhere. (b) As drafted, with one named adjustment (a founder's correction of a record fault) on the decision-rights register with its trigger. (c) The book's form: a discretionary component set by the manager within a band. Each commits Sŏn to a different answer on whether a person can compute their own pay.

## 7. The daily calculation, the pay page, the rhythm, and what is published

**Calculation.** At close the pool is computed from the base and the percentage, every working person's points are summed, each share is proportional to their points over the total, and each page is written by the next morning in the person's language. No lead computes anyone's pay or sees anyone else's page. Until software support exists, this is a back-office process, and 5.5.39 has it run through the training services before the first pay date.

**The page.** The day, seat and horizon, weight, active markers, points, share, accrued share to the next pay date, base hours, the day's total points, and the weight-move history. Visible to the person and their holder only (2.2.6 left the pool's visibility to this chunk).

**The unresolved legibility trade.** To check one's share by arithmetic a person needs the day's total points (not sensitive) and the day's pool amount; the pool amount and the known percentage let anyone derive daily revenue, which the transparency line keeps private. Two options: show the pool total (verifiable, revenue derivable) or withhold it while showing enough to check the share against total points and the rule (private, but the one number that decides the payout stays invisible). A middle path the earlier work did not state: show the share as a fraction of the pool without the pool's amount, so the arithmetic is checkable up to the last multiplication.

**Rhythm.** "Paid on a reasonable rhythm" (WP p. 20). The earlier work: the share pays on the same date as base pay, never later, because a share arriving weeks after the nights that earned it does not feel like the upside of a full night; hourly workers in this market are commonly paid weekly or every two weeks, so a slower cadence risks "reasonable" from the first pay date. The cycle is bounded by pay-date law and the payroll platform and is Dominic's.

**What is published house-wide.** Open, leaning favorable in the earlier work: every seat's base wage and weight visible to everyone, not only to that seat's candidates. The argument for: WP p. 11's "visible to anyone" and the comparison case (section 9) are both answered by a rule anyone can read. The argument against: it is more than most restaurants publish, and the candidate sheet already carries the seat's own pay. Whether the percentage itself ever appears on the candidate sheet is the same question one level up.

## 8. The routed pay lines: training, teaching, translation, the discretionary range

**Training.** Base pay for every scheduled training hour from the first paid hour, plus the seat's entry-horizon weight in the pool from that same hour, with no reduced training weight, because a lower weight functions as an unstated probation label. Alternative: a reduced weight during training. The unpaid trial shift stays a counsel-defined question (3.1.37).

**Teaching.** 3.3.18 recorded three constraints: authorship paid on approval, not on writing; completion paid on the platform's own record; mentoring paid as ordinary shift pay. The earlier work's forms: authorship as a flat payment per approved module and per approved revision, as base pay, with nothing per completion by others (which would reward pushing modules and rank authors by reach); completion carried by paid module time and the unlock it leads to, with no separate payment (which would reward finishing over readiness), the alternative per-completion payment stated for the founders to weigh against WP p. 19's "the more modules completed, the more earned"; mentoring as shift pay plus a teaching marker for every day a mentored plan is open, never tied to the mentee's outcome (which would make the mentor a judge) and never stacked.

**Translation and interpreting.** Translation like authorship: a flat payment per approved version, requiring the document's owner and a second fluent reviewer, as base pay; a software-assisted draft paid for the human review, with a disclosure note (4.8.3). Live interpreting is an on-clock designation carrying ordinary shift pay (4.8.4).

**The discretionary range.** Two values (what may be comped on judgment; the escalation threshold) set in Dominic's domain against the financial model, written as actual numbers on the range card (3.3.40) rather than "use your judgment," versioned, and reviewed at the reset against how often the range was used or exceeded. Until set, a person told to use judgment is "unprotected, not free." The comped-item rule in section 5 is what keeps the range from taxing the pool.

## 9. The pay conversation, comparisons, disappointment, corrections

**The book.** Compensation is the most fraught conversation; people want to know they are treated fairly and recognized (p. 414). Educate yourself; instill trust in the system and say it will evolve; understand the motivators; have the conversation on a known schedule, in person, tied to what is rewarded, and even when nothing changes (pp. 414 to 417). Redirect comparisons to the person's own performance and never disclose another's pay (pp. 417 to 418). Disappointment comes from someone who has not absorbed feedback or from a high performer with high expectations; handle each differently (pp. 417 to 418). The appendix guide (pp. 471 to 473) is a good preparation checklist.

**Earlier work.** There is no discrete pay event, so her chapter is replaced by three things: a one-time explanation at the offer; a page a person checks daily; and a destination per kind of question. Arithmetic goes to the pay page and the correction path; the rule itself to the improvement channel; what is possible next to the check-in or career conversation; another person's pay has no destination. What a lead may say: explain the mechanism, state what the published sheet says, confirm a recorded unlock's date, say "I don't know, I'll find out." May not: state a figure not on the person's own page or the sheet, discuss anyone else's pay, promise or predict an amount, link any review, check-in, designation, or recognition to pay, or imply pay could have been higher "if." Comparisons are answered by the rule: the same seat, horizon, markers, and days always produce the same share, and any difference traces to one of those four on the person's own record. Disappointment is answered the same three ways: an error through correction, a path question through the check-in from the sheet, a complaint about the rule through the channel. The book's suggestion that a pay conversation can make a managing-out concrete (p. 418) was explicitly refused: there is no pay conversation to use as a lever.

**Corrections.** Acknowledged by a named owner within a short window, corrected or declined with a written reason within a longer one, paid on the next pay date, a systemic cause routed as a process item. 2.3.16's loop window is the pattern.

**What the session should weigh.** Even with no pay event, a person's pay changes on the date an unlock is recorded, and the book's point stands: the moment should be marked, in person, and tied to what was demonstrated. Whether that is a conversation, a line at the next check-in, or the page alone is 5.5.23's. The book's "when there is no change, say so and why" (pp. 416 to 417) has no obvious Sŏn form if pay never sits still long enough to have a review point; the session should decide whether the check-in ever says anything about pay at all.

## 10. The parity read

The book checks roll-ups for bias (pp. 410 to 411). The white paper tracks retention and internal promotion "with the same seriousness as cost" (WP p. 23). 4.7.4 decided whether anyone is asked to self-identify; 4.7.5 gets counsel's view on identity data; 4.7.9 lists a pay read across the point system. The earlier work's design: since pay reduces to three rates (unlock rate, marker rate, days scheduled), apply existing anonymized tags to those rates by seat class at a periodic point, with no names, figures, targets, or individual consequence; never a performance input; marked "cannot read yet" until enough tagged data and time exist. Whether tags include self-identified demographic data is 4.7.4's with counsel.

## 11. Counsel questions

None of these is settled by designing around it. Whether embedding compensation in price rather than tips creates wage-law exposure in Texas and federally: classification, whether a tip credit applies, how overtime's regular rate is calculated when pay is revenue-linked, disclosure. Whether the daily share is legally a wage, a tip, a bonus, or something else, which bears on final pay and paid leave. Whether excluding partners from the pool changes its wage character or the honesty of "the whole team." The pay rule for an unexpectedly closed day. Whether a founder is an employee for any purpose; what happens to records and authorities on a founder's departure or incapacity; whether internal records carry retention or disclosure consequences. Separation pay and pay during an investigatory hold are register rows with 5.10 as their chunk. A reduction in hours that must not function as a disguised termination (a published non-discretionary rule for who loses shifts, seasonal patterns disclosed in advance) is 5.10's, beside 4.3.

## 12. Sequencing against the leads' offers

The candidate sheet promises the compensation mechanics at the first interview (WP p. 18), and the two leads are hired months before this chunk can run in full. 3.2.18 and 3.1.30 already decided the order in principle. The earlier work's resolution: a first interview may run on the mechanism shown with values marked unset; no offer goes out before the rows an offer needs are set: the percentage, the revenue classes, the two lead seats' salaries and weights, and the payment rhythm. Everything else can be settled as a mechanism first. Which seats are salaried also decides who gets the final-round dinner (WP p. 18).

## 13. Benefits

"Planned from the start rather than deferred"; the payroll platform offers "cost-effective benefits for frontline employees and a fast path to full benefits as the business supports them" (WP p. 20). The platform's features are not confirmed here (3.3.32). The earlier work: which benefits exist at hire is a fact on the candidate sheet; "fast path" is a written conditional gate, never a promised date, so no surface states a benefit date the gate has not reached. The gate's conditions are Dominic's and appear here only as a row name.

## 14. Where Sŏn may diverge from the book

Almost none of her compensation machinery transfers: salary bands, equity targets, an equity refresh, a bonus program, a job ladder, market-data pricing by level, a compensation conversation on a schedule tied to the review. What transfers is the underlying demand that pay be explainable, that the system's evolution be said out loud, and that the moment pay changes be marked in person. The white paper requires the form (built into price, shared with the kitchen, day-weighted, daily) and leaves every mechanism open. The earlier work built one coherent mechanism; each of its parts appears above as an option with its alternative. The largest divergence is the one shared with 5.4: pay is kept out of the review. If the founders keep it that way, the trust the book builds through the review-to-pay link has to be built by legibility instead, and sections 6 and 7 are where that legibility is won or lost.

## 15. How others have handled it

General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Restaurants that replaced tipping with a service charge or higher menu prices (several well-known operators in New York, San Francisco, and elsewhere tried it in the last decade) mostly reported that the customer reaction to a visible charge was the hard part and that kitchen pay rose; several reverted. The white paper's customer research (WP p. 20) matches the first finding and is why the pool is built into price rather than shown.
- Tip pools in restaurants that keep tipping are commonly weighted by role and hours, and the weights are a frequent source of dispute; the earlier work's "days, not hours" and "published weight per seat" are answers to that dispute, not a copy of it.
- Profit-sharing and gain-sharing plans in other industries usually pay on a slower cadence than wages (quarterly or annually), which is the pattern the white paper's "calculated daily" is written against.
- Pay transparency (publishing every role's pay internally) is practiced by a small number of technology companies and cooperatives; the common report is fewer disputes and more questions about the rule itself, which is what section 7's destinations are for.
- The book's own advice on comparisons (redirect, never disclose another's pay) is standard HR practice everywhere (pp. 417 to 418).
