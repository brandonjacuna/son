# 5.5 Compensation: working session

## 1. Orientation

This chunk builds the mechanism around a pay form the white paper has already settled: compensation built into the menu price, never a service charge, distributed to the whole team including the kitchen on a point system weighted by days worked, calculated daily, paid on a reasonable rhythm, with the percentage itself still open (WP pp. 20, 22). The book (pp. 413 to 418) asks for a philosophy, a decision about what is fixed and what is variable, levels to price against, market data on a refresh schedule, and managers who can explain all of it.

Two rules hold for the whole session. No figure is spoken into the record: no percentage, no wage, no salary, no comp value. Where a value is needed, it goes on the parameters register as a row Dominic sets against the financial model, with counsel where wage law applies. And nothing about any founder's own pay is recorded anywhere in this repo.

Upstream, a lot is fixed. The compensation commitments are in the principles and Dominic has reviewed them (2.1.5, 2.1.6). The transparency line (2.2.6) left the pool's visibility to this chunk. 3.1.28 and 3.2.17 decided what the leads' candidate sheet says before this chunk is done and the order against their interviews; 3.2.19 who holds the lead seats' pay conversation. 3.3.18 recorded three constraints on module pay. 4.1.8 decided what a lead enters at, 4.1.9 the salaried test, 4.1.10 the fluency definitions, 4.1.11 who records an unlock, 4.1.19 the designation rule, 4.1.28 the cleaning crew's place. 4.8.3 and 4.8.5 decided how translation and interpreting are done. 5.4.5 decided whether the review feeds pay; this chunk builds against that answer.

Who else must agree: Dominic on nearly everything, as owner of the financial model and the payroll platform; both founders on every policy; the chef partner on the kitchen's weights and salaried seats (5.5.31), once seated; counsel on the legal character of the whole structure (5.5.30), before any offer. Several decisions will be recorded as Brandon's position, pending Dominic's agreement.

Why it sits here: the book puts compensation last in the review sequence because pay follows performance. At Sŏn the order is the same for a different reason: whether pay follows the review at all was decided in 5.4.5, and this chunk builds whichever answer was given.

## 3. Briefs

### 5.5.2 Decide the compensation philosophy in plain words

**The question:** in a few sentences a line cook could repeat, what does Sŏn pay for, and what does it never pay for?

**Why it matters now:** a strong candidate asks this at the first interview (cons. 1), and the earlier work found Sŏn had a structure but no philosophy anyone could say. Every mechanism below has to be consistent with the sentence. It also has to match 5.4.5: if the review feeds pay, "pay for performance" is honest; if not, the sentence has to say what performance means without a rating.

**Openers:**
- A cook asks you at the pass: "why does she make more than me?" Answer in one breath, with no number.
- Sideways: the book's philosophy is "pay for performance." Say it about Sŏn. Does it feel true, or does it feel like a phrase from a different building?
- The white paper says the kitchen shares in "the nights they help fill." What is the word for what that rewards? It is not performance and it is not seniority.
- Would you rather a person could compute their own pay, or that a lead could recognize a great month with a number? Say why.

**Narrowing questions:**
- What does pay reward: assessed competency, demonstrated versatility, nights worked, something else?
- What does it never reward: a rating, a lead's opinion, tenure, a customer's review, negotiation?
- Is "pay for performance" a phrase Sŏn uses? If yes, what does it mean here?
- Is the sentence consistent with 5.4.5?

**What the book says:** a company needs a core philosophy; hers is market-competitive pay with higher reward for higher performance (pp. 413 to 414).

**White-paper default:** labor as an asset (WP p. 16); costs not shifted to employees (WP p. 19); the kitchen brought into the upside (WP p. 20); fluency recognized in pay (WP p. 11); teaching paid (WP p. 19). Never "pay for performance."

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most restaurants have no stated philosophy; the ones that moved away from tipping usually published a short one because customers and staff both asked (cons. 15).

**Options:** (a) legible and defensible, disconnected from judgment: pay for assessed competency, current versatility, and days worked, the same for everyone in a seat. Commits Sŏn to no differential reward for doing the same seat better. (b) the same, plus a stated element of performance reward routed through the review (requires 5.4.5 to allow it). Commits Sŏn to a rating and to calibration. (c) the book's sentence as written. Commits Sŏn to bands, market pricing by level, and a manager's discretion. Depth: cons. 1.

**Watch for:** a philosophy that sounds like a value statement ("we invest in our people") rather than a rule a person can test against their own pay page; a sentence that quietly promises a raise for effort.

**A finished answer:** the sentences, said aloud, with "rewards" and "never rewards" both named, the phrase question answered, and 5.4.5 checked.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.5.3 Decide the two layers: base pay and the shared pool, and how they relate

**The question:** what does a person's pay consist of, named?

**Why it matters now:** the white paper describes only the pool and assumes a wage everywhere else without naming it (cons. 2). An offer, a pay page, and a mechanics page all need both named, and an offer must be clear about which part is guaranteed.

**Openers:**
- Read a person their offer out loud. Which words are the wage and which are the share? Could they tell?
- Sideways: at rooms you worked, what did people call the part of their pay that varied? What did they call it when it was short?
- Is there a third thing? A referral payment (3.1.13), anything at year end, anything at all?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Both layers named, in what words?
- Base pay is one rule per seat, never conditioned on the pool in an offer: yes?
- Any third element?

**What the book says:** decide what is fixed and what is variable (pp. 413 to 414).

**White-paper default:** the pool described (WP p. 20); base pay assumed and unnamed (WP p. 18).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Wage plus tips is the industry's two layers; the second is rarely explained beyond the pool's split.

**Options:** cons. 2 (a), (b), (c).

**Watch for:** an offer that reads as a total with the pool assumed at a full house.

**A finished answer:** two names, the one-rule-per-seat statement, the third-element answer.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; pending Dominic.

### 5.5.4 Decide what the pool is a share of

**The question:** which revenue counts, and does a comped plate count?

**Why it matters now:** the percentage is Dominic's; the classes are a mechanism question the house has to be able to explain. The comped-item rule decides whether the generosity range (WP p. 26) taxes the team's own pool.

**Openers:**
- A server comps a dessert for a table that waited too long. That night's pool: smaller, or the same? Say which you want and why.
- Sideways: the white paper calls comps "a line item to be fully used." If the pool shrinks when they are used, what does the floor learn?
- When the early morning opens, does a coffee count toward the same pool as a tasting menu? Do you know yet, or does that wait?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The classes that count, by name.
- The comped-item rule: full menu price, or not.
- Which classes wait for a later period, and the date they are decided.
- Recorded that the percentage is Dominic's, against the financial model, with counsel.

**What the book says:** be able to explain how pay is set and when its basis changes (p. 414).

**White-paper default:** "a defined percentage that is still being finalized" (WP p. 20); "the remaining open piece is the final percentage" (WP p. 22).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Revenue-share and tip pools usually exclude comps and voids by default because the point-of-sale does; counting comps at menu price is a deliberate configuration.

**Options:** (a) the named classes with the comped-item rule. (b) the named classes without it. (c) defer the whole list to Dominic and record only the rule. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** a figure slipping into the conversation.

**A finished answer:** the class list by name, the comp rule, the deferred classes dated, the percentage's ownership recorded.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; pending Dominic.

### 5.5.5 Decide one pool per day or one per service period

**The question:** when the early morning and late night open, do they fill the same pool as dinner?

**Why it matters now:** it decides whether periods compete for people, whether a second-period marker means anything, and whether the one-restaurant thesis (WP p. 33) reaches pay.

**Openers:**
- A cook works the early morning and dinner on the same day. One day, two periods. What are they paid for the second one?
- Sideways: the white paper's whole argument is that a bolted-on daypart becomes a worse version of dinner. If the morning team is paid only from morning revenue, which version of dinner are they?
- Dinner runs alone for a quarter. Does this decision need to be made before the morning opens, or now?

**Narrowing questions:**
- One pool per calendar day, or one per period?
- If one: the rule for a day with one period open.
- If per period: how a two-period person is paid.

**What the book says:** the system must be explainable (p. 414).

**White-paper default:** "calculated daily" (WP p. 20); "one restaurant ... met at different times of day" (WP p. 33).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Tip pools are almost always per shift or per period; a whole-day pool across periods is unusual.

**Options:** cons. 5.

**Watch for:** deciding this for dinner alone and discovering it at the morning's opening.

**A finished answer:** the choice, its reason, the edge rule.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; pending Dominic.

### 5.5.6 Decide who is in the pool

**The question:** who, on a given day, holds points?

**Why it matters now:** "the whole team" needs a seat-by-seat answer. Training, the paid practical, contracted labor, and future seat classes each need placing before the first offer.

**Openers:**

- Sideways: the overnight cleaning crew is "at the same cost" (WP p. 26). Are they team? Does the pool say so?

**Narrowing questions:**
- By seat class: floor, kitchen, beverage, host, steward, events (4.1.27), the crew (4.1.28).
- Training from the first paid hour: in?
- The paid practical: in or out (3.1.34)?
- Founders and chef partner: to 5.5.7.

**What the book says:** the framework needs to know who is priced (p. 414).

**White-paper default:** "distributed to the whole team, the culinary team included" (WP p. 20).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Tip pools exclude managers by law in most cases and exclude back of house in many; the white paper's kitchen inclusion is the departure and is why counsel is needed (5.5.30).

**Options:** as drafted in cons. 5, or with a named exception.

**Watch for:** a seat class left out because it did not exist yet.

**A finished answer:** the inclusion rule by seat class with the edge cases placed.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.5.7 Decide whether the founders and the chef partner hold points in the pool

**The question:** when a partner works a service, do they draw from the pool the team fills?

**Why it matters now:** it is gated on counsel (whether excluding partners changes the pool's legal character or the honesty of "the whole team") and on the operating agreement (2.1.1). The pay system has to be configured either way before the first close. Nothing about any founder's own pay is recorded here.

**Openers:**

- Sideways: what does the floor think when a founder works a Saturday and the day's total points go up?

**Narrowing questions:**

**What the book says:** a philosophy a company can explain (p. 414).

**White-paper default:** the chef partner "owns culinary execution during service" and is an equal founding partner (WP pp. 3, 10); the pool goes to the whole team (WP p. 20). Silent on partners drawing from it.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Owners and managers are generally kept out of tip pools by wage law; whether the same applies to a share built into price is one of the counsel questions.

**Options:** the three positions per partner, each with counsel's answer.

**Watch for:** any figure or any statement about a founder's own pay entering the record.

**A finished answer:** a position per partner, counsel's answer attached, the configuration stated, nothing about anyone's pay recorded.

### 5.5.8 Decide what sets a person's points, and what never does

**The question:** what moves a person's share, and who can touch it?

**Why it matters now:** this is the heart of the mechanism and the seam with 5.4.5. If any judgment can move points, a person cannot compute their own pay and the legibility argument fails. If nothing can, there is no way to recognize a great month with money, and the founders should choose that knowingly.

**Openers:**
- Your best server had the best month of anyone in the room. Under the earlier design, their share did not move. Say how you feel about that out loud.
- Sideways: at rooms you worked, who could move someone's pay by hand, and what happened around that person?
- The white paper says no one's advancement waits on a manager's judgment. Does that sentence stop at advancement? Say why it should or should not reach pay.
- If a record is wrong and a person's points are off, who fixes it, and is that "moving points by hand"?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The four sources (seat weight at horizon; active markers; days; the salaried rule): all four, fewer, more?
- The never-list: every item on it (cons. 6), or an exception named with its owner and trigger?
- No hand adjustment at all, or one named correction on the decision-rights register?
- If 5.4.5 let the review feed pay: the route, here.

**What the book says:** pay for performance; fixed and variable elements; levels as the pricing basis (pp. 413 to 414).

**White-paper default:** "a point system weighted by the days each person works" (WP p. 20); fluency in pay (WP p. 11); "no one's advancement waits on a manager's attention or judgment" (WP p. 11).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Almost every pay system has a discretionary element; systems with none (some cooperatives, some unionized settings) report fewer disputes and less ability to retain a standout with money.

**Options:** cons. 6 (a), (b), (c).

**Watch for:** "hours" creeping in for "days"; sales attribution arriving through the customer-data layer (WP p. 27) as a pay input no one decided on.

**A finished answer:** the sources with their records, the never-list, the hand-adjustment answer, the 5.4.5 route if any.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; pending Dominic.

### 5.5.9 Decide what counts as a day

**The question:** what earns a day's points?

**Why it matters now:** "weighted by days" is the white paper's one mechanical commitment about individuals. The definition decides whether a check-in, a training hour, and a two-period day count, and it decides what an unexpectedly closed day does to pay.

**Openers:**
- A person works a half-hour check-in on a day they are not otherwise scheduled. A day?
- Sideways: a storm closes the building at four. Who was going to work tonight, and what do they get? Do you know, or is that counsel's?
- Why days and not hours? Say the reason in one sentence.

**Narrowing questions:**
- The one-sentence definition.
- Paid blocks count as a worked shift: yes?
- Scheduled but not worked: no?
- The closed day: to counsel, with a register row.

**What the book says:** the mechanics must be explainable (p. 414).

**White-paper default:** "weighted by the days each person works" (WP p. 20); costs not shifted to employees (WP p. 19).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Pools weighted by hours are the norm; a day-weighted pool is a Sŏn choice with the white paper behind it.

**Options:** the earlier definition (cons. 6) or a minimum-length threshold for a day.

**Watch for:** a threshold that turns a short paid block into an unpaid one.

**A finished answer:** the sentence, the two rules, the counsel item.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; pending Dominic.

### 5.5.10 Decide how a salaried seat is paid: salary plus the pool by a day rule, or salary outside the pool

**The question:** does the Maitre d share in a full Saturday?

**Why it matters now:** 4.1.9 decided which seats are salaried by a structural test; this decides what that means in pay. The two options carry opposite appearance risks: a leader drawing from the pool their team fills, or a leader for whom a full night is worth nothing extra.

**Openers:**
- The room does its biggest night. Every server's share rises. The Maitre d, who held the room, gets the same salary. Say how that sits.
- Sideways: now the reverse. The Maitre d holds every floor review and also draws from the pool. What does a server think the first time they wonder about it?
- Does the fact that no one can move anyone's points by hand answer the second worry, or only on paper?

**Narrowing questions:**
- In the pool by a day rule (every open day in the period), or outside?
- If in: does a closed day count? Which days?
- If out: any share in a full night at all?
- Kitchen's salaried seats: to 5.5.31. Salaries: register rows.

**What the book says:** salary is the fixed element; variable elements are decided separately (pp. 413 to 414).

**White-paper default:** "leadership and salaried roles" named together, never listed (WP p. 18).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Salaried managers are excluded from tip pools by law in most settings; profit-sharing plans in other industries usually include managers.

**Options:** cons. 3.

**Watch for:** a day rule that makes a salaried seat's share depend on which days the seat holder chose to be present.

**A finished answer:** the choice with its reason, the day rule or the full-night answer, the kitchen deferred.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; pending Dominic.

### 5.5.16 Decide how teaching is paid: authorship, completion, and mentoring

**The question:** what does the white paper's "get paid for teaching everyone else" mean in three cases?

**Why it matters now:** 3.3.18 recorded three constraints this chunk must honor. The white paper's "the more modules completed, the more earned" reads as a per-completion payment; the earlier work argued the unlock already carries it. This is the one place the earlier work read the white paper against its plain words, and the founders should decide it knowingly.

**Openers:**
- A cook writes a module on the wood grill. It is approved. Twelve people complete it. What is the cook paid, and when?
- Sideways: "the more modules completed, the more earned." Say it as a per-module payment. Now say it as "complete the module, pass the assessment, your weight moves." Which one did the white paper mean, and which one do you want?
- A mentor's mentee fails their readiness read. Does the mentor's pay notice?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Authorship: flat per approved module and revision, as base pay, nothing per completion by others?
- Completion: a payment per module, or the unlock carries it?
- Mentoring: shift pay plus a teaching marker while a plan is open, never tied to outcome, never stacked?
- The three constraints honored; amounts on the register.

**What the book says:** p. 414.

**White-paper default:** "Any qualified team member can create training modules and get paid for teaching everyone else. The more modules completed, the more earned" (WP p. 19).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Trainer premiums (a fixed add-on while training someone) are common in restaurants; paying for authored content is unusual outside technology companies' internal learning programs.

**Options:** cons. 8, with the per-completion alternative stated.

**Watch for:** an authorship payment that ranks authors by reach; a completion payment that rewards finishing over readiness.

**A finished answer:** three forms with triggers and layers, the completion sentence answered with reasoning, the constraints checked.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; pending Dominic.

### 5.5.18 Decide the payment rhythm
