# 3.1 Recruiting: working-session guide

## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** How Sŏn finds people: the founders' place in every hire, who owns a hire, what every interview reads, how seats are classed and screened, how candidates are answered and kept, referrals, the talent brand and the documented build, the posting and the candidate sheet, the two operating leads and the chef partner, and what internal succession will mean once there is anyone to succeed. The next chunk, 3.2, designs the interviews themselves; 3.3 takes over at the offer.

**Upstream.** 2.1 has settled, or is settling, the chef seat's standing (2.1.10), the chef partner's seat description (2.1.11), and the honest description of working at Sŏn for candidates (2.1.19). 2.2 holds the decision-rights register (2.2.4). 4.1 holds the leadership lines (4.1.6), the salaried test (4.1.9), and the seat inventory (4.1.15). 5.5 has not run, and the candidate sheet depends on it; that conflict is on the agenda, not hidden.

**Who else must agree.** Dominic on anything about the leads, the chef partner search, and the founders' own stage. The chef partner, once seated, on every kitchen seat's class and pipeline; those are left open with a marker. Counsel closes the practical's employment form.

**Why it sits here.** The book puts recruiting first in its talent chapter because everything downstream inherits its quality. For Sŏn it is also first in time: the two leads are the first hires and their first interviews may come before the compensation design, the seat inventory's final form, or the room exist.

**How to run it.** Twenty-seven decisions is more than one sitting. The agenda below has a natural break after conversation 4. Take the first four in one sitting and the rest in a second; the first four are enough to open the lead searches.

## 3. Briefs

### 3.1.2 List the jobs each founder has done in the last three weeks, and sort them

**The question:** what have you actually been doing, and which of it only you can do?

**Why it matters now:** the book's point is that founders cannot see how many jobs they hold (p. 182). The sorted list is the honest input to which leadership lines open first (3.1.18) and what each lead inherits on day one.

**Openers:**
- Walk back through the last three weeks, day by day. Name the jobs, not the tasks: "vendor negotiation," "content edit," "site walk," "the hiring conversation you had at the bar."
- Sideways: if Dominic did the same list, which items would appear on both? Those are the ones nobody owns.
- Which job on the list would you be relieved to hand over, and which would you quietly keep even if someone else held the title?

**What to produce:** each founder's own list in three columns. Do not merge them; set them side by side. This is an action, not a decision; nothing here is recorded as a position.

**Capture for the kit:** the prompts that got a real list rather than a job description go into `kits/leadership-search/intake.md`.

**Needs agreement from:** each founder for their own list.

### 3.1.3 Decide the founders' stage in every hire before opening, and when it changes

**The question:** in every hire made before opening, what does a founder do, can it overturn the hiring manager, and what event ends it?

**Why it matters now:** the leads are the first hires and the founders are their hiring managers regardless. The question bites on the first frontline cohort: whether every line cook and server meets a founder, and what that meeting decides. It is hours on both founders' calendars for the whole cohort, and until 3.2.40 answers what replaces it, it is the only outside-domain check on an entry-level packet. It shapes the decision-rights entries (3.1.32), the mutual commitments (3.1.31), and the after-opening question of what replaces it (3.2.40). This brief also carries the question 3.2 once asked separately, whether a founder's "no" must name a read and what event ends the stage; the two were merged in the cross-chunk pass.

**Openers:**
- A lead you hired brings you a server candidate they want. You meet her for twenty minutes and something is off you cannot name. What happens next, and who decides?
- It is the cohort hiring month and forty candidates are past the first interview. How many of them do you personally want to meet, and what would you be looking for in fifteen minutes that the domain lead missed in sixty?
- Sideways: the book's Stripe story is founders meeting every finalist until the company was hundreds of people (p. 171). Picture yourself in week three of hiring the dinner cohort. How many of those meetings are you actually going to hold well?
- Sideways: the white paper's proudest line about the founders is that the company is built to run without them (WP p. 6). What is the first hire you want the leads to make without you in the room, and why that one?
- If your stage cannot overturn the lead, what is it for? If it can, what is the lead for?
- The book says a founder's job is to model the hard "no" on a candidate who is just fine (p. 171). Can you say no to a candidate without naming a read? Should you be allowed to?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Is the founder's read a finalist interview, a narrow read of two things (conduct, and whether the building can support this person), or nothing?
- Does it apply to every seat class or only to leadership and salaried seats?
- Must a founder's "no" name a read, like everyone else's? If not, where is the reasoning written so it can be taught (p. 171)?
- What ends it for frontline seats: a date, a headcount, a number of consecutive clean calibration reads, a quarter of dinner, a mechanism reset (2.3.34), or never? Does it ever end for leadership seats?

**What the book says:** below roughly 100 employees per founder, founders interview every finalist; they model the high bar and the hard no; the no should be explained and, over time, built into process (p. 171).

**White-paper default:** interviews assess competencies, cultural alignment, growth potential, and whether the building can support the person (WP p. 18). No default on the founders' stage.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Stripe's founders met every finalist until senior leaders could proxy the values (p. 171). Amazon's "bar raiser" is a trained interviewer from outside the hiring team with a defined vote, which is the form the book's "build the no into process" usually takes.

**Options:** (a) the book's finalist interview with a founder no, until a headcount. Commits both founders to a slot in every hire and puts the last word above the lead. (b) A narrow stage with two scored reads (the conduct standard and the building-side question) that cannot overturn the lead on competency, with a named end condition. Commits Sŏn to defining those reads and to living with hires a founder would not have made. (c) No founder stage below leadership, from the first cohort. Commits Sŏn to trusting leads before they have hired here. Depth: cons. 1.

**Watch for:** a "narrow" stage that is a veto in practice; a founder stage that becomes the real decision, with the lead's packet as advice; an end date that is really "when I feel ready"; a stage nobody has time to hold in a hiring week.

**A finished answer:** the stage named, its power over the lead stated, whether a founder's no must name a read, the seat classes it applies to, and the event that ends it for frontline and for leadership seats, in two sentences.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic, since it is his hours too.

### 3.1.4 Decide who is the hiring manager for each seat, before and after the leads exist

**The question:** who owns the hire decision for each seat, who owns it for the two leads themselves, and who owns frontline hires if any open before their lead is seated?

**Why it matters now:** the book's line is that a recruiter never substitutes for the hiring manager, who is accountable for the new hire's work (p. 175). The order of the first hires makes this concrete: if a Head of Beverage is sought before the Maitre d is seated, someone is that hire's manager.

**Openers:**
- The Maitre d owns team development (WP p. 10). Does that make them the hiring manager for a Chef on prep? Say why or why not.
- Sideways: at the last opening you worked, who actually decided a hire, and who was blamed when it went wrong? Were they the same person?
- For the two leads: is it both founders jointly, one founder each by domain, or one founder with the other's read?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Is the rule "the domain lead is the hiring manager for their domain's seats," and if so, what happens to seats whose lead is not yet seated?
- Where does the Chef Partner sit: hiring manager for every kitchen seat, or for the Station Leads only?
- Who is the hiring manager for a seat that works across the floor and the kitchen, such as the Steward?

**What the book says:** a single manager decision at the base of the pyramid (p. 170); the hiring manager stays accountable (p. 175).

**White-paper default:** the Operations Lead and the Maitre d are peers over different systems; the Maitre d holds team development and training; the Chef Partner owns culinary execution during service (WP p. 10). Implied, not stated: the domain's lead hires for it.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most restaurant groups make the department head the hiring manager and the general manager the approver; Sŏn has no general manager to approve, which is the point of this decision.

**Options:** (a) domain lead per domain, founders for the leads, no frontline seat opens before its lead. Commits Sŏn to the hiring order in 3.1.18. (b) The Maitre d for all floor seats, the Chef Partner for the kitchen, the Operations Lead for none. Commits the Maitre d to reading inward-facing work. (c) Both founders for the first cohort with a handover date. Commits the leads to inheriting a team they did not choose. Depth: cons. 1.

**Watch for:** "we decide together" with no name on it; a lead who is hiring manager on paper and overruled in practice.

**A finished answer:** a table by seat class with a name or seat in every row, including the two leads and the "lead not yet seated" case.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic; the chef partner for kitchen rows, once seated.

### 3.1.5 Decide what every hire is read on, and what is never read

**The question:** what are the few things every interview at Sŏn scores, defined so two interviewers score them the same, and what is off the table?

**Why it matters now:** this list is the parent of the profile (3.1.6), the conduct statement (3.1.7), every posting (3.1.26), every rubric 3.2 builds, and the founders' own training (3.2.12). "Culture fit" here becomes bias downstream.

**Openers:**
- Think of the best person you ever worked a floor with and the best you ever worked a line with. Name what they had in common in one word each. Now name what only one of them had.
- The white paper says interviews assess "cultural alignment" (WP p. 18). Say what that would look like in an answer to a question. If you cannot, it is not a read yet.
- Sideways: the earlier work argued that service pressure rewards the assertive and expressive, and that an interviewer pool with that skew screens out quiet, precise people without noticing. Who on your future interviewer pool is the quiet, precise one?
- What did you teach yourself, without being asked, that made you good at this? The book's curiosity test (p. 189) is asking for that story from candidates.

**Narrowing questions:**
- Are the reads the earlier four (competency for the seat's type of work, self-directed growth, conduct and comfort with real autonomy, what the building must provide), the white paper's three plus one, or the book's three circles?
- Is "cultural alignment" defined against the principles in 2.1.5, or replaced by a conduct read?
- What is on the never-read list: personality type, years of experience as a threshold, anything about who the candidate is as a person, format choice (3.1.10)?
- Does the "what the building must provide" read ever produce a no, and if so, who says it?

**What the book says:** the three circles, good at the work, high impact, loves the work; mine what successful people share for questions (pp. 178 to 179); test for curiosity and learning aptitude over credentials (p. 189); watch for what transmits bias (p. 174).

**White-paper default:** competencies, cultural alignment, growth potential, and whether the building is equipped to create the environment the person needs; not gut feeling (WP p. 18).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Structured interviews scoring a small fixed set of attributes predict performance better than unstructured conversation across decades of research; Google's published practice of structured interviews and scoring rubrics is the best-known corporate example. Union Square Hospitality Group's stated weighting of emotional skills over technical ones in hiring is a well-known restaurant example of naming the reads rather than hiring on feel.

**Options:** (a) the four reads, with alignment rendered as a conduct read. Commits Sŏn to defining conduct concretely and to never scoring "fit." (b) The white paper's four as written. Commits interviewers to agreeing what alignment looks like. (c) The book's three circles. Commits Sŏn to scoring "impact" before anyone has worked here. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** a read that is a synonym for "I liked them"; more than five reads; a never-read list that quietly leaves a door open for personality typing.

**A finished answer:** each read named and defined in one sentence a new interviewer could score against, the never-read list written, and Brandon able to say which read would have caught the worst hire he has seen made.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic; the chef partner may add a kitchen-specific competency later, never a new read.

### 3.1.6 Write the profile of who does well at Sŏn, version one

**The question:** with no employees yet, what is the best hypothesis of who thrives here, strand by strand?

**Why it matters now:** the book says study who fits and mine what they share (p. 178); Sŏn has to start from a guess and revise it at 3.1.39. Writing the guess down is what makes it revisable.

**Openers:**
- Describe the person who would be excellent on the inward-facing strand and mediocre on the outward one. Now the reverse. Both get hired here (WP p. 11).
- Who have you seen love this work at hour eleven of a double? What did loving it look like?

**What to produce:** one page per strand, labelled hypothesis. Nothing about any named person.

**Needs agreement from:** none to write; 3.1.39 revises it.

### 3.1.7 Decide the hiring conduct statement: what a hire is never selected on, and what pipeline data may change

**The question:** what does Sŏn say, in writing, about what it will not select on, what it records about a candidate, and what a pattern in the pipeline is allowed to change?

**Why it matters now:** it is needed before the first seat is posted publicly; it shapes the applicant system's fields (3.1.33), the report (3.1.37), and what the careers page says (3.1.25). 4.7 holds the larger inclusion question; this is the hiring conduct only.

**Openers:**
- Six months in, the report shows one source supplies most of the floor's applicants and the phone screen declines one language group at twice the rate. What do you do, and what do you refuse to do?
- Sideways: the book says our friends tend to be just like us (p. 172). The build's audience chose Sŏn's story. What does a team hired mostly from that audience look like?
- Is there anything about a candidate, beyond the languages they work in, that Sŏn should write down?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Does the statement say Sŏn sets no target for its team's makeup, so no one is measured against one? Or is that left to 4.7?
- What may a pattern change: outreach and source mix only, or the process itself? Never the bar, never a scorecard?
- Who reviews the report and at what interval?
- Does counsel need to confirm that keeping no identity record conflicts with nothing an employer must keep?

**What the book says:** networks and referrals narrow a team and undermine diversity efforts; watch for it (pp. 171 to 172, 180 to 181).

**White-paper default:** language choice protects dignity (WP p. 17). No target for the team's makeup is stated anywhere.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Many employers separate the monitoring of applicant flow from the decision on any individual; the report is read for the process, never for the candidate.

**Options:** (a) the drafted statement: selection on the reads only, every stage in the candidate's language, no identity record beyond languages, patterns reviewed as information and answered with wider outreach, no target. Commits Sŏn to publishing what it will not do. (b) The statement plus an aspiration on composition, taken up in 4.7. Commits Sŏn to a position the white paper does not take. (c) No statement. Commits Sŏn to candidates inferring its conduct. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** a statement that reads as legal boilerplate; a monitoring habit with no owner; "we do not see identity" used to avoid looking at the report.

**A finished answer:** the statement in under a page, the report's owner and interval named, and the 4.7 question parked explicitly.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic.

### 3.1.8 Decide how seats are classed for hiring

**The question:** which few classes of seat determine a candidate's path, and what stages does each path contain?

**Why it matters now:** the class fixes the phone screen, the practical, and the final-round meal per seat; 3.2 designs each stage's content and cannot start until the classes exist. The applicant system holds one path per class.

**Openers:**
- A Steward, a Lead Host, a Chef on the line, a Head of Beverage. Walk each from application to offer out loud. Where do the paths split?
- Sideways: the book's pyramid scales rigor with altitude (p. 170). Say why a phone screen for a porter is not a lower bar than a dinner for a lead, in your own words. If you cannot, the pyramid is still in the design.
- Which seat sits in two classes at once?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Are the classes high-volume, physical-skill, and leadership or salaried, with the chef partner handled separately in 3.1.19?
- Which stages does each carry, in order? Does every class have at least two independent scorecards before a decision?
- Do kitchen seats get a class now, marked "pipeline waits for the chef partner and a menu," or wait entirely?
- Is the class stored on the seat inventory row (4.1.15)?

**What the book says:** volume hiring on a set process with role-holders as interviewers and a single manager decision; a standing committee in the middle; a bespoke top (p. 170); screening steps (p. 181).

**White-paper default:** a short phone screen for high-volume roles such as line cook and porter (WP p. 17); a paid practical as the second interview for physical-skill roles; a dinner in the room as the final round for leadership and salaried roles (WP p. 18).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Restaurant groups commonly run a phone screen, an interview, and a trail for hourly seats, and add a meal or a tasting for management and chef seats; the difference at Sŏn is that the trail is paid and scored.

**Options:** (a) three classes plus the chef partner, a seat may hold more than one, stages listed per class, no class decides on one scorecard. Commits Sŏn to the practical's form (3.1.34) before any physical-skill seat is hired. (b) Two classes, hourly and salaried. Commits Sŏn to a practical for every hourly seat including the phone-screen ones, or none. (c) A path per seat. Commits Sŏn to more design than it can maintain. Depth: cons. 1, 7.

**Watch for:** a class that is really a rank; the salaried test from 4.1.9 being redone here; the one-scorecard rule dropped for speed.

**A finished answer:** the class list, the stage list per class, every inventory seat assigned, kitchen seats marked as waiting, and the single-scorecard rule written.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic; the chef partner for kitchen seats, once seated.

### 3.1.9 Decide what happens between an application and the first interview, per class

**The question:** who looks at an application, against what, is there any assessment before the first interview, and who runs the phone screen?

**Why it matters now:** WP p. 17 promises no unanswered application and a few-minute phone screen for high-volume roles. Someone has to be on the other end, and the response windows (3.1.11) start here.

**Openers:**
- Forty applications arrive for a Chef on prep seat the week the chef partner is plating the menu. Who opens them, on what day, against which lines of the posting?
- Sideways: the book notes that a written project before the interview costs pipeline while you are still building one (p. 181). Is there any seat where Sŏn wants that cost?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Is the review against the posting's competencies only, with no years-of-experience line?
- Who runs the phone screen: the hiring manager, a trained peer in the seat, or a founder before the team exists? How long is it, and what does it read?
- Is the phone screen the first interview, or the step before it?

**What the book says:** résumé review against outlined qualifications, an optional assessment, a phone screen, then onsite (p. 181).

**White-paper default:** the phone screen identifies fit in a few minutes for high-volume roles; no manual re-entry; no unanswered application (WP p. 17).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. A short scripted phone screen run by someone who does the job is the common form; the book's own worked example runs a recruiter screen and a team screen (pp. 229 to 245).

**Options:** (a) review by the hiring manager against the posting, no pre-interview assessment for any class, a scripted ten-minute phone screen by a trained peer or the hiring manager. Commits Sŏn to training peers early. (b) Same, with a short written prompt for leadership seats only. Commits Sŏn to reading them. (c) Founders review everything until the leads are seated. Commits founders to the volume. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** a review step nobody owns on a Friday; a phone screen that becomes a full interview.

**A finished answer:** for each class, the reviewer, the criteria, the assessment answer, the phone screen's owner and length.

**Needs agreement from:** the hiring managers named in 3.1.4.

### 3.1.10 Decide the format-choice rule

**The question:** is a candidate's choice of phone, video, or in person ever scored, noted, or used in a decision?

**Why it matters now:** the white paper says both that format "does double duty as a qualification filter" and that it protects the candidate with a transportation constraint (WP p. 17). The applicant system will record whichever rule Sŏn picks, and interviewers will act on it.

**Openers:**
- Two candidates for the same seat. One chooses in person and arrives in a pressed shirt. One chooses video because the bus from her side of town takes ninety minutes each way. Which one is more serious?
- Sideways: have you ever chosen the phone for something you cared about, because that was what your day allowed?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Never scored, never noted, never a reason to decline: is that the rule?
- Or is format one input at the offer stage only, with the interviewer required to write why?
- Is in-person required for the final stage of any class, and if so, who pays for travel?

**What the book says:** watch for anything that transmits bias (p. 174).

**White-paper default:** stated outright both ways (WP p. 17); the two sentences cannot both be rules.

**How others have handled it:** Nothing useful to add; this is a Sŏn tension.

**Options:** (a) the candidate's own signal, never used. Commits Sŏn to reading seriousness in the room. (b) Noted and usable at the offer stage with a written reason. Commits Sŏn to defending it. (c) In-person for the final stage only. Commits Sŏn to travel costs. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** "we don't score it, but we notice"; a system field that records format next to the score.

**A finished answer:** one sentence, and the applicant system configured to match.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic.

### 3.1.11 Set the candidate response commitments

**The question:** how fast is an application acknowledged, a stage followed by the next, and a decision delivered, who owns each window, and what happens when one is missed?

**Why it matters now:** "speed is respect" and "no one is ghosted" (WP p. 18) are promises to people who need work. Without windows and an owner they are sentiments. 3.1.33 turns the windows into timers.

**Openers:**
- You applied somewhere once and heard nothing. How long before you stopped checking? That is the outer edge of the window.
- Sideways: the earlier work called an unanswered application a system failure, not a lapse. Whose pager goes off?
- The lead is mid-service and a decision is due today. Is that a reason to miss the window, or the reason the window exists?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Windows in days for: acknowledgement, stage to stage, decision after the last stage, the no.
- Published on the what-to-expect page, or internal?
- Who is alerted on a miss, and does a miss appear on the leads' review (2.3.19)?
- Is the no always a person, or may it be a system message for early stages?

**What the book says:** recruiting commits to prompt follow-up and a transparent process (pp. 175 to 178).

**White-paper default:** quick decisions when skills and alignment are there; an arbitrary multi-week wait is a signal; every candidate gets a clear answer including the no (WP p. 18).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies that publish response windows to candidates tend to keep them; the ones that keep them internal tend not to. The book's own commitments list includes prompt follow-up as an employee's obligation, not only recruiting's (p. 177).

**Options:** (a) published windows with an owner and an alert. Commits Sŏn to being seen missing them. (b) Internal windows. Commits Sŏn to less than "exactly what to expect." Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** windows set to what is comfortable rather than what respect requires; a no that goes out by system at the final stage.

**A finished answer:** the windows in days, published or not, the owner, the miss rule.

**Needs agreement from:** the hiring managers; Dominic on publication.

### 3.1.12 Decide how a declined candidate enters the pool and how a pool candidate is re-read

**The question:** which no's are a "not now," what is the candidate told, how long does the record live, and does a pool candidate for a critical seat repeat the scored stages?

**Why it matters now:** WP p. 18 says a candidate treated well at the no returns to the pool next year. A pool that exists only as a sentiment is a list nobody opens. The applicant system needs the state (3.1.33), and the succession rule (3.1.38) reads the internal ready-now pool before any outside search; this is the external one.

**Openers:**
- A strong server you declined because the seat filled first applies again in eight months. What does she skip, and what does she repeat?
- Sideways: the earlier work noted a follower-candidate already knows the operation well; a careless no loses a customer too. What does a good no sound like to someone who has watched the build for a year?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Every decline after the first interview enters the pool unless conduct ruled them out or they decline it; or only declines named "not now"?
- Record life: a year, as WP p. 18 implies?
- A pool candidate for a critical seat repeats the practical: yes or no?
- Who tells the candidate they are in the pool, and in what words?

**What the book says:** the funnel treats a decline as a loss (p. 169); treat every candidate well (pp. 180 to 181).

**White-paper default:** the pool, next year (WP p. 18).

**How others have handled it:** Nothing useful to add beyond the book.

**Options:** (a) broad pool, scored stages repeated for critical seats. Commits Sŏn to records and to telling candidates. (b) "Not now" only. Commits interviewers to naming it. (c) No formal pool. Commits Sŏn to less than promised. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** a pool that becomes a way to soften a real no; readiness assumed from a year-old scorecard.

**A finished answer:** the entry rule, the record life, the re-read rule, and the sentence the candidate hears.

**Needs agreement from:** the hiring managers.

### 3.1.13 Decide the referral rule: whether a referral is paid, and how a referrer is kept out of the read

**The question:** does Sŏn pay for a referral, and how does a referral run so the referrer neither reads nor is blamed for it?

**Why it matters now:** referrals start with the first posting, and the first team's friends are the second team. The book values them and warns about them in the same breath.

**Openers:**
- Your best line cook says "my cousin is great." What happens in the next ten days, and what does your cook get, hear, and never touch?
- Sideways: the earlier work argued a bounty turns insider knowledge into a paid tip. Do you agree, or is that a story people tell to avoid paying?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Cash, recognition through the platform (WP p. 19), or nothing beyond thanks?
- If cash, after what point, and does 5.5 place it?
- Tagged source with referrer named; referrer takes no part in any stage; referrer told the outcome when the candidate is: agreed?

**What the book says:** strong once success is defined, not sufficient, can undermine diversity, treat them well regardless; the author's own referral still got the full screen (pp. 180 to 181).

**White-paper default:** peer recognition runs through a platform (WP p. 19). Silent on pay.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Referral bonuses after a retention period are common in technology and larger restaurant groups; smaller rooms often rely on recognition alone.

**Options:** (a) no cash; recognition; the mechanics. Commits Sŏn to watching whether referrals arrive. (b) Cash after a retention point. Commits Sŏn to a cost and to the diversity warning with an incentive on it. (c) Decide after the first cohort. Commits Sŏn to telling the first referrers so. Depth: cons. 6.

**Why it matters now:** the promise is on WP p. 19 and candidates are told an advancement path. On day one every hire is external. Without a rule for when the promise starts counting it is never tested.

**Openers:**
- Eighteen months in, the Maitre d leaves. Name the moment you decide whether to look outside, and what you have looked at first.
- Sideways: the book's line is "don't assume the folks who got you here will get you there" (p. 191). Read it against WP p. 19 and say which one you would defend to the team.

**Narrowing questions:**
- Counting from the first opening after dinner is running, or from day one?
- Pool first (WP p. 14), always?
- The book's one-third split, no ratio with the promotion rate tracked (WP p. 23), or something else?
- An outside leadership hire carries a note: no one internal was ready, and how close the nearest was. Where does the note live and who reads it?

**What the book says:** outside senior hires are slow and often fail; start with people you know; the rough split for fast growth; the experience trap (pp. 189 to 191).

**White-paper default:** internal succession to the point where a manager is rarely hired from outside (WP p. 19); the ready-now pool (WP p. 14); promotion rate tracked (WP p. 23).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Groups with strong internal promotion typically still hire a new property's first management layer from outside; the promise starts with the first backfill.

**Options:** (a) from the first opening after dinner is running, pool first, no ratio, the note. Commits Sŏn to writing that note where the team can see it. (b) The book's split after year one. Commits Sŏn to a lagging ratio. (c) From day one. Commits Sŏn to explaining every early hire. Depth: cons. 8.

**Watch for:** a note that becomes a formality; a pool nobody has updated.

**A finished answer:** the event, the pool rule, the ratio answer, and the note's form and home.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic; the chef partner for kitchen leadership.
