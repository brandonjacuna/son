# 3.1 Recruiting: considerations

Context for the working session, in the book's order. Each topic gives what the white paper holds (WP p. N, or where it is silent), what the earlier extraction work found worth keeping, options with what each commits Sŏn to, and where Sŏn may diverge from the book. Nothing here is a decision. Task numbers refer to `tasks.md`.

A "seat" is one position in Sŏn's structure (the word 2.1 and 4.1 use); a "read" is one thing an interview scores.

## 1. Why hiring is everything: the funnel, the pyramid, and the founders' role

**What the book says.** Talent is destiny, so hiring must be rigorous at every level and involve nearly everyone (p. 168). Talent acquisition is a funnel from awareness to onboarding (p. 169). Different levels need different processes: a set process with a single manager decision for volume roles, a standing committee in the middle, a bespoke process at the top (p. 170). Founders interview every finalist while the company is small, model the no on a candidate who is merely fine, and explain that no so it can be taught (p. 171).

**What the white paper holds.** Hiring is a system that respects the candidate's time and reads competency rather than instinct (WP p. 17). Interviews are semi-structured, documented, and assess competencies, cultural alignment, growth potential, and whether the building can support the person (WP p. 18). The white paper does not say what stage the founders hold in a hire, and it describes no hiring committee. Its structure has no top: the web has a centre and an outside edge, and new people always enter at the outside edge (WP p. 09). The management layer is two peers, the Operations Lead and the Maitre d, with the Chef Partner owning culinary execution during service (WP p. 10).

**From the earlier work.** The funnel's useful residue is measurement (counts, stage timing, source), not the posture of advertising and closing; Sŏn has no single hiring bottleneck and treats a good no as a route back to a pool. The pyramid scales rigor by altitude, which does not fit a structure that classes seats by the shape of the work: a phone screen for a high-volume seat is a different process, not a lower bar. A standing committee is replaced by independent structured scorecards and a single decision owner, with disagreement captured as a named read, which keeps the book's point that disagreement should be specific (pp. 176 to 177). The founders' stage was argued narrow: a check on conduct and on whether the building can support the candidate, not a veto over the hiring manager; ending a quarter after opening for frontline seats and never for leadership seats, which raises what outside read, if any, replaces it.

**Options for the founders' stage (3.1.3).** (a) The book's version: a founder interviews every finalist and may say no. Commits both founders to a slot in every hire before opening and puts the last word above the hiring manager. (b) A narrow founder stage with two reads, conduct and whether the building can support the person, that cannot overturn the hiring manager on competency. Commits Sŏn to defining those two reads well enough to score, and to living with a hire the founder would not have made. (c) No founder stage; the founders are hiring managers for the leads and step back from frontline hiring from the start. Commits Sŏn to trusting leads who have not yet hired at Sŏn. Whichever is chosen, 3.1.41 asks what replaces it when it ends.

**Options for the hiring manager (3.1.4).** (a) The domain lead is the hiring manager for every seat in their domain, the founders for the leads. Commits Sŏn to not opening frontline seats until the lead is seated, or naming a stand-in. (b) The Maitre d, who owns team development (WP p. 10), is hiring manager for all floor seats including the inward-facing strand. Commits the Maitre d to a read of the inward-facing work they may not do themselves. (c) Both founders jointly for the first cohort, handing over at a named date. Commits the founders to the load and the first leads to inheriting hires they did not make.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** No hiring committee; classes by shape of work rather than level; the founders' role possibly narrower than the book's finalist interview. The book itself says the founders' no should be built into process over time (p. 171); the earlier work treats that as the starting design.

## 2. Building the pipeline: talent brand and sourcing

**What the book says.** Networks work first, do not scale, and narrow the team (pp. 171 to 172). A values-expressive project aimed at your candidate audience builds the talent brand better than generic postings; Stripe's Capture the Flag is the story (pp. 172 to 174). Post where the audience already lives; host events (p. 172).

**What the white paper holds.** The documented build is the acquisition engine, producing content that persists and a community that arrives invested; a chef search is named as an example of that content, and the community weighs in on some decisions (WP p. 29). Streaming gives an audience hours with the founders directly (WP p. 30). Creators are offered a seat at a training day (WP p. 31). The reputation that makes the best people want to work here before a role is posted is named as a return on culture (WP p. 23). The local labor market is tight and the best people have options (WP p. 16). Korean fine dining at this level does not exist locally (WP p. 37); the implication, not stated, is that deep cuisine fluency will be developed in-house rather than found.

**From the earlier work.** A follower-candidate (someone who applied because they watched the build) already holds an accurate picture of the operation; a careless no loses a community member and a customer, not only an applicant. Showing the search (the seat, the process, the reasoning) is low-risk content; showing an identifiable candidate belongs to top-tier review with consent obtained after the decision, never as a condition of being considered. Never invite the audience to weigh in on a hire, film or stream an assessment, or use a rejection as content mid-process. Sourcing through the build's audience and the founders' networks reproduces the existing makeup; a source-and-stage report exists so a later position on composition has data.

**Options for sourcing (3.1.17).** (a) The build's audience first, postings and networks second. Commits Sŏn to the candidate rule in 3.1.18 before the first search is shown, and to a pool that skews toward people who already like the story. (b) Postings and industry peers first, the build kept to the seat and the reasoning. Commits Sŏn to slower reach and a wider pool. (c) The founders' networks for the leads, the build and postings for the cohort. Commits the founders to the book's warning about networks (p. 172) and to watching source share on 3.1.39.

**Options for what the build may show (3.1.18).** (a) The search and nothing of the candidate until consent after the decision, as above. Commits Sŏn to one added rule in the content framework and a consent record. (b) Candidates may appear with consent obtained before the stage, as many documented businesses do. Commits Sŏn to asking consent from someone who cannot freely refuse. (c) Nothing of any search is shown. Commits Sŏn to forgoing what WP p. 29 names as some of its strongest content.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book's talent-brand project is a contest; Sŏn's is the build itself. The tension the book does not face is that the audience is invited into decisions (WP p. 29) and a hire is a decision that must be walled off from that.

## 3. The careers page, the posting, and the candidate sheet

**What the book says.** Once you drive traffic you need a page that explains the company and lists roles. Descriptions should be clear about the work and expectations without being verbose, prescriptive, or rosy; say when a role is not fully defined; ambiguity that makes a candidate uncomfortable is useful self-selection; watch for language that transmits bias. Early descriptions are the start of the talent brand (pp. 174 to 175).

**What the white paper holds.** Every position has documented competency requirements visible to anyone (WP p. 11). The candidate is told exactly what to expect at each step (WP p. 17). At the first interview the candidate receives one sheet with the org chart, their direct lead, compensation and benefits, communication expectations, the training plan with pay dates, the compensation mechanics, and the advancement path, "no surprises and nothing withheld" (WP p. 18). The org chart is elsewhere called a structural misread, and the model is a web with no top and no bottom (WP pp. 07, 09). The compensation percentage built into menu price is "still being finalized" (WP pp. 20, 22). The white paper is silent on a careers page; the honest description of working at Sŏn is 2.1.19's deliverable.

**From the earlier work.** "Direct lead" on the sheet should mean the domain lead who signs off competencies and holds the check-in, not a manager who assigns and reviews from above. The org chart item should be a rendering of the person's node and its neighbours in the web, drawn by hand if the designed artifact (WP p. 15, 4.1.19) does not exist yet; handing a candidate a conventional chart with themselves at the bottom contradicts the argument before their first day. Postings and the sheet should state the seat's decision range and escalation triggers directly, so a later readiness check (every server can state their own range unprompted) is achievable. A years-of-experience requirement is among the weakest predictors of performance. One labor pool staffs several times of day, so a posting states which periods a seat works. The sheet's compensation section cannot be delivered as designed until 5.5 is complete, yet the leads' first interviews may come months earlier: a sequencing conflict, not a missing number.

**Options for the careers page (3.1.27).** (a) A page on the restaurant's site holding 2.1.19's description, open seats, what to expect, and the conduct statement, with the applicant system behind it: commits Sŏn to keeping it current and to a public conduct statement. (b) The applicant system's page, linked from the build: commits Sŏn to what that page can hold. (c) No page at launch: commits Sŏn to candidates who did not watch the build arriving without the description.

**Options for the sheet before 5.5 is complete (3.1.30).** (a) The mechanics as designed with the open piece named: candidates decide on an unfinished design. (b) A range and the date the mechanics will be final: commits Sŏn to both. (c) No first interview for a lead until the sheet is complete: commits 5.5 to finishing first. Financials are not a source for this work; no figure is recorded either way.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book's careers page is a destination; WP p. 29 treats the build as the destination and a page as a listing. The book has no candidate sheet; the sheet is the white paper's own promise and its hardest sequencing problem.

## 4. Hiring is everyone's job: the recruiter, the commitments, and the candidate's time

**What the book says.** Do not let recruiting become a silo; people lose the habit of representing the culture and feeling responsible for who joins (p. 175). A recruiter or coordinator becomes necessary past roughly 10 to 20 people when hiring is constant; a team when hiring several people a week; a recruiter never substitutes for the hiring manager (p. 175). Stripe wrote mutual commitments: what everyone owes hiring (accurate calendars, knowing the role and the interview, reading the candidate in advance, feedback on time, specific participation, prompt follow-up, always recruiting) and what recruiting owes back (respecting calendars, valuing referrals, clear descriptions and a transparent process, well-run meetings, care with sensitive information, the company's long-term interest over any single hire) (pp. 175 to 178).

**What the white paper holds.** The applicant system lets candidates self-schedule, choose language and format, see what to expect, with no manual re-entry and no unanswered application (WP p. 17). Format choice "does double duty as a qualification filter": someone exploring chooses a phone screen, someone serious chooses in person, someone with a transportation constraint chooses video (WP p. 17). Language choice protects dignity (WP p. 17). Speed is respect; a multi-week wait signals the business does not understand the candidate's reality; no one is ghosted; a candidate treated well at the no returns to the pool next year (WP p. 18). Software removes the back-office administrator (WP p. 22). Employees are the users of the internal systems (WP p. 17).

**From the earlier work.** A standalone recruiting seat was argued against even past the book's thresholds: the recruiter's two jobs are logistics, which the applicant system carries, and closing, which a fully transparent sheet is designed to make unnecessary; what remains already has an owner in the structure. A pipeline that depends on someone remembering to check it is a system failure. WP p. 17's format-as-filter line sits in tension with the same page's dignity claim; the earlier team read format as the candidate's own signal, never scored. The mutual commitments were drafted as guarantees: a complete seat description before a seat opens, training before anyone runs a stage, interview time scheduled as work, scorecards before discussion, confidentiality, the bar unchanged when the floor is short, every candidate answered. Every recruiting decision point needs an entry in 2.2.4's register, never filled by guessing.

**Options for the recruiting seat (3.1.14).** (a) Never a standalone seat; functions distributed and the applicant system as coordinator: leads carry their own pipelines on top of service, and the system must actually be configured (3.1.35). (b) A coordinator at the book's trigger, never a closer: a seat the white paper's headcount argument (WP p. 22) does not include. (c) The Maitre d's domain holds candidate experience for the whole house: a load that crosses the seam with the Chef Partner.

**Options for the format rule (3.1.10).** (a) The candidate's choice, never recorded against them. (b) Noted and one input at the offer stage, with a written reason: the white paper's own words, and an explanation owed to the video candidate with a bus schedule. (c) In person required for the final stage only: travel costs for candidates Sŏn invites.

**Options for the response commitments (3.1.11).** (a) Fixed windows per stage, an owner alerted on a miss, published on the what-to-expect page: Sŏn is seen missing them. (b) Internal windows only: less than "exactly what to expect" (WP p. 17).

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book adds a recruiter at a headcount; Sŏn may never add one. The book's commitments are between employees and a recruiting team; Sŏn's are between the house and anyone who takes part in a hire.

## 5. Insight on talent needs before a seat opens: the reads, the profile, and the conduct statement

**What the book says.** Before opening a role, ask who you have hired, who is doing well and scaling, and where perspectives are missing (p. 178). The ideal hire is good at the work, has high impact, and loves the work; study the people who fit and mine what they share for questions (pp. 178 to 179). Role profiles shift as the company and the role evolve (pp. 179 to 180). A suggested question: is the candidate comfortable owning their own progress rather than expecting it handed to them (pp. 180 to 181). Test for curiosity and learning aptitude over credentials; experienced candidates are often just better at being interviewed (p. 189).

**What the white paper holds.** Interviews assess specific competencies, cultural alignment, and growth potential, and whether the building is equipped to create the environment the person needs (WP p. 18). Two strands need different intelligences, and neither is senior (WP pp. 10 to 11). Advancement requires preparation, not permission, and favours the people who want it most (WP pp. 11, 14). No target for the team's composition is stated anywhere; the inclusion mechanics the white paper names are language choice and format choice (WP p. 17).

**From the earlier work.** Four reads were proposed: competency for the seat's type of work; evidence of self-directed growth (something taught to oneself unasked, echoing p. 189); alignment with baseline conduct and comfort with real autonomy; and what the business would need to provide for this person to succeed. "Fit," "attitude," and "culture fit" were treated as unscoreable, the place bias enters. No temperament or personality typology anywhere in hiring: service pressure rewards assertive, expressive people, an interviewer pool with that skew screens out quieter, detail-oriented candidates, and no one notices because the same people do the noticing; the fix is structural (detail-oriented work in its own seats with its own rigor, measurement in systems, the practical scored by a fixed rubric by someone other than the decision owner). The typology may live in a lead's own working-with-me document (1.1). A conduct statement was drafted: selection on the defined reads only; every stage in the candidate's chosen language; no record of personal identity beyond working languages; source-and-stage patterns reviewed as information, never targets, with a narrowing pool answered by wider outreach, never a moved bar; no target for the team's makeup.

**Options for the reads (3.1.5).** (a) The four reads above, with "cultural alignment" from WP p. 18 rendered as a conduct read against 2.1.5's principles. Commits Sŏn to defining conduct concretely enough to score and to never scoring "fit." (b) The white paper's three plus one as written, with cultural alignment defined against the principles. Commits Sŏn to interviewers agreeing on what alignment looks like in an answer. (c) The book's three circles as the reads (good at it, impact, loves it). Commits Sŏn to "impact" being scoreable before anyone has worked here.

**Options for the conduct statement (3.1.7).** (a) The drafted statement, with monitoring and no target. Commits Sŏn to publishing what it will not do and to the report in 3.1.39. (b) The statement plus an aspiration on composition, decided in 4.7. Commits Sŏn to a position the white paper does not take. (c) No statement; the reads and the language rule speak for themselves. Commits Sŏn to candidates inferring its conduct.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book mines existing successful employees; Sŏn has none on day one, so version one of the profile (3.1.6) is a hypothesis and 3.1.42 is where the book's method actually runs. Sŏn's structure has no "impact on the company" ladder to read; the strands and cross-strand fluency (WP p. 11) replace it.

## 6. Referrals

**What the book says.** A strong source once you know what success looks like; not sufficient; hard to scale; can undermine diversity; treat referred candidates well regardless, since it doubles as an engagement signal (pp. 180 to 181). The author's own hire came through a referral where the referrer still ran her through a rigorous screen (p. 181).

**What the white paper holds.** Peer recognition runs through a platform (WP p. 19). Silent on referral pay and on referral handling.

**From the earlier work.** Referrals enter the applicant system as a tagged source with the referrer named, run the same stages as any candidate, the referrer takes no part in reading them, and the referrer learns the outcome when the candidate does. Against a cash bonus: a referral is information from someone who knows the local market, recognition already exists, and a bounty turns a trust signal into a paid tip; source data will show whether referrals flow without one.

**Options (3.1.13).** (a) No cash bonus; recognition through the platform; the mechanics above. Commits Sŏn to watching whether referrals arrive at all. (b) A bonus paid after the hire passes a retention point. Commits Sŏn to a cost 5.5 must place and to the book's diversity warning with an incentive on it. (c) Decide after the first cohort, from source data. Commits Sŏn to the first cohort's referrers receiving nothing they were not told about.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Referral bonuses paid after a retention period are common in technology companies and in larger restaurant groups; the book's own caution is that referral pipelines tend to reproduce the team.

## 7. Screening, the pool, and the paid practical

**What the book says.** Résumé review against outlined qualifications; some companies add a short assessment for signal, at a cost to the pipeline while it is still being built; then a phone screen and onsite (p. 181). The funnel does not end at acceptance; it ends at onboarding (p. 169).

**What the white paper holds.** A short phone screen for high-volume roles such as line cook and porter (WP p. 17). For roles with a physical skill component, the second interview is a paid practical in a real environment, replacing the unpaid tryout shift, which is "frequently degrading, legally exposed, and a poor predictor of actual performance" (WP p. 18). No unpaid stage of onboarding (WP p. 19). No one touches a table until they are actually ready (WP p. 19). A candidate treated well at the no returns to the pool next year (WP p. 18).

**From the earlier work.** The practical's employment form (on payroll for the day, or engaged another way) is unresolved and carries exposure either way; it needs counsel and an interim form, and no practical runs without one. A later note from Brandon fixed the practical's place: paid at base rate, outside the regular roster while on the practical, with the form left to counsel. A practical costs the running shift (less slack, an assessor's attention, a slower section); it should not run on a shift flagged short, on the first shift of a newly opened service period, on any shift being filmed, or on a schedule set to a hiring deadline. Two sessions reconciled "no one touches a table until ready" with the practical: a candidate on a practical is never alone on a table or section and never the shift's coverage; a new hire's first solo shift still has someone nearby until the readiness check. A pool candidate for a critical seat repeats the scored practical rather than being taken as ready.

**Options for the practical's interim form (3.1.36).** (a) Brandon's prior position: paid at base rate, outside the roster, form to counsel. Commits Sŏn to running the first practicals on an interim footing. (b) No practical until counsel has answered. Commits Sŏn to hiring the first physical-skill seats on interviews alone, or delaying them. (c) Every practical candidate is onboarded as an employee for the day. Commits Sŏn to the full paperwork per candidate and to counsel's view of what that creates.

**Options for the pool (3.1.12).** (a) Every decline after the first interview enters the pool unless the candidate declines it or conduct ruled them out; a pool candidate for a critical seat repeats the scored stages. Commits Sŏn to keeping records for a year and to telling candidates so. (b) Only "not now" declines enter the pool, named as such at the no. Commits interviewers to distinguishing not-now from no. (c) No formal pool; a declined candidate may reapply. Commits Sŏn to less than WP p. 18's promise.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Employment counsel in the United States commonly treat a working trial as compensable time, which is why some groups now pay for trail shifts. Structured screening predicts performance better than unstructured conversation; Google's published move to structured interviews is the best-known corporate example.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book lists an optional assessment before the interview; Sŏn's assessment is paid and real, and comes second. The book's screening is a step; Sŏn's carries a legal question the book does not face.

## 8. New leader recruiting

**What the book says.** Leaders are needed before the organization asks for them (pp. 181 to 182). List the jobs you have done in the last three weeks and sort them (p. 182). Give away your Legos roughly every six months (pp. 182 to 183). Settle what the work is, what great looks like, and how you will assess it before sourcing; never calibrate on live candidates; talk to advisers and people who have held or hired the role (pp. 183 to 184, 187 to 189). Question whether a COO is needed at all; a narrower operations role may proxy it (pp. 186 to 187). Outside senior hires are slow and succeed a quarter to half the time; start with talent you know; a rough one-third split for fast-growing companies; the experience trap and the playbook thinker; search firms need heavy investment and networks often outperform them (pp. 189 to 191). Do not assume the people who got you here will get you there, weighed against loyalty (pp. 191 to 192).

**What the white paper holds.** The management layer is two peers, the Operations Lead and the Maitre d (WP p. 10); the Head of Beverage owns the program (WP pp. 10 to 11); roughly eight leadership lines run the full day (WP p. 22). The chef partner is an equal founding partner who owns the culinary vision, and that is what makes the culinary seat a founding one rather than a hire (WP p. 03); the business does not rest on any one person (WP p. 37); a chef search is content of the build (WP p. 29). Dinner opens first and runs alone for at least a quarter (WP p. 36). For leadership and salaried roles the final round is a dinner in the room (WP p. 18). Internal succession is the goal, to the point where a manager is rarely hired from outside (WP p. 19); the ready-now pool surfaces who is furthest along on a departure (WP p. 14); internal promotion rate is tracked (WP p. 23).

**From the earlier work.** The record disagreed with itself on the chef seat and laid out both paths: as a partner, a negotiation belonging with the operating agreement, 2.1.11's criteria as due-diligence questions, a cooking session in a kitchen of the candidate's choosing; as a hire, the full leadership process with the criteria as a scored rubric and the cooking as a formal practical. Both paths agree that kitchen seat design waits for the seat and a menu, that public content is framed as the founders' search and never about candidates, and that the food's identity belongs to the business regardless of who executes it. The two lead roles do not map to titles a search firm would search against. The final-round dinner cannot happen in a room that does not exist; a meal at a chosen local restaurant preserves the read (how a candidate behaves as a customer, how they treat staff, whether they can hold a table without performing) with no filming and no scoring of that room's staff, and with the caveat that it reads the candidate against that room's standards. No fixed internal-to-external ratio: the book's split is for a company outrunning its bench, Sŏn's growth is in service periods, and day one has no bench; a tracked promotion rate is more useful. The succession commitment counts from the first opening after dinner is running, the pool is checked before any outside search, and an outside leadership hire carries a note saying no one internal was ready and how close the nearest was.

**Options for the chef partner search (3.1.21).** (a) Partnership search: 2.1.10's standing governs, the founders run it as due diligence, the operating agreement is the instrument, and 2.1.11's criteria are the questions. Commits Sŏn to a search outside the applicant system and to the build showing only the founders' side of it. (b) Leadership hire: the standard process, the criteria as a rubric, the cooking session as a practical. Commits Sŏn to rewriting WP p. 03's framing and parts of the founding documents. (c) A hire with a path to partnership after a defined period. Commits Sŏn to a two-step instrument and to a chef who joins without the vote WP p. 36 gives the culinary partner on lunch.

**Options for the search firm (3.1.22).** (a) No firm for either lead; the build, networks, and industry peers carry it. Commits the founders to being the recruiters (p. 191). (b) A firm for the Operations Lead only, whose work is closer to a market role. Commits Sŏn to briefing a firm on a split role, and to a cost this work does not price. (c) A firm for both. Commits Sŏn to the book's warning that firms need heavy investment to represent you.

**Options for the final round before the room exists (3.1.26).** (a) A meal at a local restaurant the founders choose, same rules. Commits Sŏn to reading the candidate in someone else's room. (b) A working session in the build (a walk of the site, a vendor call, a decision) as the final round instead. Commits Sŏn to losing the customer-side read the dinner gives. (c) No final round until dinner exists; the leads are hired on interviews and a reference read. Commits Sŏn to fewer signals on the two most consequential hires.

**Options for succession (3.1.40).** (a) Counting from the first opening after dinner is running; pool first; no ratio; promotion rate tracked; the note on an outside hire. Commits Sŏn to writing that note in public inside the team. (b) The book's split adopted as a guide after year one. Commits Sŏn to a ratio that lags what already happened. (c) Counting from day one, which makes every early hire an exception to explain. Commits Sŏn to paperwork with no information in it.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Founders meeting every finalist until the company was hundreds of people is the book's Stripe account (p. 171). Amazon's "bar raiser," a trained interviewer from outside the hiring team with a defined vote, is the best-known form of the outside read 3.1.41 asks about. Groups that promote from within still usually hire a new property's first management layer from outside (p. 190 says why).

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** No COO question, because the general manager is already split into two peers (WP p. 10). No ratio. The founders' first two hires are peers who will not report to either founder's day-to-day direction, which the book's leader-hiring method does not quite anticipate. The chef seat may not be a hire at all.
