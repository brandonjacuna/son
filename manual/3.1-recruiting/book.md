# 3.1 Recruiting: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 3, pp. 167 to 192 (the chapter's opening and its recruiting section, including new leader recruiting), with the chapter's exercises at pp. 228 to 259. Full text: `extraction/build/s05/book.md` and `extraction/build/s05/exercises.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## The chapter's frame: talent is destiny

If talent is everything, the hiring process should be everything too. The goal is to find the people who will thrive and have the most positive impact at every level, then acclimate them so they can carry the mission and culture forward (p. 168). Early talent becomes future leaders; her example is a recruiter among her first reports who now runs Stripe's recruiting and people-partner organization (p. 168).

Three rules run through the chapter:

- **Rigor at every level.** Companies that mechanize hiring for most roles and treat leadership hiring differently damage quality and trust. Leadership hiring may be more customized, but the fundamentals and the cultural weight of the process should be the same for leaders and employees. Leaders who "seemingly waltz in" are suspected even with strong résumés (p. 168).
- **Quality and speed together.** The scariest thing you can say to an operations leader is "I want quality and speed," and a fast-growing company has to reach that anyway. The only way is that practically everyone is involved in hiring, prioritizes it, and holds the operational and cultural standards. People are motivated to, because these are their future colleagues (pp. 168 to 169).
- **It ends at onboarding, not at the offer.** Hiring starts with the organization's objectives and cultural principles, then an outline of the process and how success is measured. The process ends with a successful onboarding and strong connections between the new hire, their manager, and the company (p. 169).

## The funnel and the pyramid

Talent acquisition, she says, is marketing and sales: invest in the talent brand, lead identification, and outreach to drive traffic (job page views, applicants) into a funnel, then assess lead quality and optimize conversion at each stage. The human element is more variable than a sales funnel, but the structure repeats (p. 169). Figure 9 is the hiring conversion process.

Hiring needs differ by level, which she draws as a pyramid (Figure 10, pp. 169 to 170):

- **Base, volume hiring.** Much of the interviewing is done by people already proven in the role, then a final manager interview. A set process, many available interviewers; the number of interviewers mirrors the number of hires. Instead of committees, a uniform candidate review and approval process may do.
- **Middle.** A separate, set hiring team or committee with the same interviewers for every candidate, including cross-functional partners, so pattern recognition builds conviction. A first hire into a new role is tailored; more hires into an existing team can be prescriptive.
- **Top.** Still a hiring team, but bespoke: current executives plus people chosen for this search. Top roles never fully standardize.

Gain efficiency and scale only once the process repeats at high quality (p. 170).

## Sidebar: the founder's role in hiring (p. 171)

Should a founder interview every finalist? Early on, "say, 100 employees or less per founder or trusted senior leader," yes. Early hires set the trajectory, and founders are best placed to model the behavior they want: a rigorous interview and a high bar.

Where founders matter most is the candidate who is "just fine": meets every qualification, passes the rubric, and leaves a hard-to-name concern about attitude and potential. Interviewers hesitate to raise concerns that come from instinct rather than from a question in the process, so the no-hire on a just-fine candidate has to be modeled. Be transparent about those moments and explain why; use them to teach others to look for the thing that amounts to "I'm excited to work with this person, no matter the role." Eventually that filter can be built into the process, but it starts as leadership behavior.

Stripe's path: founders set the example and taught each new leader the hiring principles; senior leaders took on the finalist interview; a founder or executive met every finalist until Stripe had hundreds of employees; the founders stepped back only when confident that hiring managers would proxy both a rigorous process and the right behaviors.

The chapter then runs in three steps: recruiting (attracting candidates into the pipeline), hiring (decision-making from onsite to offer), and onboarding (setting the new hire up to succeed), with a close on improving the loop over time (p. 171). This section covers recruiting only.

## Building awareness: the talent brand

A company has to actively educate candidates that it exists and seek out hires. Small businesses use listing sites; small growing companies in competitive talent markets have to make their case to people with many options (pp. 171 to 172).

Early on the easiest thing is to mine the founders' and early employees' personal networks. It works initially, it does not scale, and it can create a lack of diversity, because "our friends tend to be just like us" in experience, background, and often race and gender identity (p. 172).

Stripe's tactics: hiring a few early users who gave the best feedback; founders answering questions where the audience already was; blog posts; and Capture the Flag, a coding contest that drew thousands of visitors, hundreds of finishers, and a few direct hires, and built the talent brand (pp. 172 to 174). The sidebar's lesson: when a developer tried a CTF, the reaction was "here is a company that values what I value," and each one was built by a self-organized group of volunteers (p. 174).

## The careers page and the job description

Once there is traffic, there must be a page that explains what it is like to work at the company and lets candidates browse open roles. Both the company description and the job descriptions are hard to get right while roles are still forming (p. 174). Her rules:

- Be as clear as you can about the work environment and the expectations, without being verbose or prescriptive.
- Open the aperture wide enough to attract people who could succeed, but not so wide that someone starts a role that does not resemble the posting they projected their hopes onto.
- If you are not sure what the role will be, say so. Candidates uncomfortable with that ambiguity are filtering themselves correctly.
- Watch for language about the role or qualifications that transmits bias about who should apply. It is subtle; she names tools that neutralize such signals or find undiscovered talent (p. 174).

What you say early about the company and the first roles is the beginning of the talent brand and the company brand. Take as much care with it as with first product releases; first candidates deserve the same attention as first users (p. 174).

## The recruiting seat and its timing

Stripe's process was run by engineers rather than recruiters for a long time. The upside: ownership by every employee of every part of the process, and a shared wish for every hire to succeed. The downside: it became so celebrated that a leader publicly said Stripe did not need recruiters, which later hurt her recruiting of recruiters (p. 175).

Her thresholds: once past the initial team (say 10 to 20 people) and consistently hiring, you likely need at least one recruiter and possibly a coordinator for scheduling and logistics. Once hiring multiple people a week, build a recruiting team with an experienced leader (p. 175).

A recruiter is never a substitute for the hiring manager, the person accountable for the new hire's work. The two need a tight partnership. Where the eventual manager is not yet known, people and a process must proxy the hiring manager's commitment (p. 175; the candidate review sidebar is in the hiring section).

## Hiring is everyone's job

Stripe holds internal guides that outline each person's role in recruiting and hiring below the leader level. She warns against recruiting becoming a separate appendage, "a human factory," because people then stop learning to represent the company and stop feeling responsible for their colleagues' selection and success (p. 175).

To involve many people and keep them engaged, especially at volume, you need a strong process and clear commitments between recruiting and everyone else, and public celebration of the people who contribute most, repeated, so hiring reads as a core responsibility rather than a side job (p. 175).

### Sidebar: recruiting commitments (pp. 176 to 178)

Stripe's internal document of mutual commitments, in two lists.

Stripes' commitments to recruiting: keep your calendar accurate; know the role and the interview (review the job description, capabilities, and frameworks; take the interview training; read prep material; know the capabilities you are assigned to assess); know the routine (do not leave the candidate alone; know who is after you and when you end); know the candidate (review their materials; managers also read earlier-stage feedback); submit feedback on time; participate actively in hiring meetings (read others' feedback only after submitting your own; be specific and clear about doubts and tie them to a capability, "candidate was dismissive toward person X" rather than "I have some culture FUD"; speak up, or go to the recruiter privately); follow up with candidates (send enthusiasm notes at offer, respond to questions promptly, reply to a recruiter's introduction within 48 hours); always be recruiting.

Recruiting's commitments to Stripes: respect calendars; value referrals and referrers; set candidates up for success (clear job descriptions and interview frameworks before interviewing starts; transparency about what to expect; advocate and sounding board from first contact through rejection or offer; direct and clear about intentions; benefit of the doubt and empathy at every stage); communicate clearly and often; shepherd candidates through onsites; set hiring meetings up for success (facilitate, moderate for fairness, keep the discussion tied to capabilities and whether the candidate can do the job); communicate with hiring managers and interviewers throughout; handle sensitive information carefully and privately; do what is right for the company, which above all means maintaining the hiring bar.

## Build insights on talent needs and candidate success

Before opening a role, study what success looks like at your company (p. 178): what kinds of people have we hired; who is doing really well; who is scaling with the company, and why, and what qualities and capabilities do they show; what perspectives and experiences are we missing, where are we less diverse, what are our weak points and capability gaps.

Her three-circle picture, from Condoleezza Rice's account of choosing work: people who are good at their work, people who have great impact on the company's progress, and people who love what they do. The ideal employee is in all three (Figure 11, pp. 178 to 179). List everyone at the company who is, find what else they have in common, and turn that into recruiting questions. Motivation, per Daniel Pink, comes from autonomy, mastery, and purpose; intrinsically motivated people have the energy to get curious and learn (pp. 178 to 179).

Roles evolve. Her country-manager example: early generalists who built traction; some scaled into team builders accountable for revenue and some did not, because they were great for the early stage but lacked the desire or ability for the evolved role. Later hires were made against an understanding of who had succeeded, adapted as the role changed. The people who scale are those who anticipate what they need to learn now to excel at what the role becomes in six months, and who can step back and ask what they are missing. Keep asking what experiences and skills the next phase will require (pp. 179 to 180).

## Open a role and help applicants self-select

Resist making job descriptions into rosy advertisements. Design descriptions and the process to attract people who might fit and discourage people who might not. Set clear expectations for the role, and supply the mission, a culture guide, and practical facts on benefits and work practices. Above all, be transparent about the work environment: if it is fast-paced and people are expected to act independently, say so. A self-aware company can hire well (p. 180).

Stripe's culture guide pairs each principle with a question a candidate can ask themselves (pp. 180 to 181): whether they are all right with substantial risk and ambiguity; whether they want to work hard somewhere that could never be called cushy; whether they are comfortable owning their own career outcomes rather than being handed a progression by one decision-maker; when they last changed their mind on a fundamental opinion; whether they want high-variance days met with a thoughtful, measured response.

## Referrals

Once success is clear and the description is posted, build a strong referral process: your employees know potential candidates, and if you hired well their networks are as strong as they are. Referrals should not be the only source; referral processes are hard to scale and can undermine diversity. But do not underestimate them, treat referrals well, and read the referral rate as a signal of employee happiness and engagement (pp. 180 to 181). Her own Google interview came through a friend's referral, and the referring manager's 45-minute grilling showed what ownership of the process looks like (p. 181).

## Screening processes

Screening differs with applicant volume. First, a résumé review against the role's stated qualifications (referrals may skip it). Some sought-after companies add a step that tests commitment and collects data, such as a coding assessment or a short written project; her example written exercise is in the chapter appendix. Beware of creating too many hurdles while the talent brand and pipeline are still being built (p. 181).

Once a candidate looks viable, a recruiter or hiring manager runs a phone screen; some companies add a second screen by a team member before the onsite. Either way, the end of screening is an invitation to formal interviews (p. 181), which the hiring section covers.

## New leader recruiting

### When you need a leader (pp. 181 to 183)

People in the company will not tell you they need a leader; her own hire was called "too early" by the team days before Stripe needed her. Growth from 100 to 400 happens fast; you need your leaders before the beast takes off (pp. 181 to 182).

The founder's blind spot: being so close to the work that you do not see how many jobs you are doing that others could do. Her exercise for a founder: write down every job you performed in the past three weeks; separate the ones only the founder can do (leadership hiring, some investor conversations, the culture, the strategic vision, major investment decisions) from the ones others could; for those, ask whether you already have someone but got too involved (a sign they are not scaling) or whether you are covering a missing role by instinct (p. 182). Pull out of the day to day, survey what changed in the last three to six months, and form a theory of what will grow in importance enough to need leadership. You may need to hire now for work that arrives in six to twelve months (p. 182).

Her Google test: when a question came in, wait for someone else on the team to answer; it was a failure if only she could. Molly Graham's line: you have to give away your job every six months, especially for founders and CEOs (pp. 182 to 183). Stay vigilant about capability gaps, your own and the leadership team's, over the next three to five years; it takes setting ego aside. Better to give the Legos away too soon than too late (p. 183). Lisa Wardell: a leader spends time on what no one else can do (p. 183).

### Determine what kind of leader you need (pp. 183 to 189)

Her Omid Kordestani story: the Google founders did not know what to ask him, so he interviewed himself. Most candidates will not, so you do the legwork (p. 183).

For a new role you may not know what you are looking for, or you may have someone doing part of it and not know what an experienced person would add. Start by building an understanding of the role and outlining the experience, characteristics, and skills of a successful candidate. Whether promoting from within or hiring outside, be clear on three things: what is the work to be done, what does "great" look like, and how will we assess people against that benchmark. Then sort must-haves from nice-to-haves (p. 184). Dominique Crenn: understand the core of the business, then peel back to where you need to be better or need someone else (p. 184).

Talk to advisers, people at companies that have reached or passed your stage and filled the role, investors and board members, and people who have done the job. Do not use your interview process to calibrate your understanding of a role: you will churn through good candidates and damage your reputation (p. 184). Her COO story sidebar (pp. 185 to 186) is a worked example: a mutual exploration of strengths and interests with no default assumptions about responsibilities, the founders spending countless hours understanding how she thinks, and a title chosen to avoid the appearance of displacing a colleague. The "Do you need a COO?" box warns that a COO is an extra layer that may not be needed at scale, that the company may not yet attract someone who can scale in the role beyond a year or so, and that a business operations team and its head can proxy the role first (pp. 186 to 187).

Questions to ask others to build the rubric (p. 187): how is the role defined at your company and what is the person accountable for; the most important skills or capabilities; what in the person's background made them qualified; the biggest challenges of their first year; how they work with the CEO or their closest collaborator; advice on finding candidates. Ask a successful holder of the role to let you interview them as if a candidate, and be honest if you secretly hope to recruit them (pp. 187 to 188). End the research with a set of profiles to react to; patterns in why each would or would not be great become the rubric and refine the description (p. 188).

Build first-principles confidence too: strip the jargon and ask what the job to be done is and what skills and capabilities it needs. Her growth-marketing example walks from the work (events, content, ads, measurement, working with product, return on spend, building a team) to the must-haves (has built a similar team, did it produce results, which parts have they personally done, can they start as a team of one) (p. 188). Add what only you know about succeeding in your environment. The rubric becomes the interviewers' guide: what to probe, what to ask, how to assess answers (pp. 188 to 189).

One more element: complementarity with the existing leadership team. Stripe had a CFO candidate take the Insights Discovery assessment from Chapter 1; sharing results with the leadership team built mutual understanding and a common language (p. 189).

The experience trap (p. 189): the more experienced someone is, the better they are at being interviewed; and some have hit a ceiling and become "playbook thinkers," stuck on one way of doing things. Examine the quality of companies and teams they sought out. Above all seek trajectory, momentum, raw curiosity, and pure learning aptitude: desire, grit, and intelligence. Test for that, not only credible experience.

### Promoting from within or hiring from outside (pp. 189 to 192)

Outside senior hiring is risky: expect at least six months of search, and only about a quarter to a half of outside senior hires succeed. Whenever possible start with talent you know, develop them, and promote from within (pp. 189 to 190). Mature organizations should always have an internal successor for a critical role; small ones face a real dilemma between promoting someone not quite ready and hiring an unknown (p. 190). Be honest about whether your stage can attract the five-year person; often you hire the next-few-years person and hope they scale. Make that call deliberately, per role, and track people as they develop (p. 190).

Her rule of thumb for fast-growing early-stage companies: at least a third of promotions from within, a third from outside, and the last third depending on growth rate, needs, and capacity to develop and onboard (p. 190).

Search firms: mixed results. Good firms produce a list fast and land meetings, but they need real investment to understand the company, its stage, and how to position the role, or they set candidate expectations wrongly. Often your own network, board, and advisers beat a firm's cold outreach; turn to a firm when networks are exhausted. Large firms suit classic roles; boutiques suit roles tailored to your definition. Either way, "you are ultimately the recruiter": leadership candidates know the CEO and leadership team are who will matter to their success (pp. 190 to 191).

The decision tree (Figure 12, p. 192) scans the current talent pool once requirements and a few profiles exist. Watch both failure modes: romanticizing external hires, and holding on to existing talent out of loyalty when "the folks who got you here" may not get you there. Keep asking whether you have the talent for the next two to four years (p. 191). Capabilities such as building and leading a team take longer to develop and, early on, more often come from outside; internal hires bring deep knowledge of the person and the company, and long-tenured early employees become some of the most valuable leaders, to be moved across the company so they embed the culture (p. 191).
