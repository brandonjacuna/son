# 5.3 Creating a culture of informal feedback: considerations

What the white paper holds, what the old work argued that is worth keeping, the options, and where Sŏn may diverge from the book. Topics follow the book's order (pp. 395 to 398). "WP p. N" cites the September 2026 white paper; "cons. N" cites a section here.

## 1. What "feedback" means at Sŏn

**White paper.** The word is used for at least four things: the tools evolving on continuous feedback from the people who use them (WP p. 17); the team weighing in on management effectiveness, operational improvements, and culture (WP p. 19); a structured channel that syncs to the execution system, where employees surface issues and see action, with the loop closed (WP p. 22); and the feedback a person receives about their own progression (WP p. 18). No distinction is drawn.

**From the earlier work.** The lack of a distinction is a risk: a hard conversation about a person could be routed onto a channel meant for process complaints, or a process complaint read as a read of a person. The proposal was to disambiguate by destination rather than by the word: each kind has a surface, and nothing about a named person travels on the channel. 4.5.11 has already named destinations for most kinds; 5.3.2 names the kinds and closes the gaps.

**Options for 5.3.2.** (a) Four kinds, each with its destination; the word "feedback" reserved for observations to a person about their work, and the others called by their destination ("a channel item," "the upward question"). (b) Keep one word, rely on the destinations page to sort. (c) Three kinds, folding "about the house" into "about a tool or process." Each commits the destinations page (4.5.17) and the module to a vocabulary.

## 2. Whether candor is asked for, or built in

**White paper.** Employees are the users and the loop is closed (WP pp. 17, 22). Feedback runs both directions (WP p. 19). The white paper states mechanisms; it never asks candor of anyone as a disposition.

**The book.** If the feedback culture is wanting, leaders steer the change; the Hallmark line, that brutal honesty needs an environment that is not brutal (p. 396); an executive who solicits feedback in front of others and makes *The Speed of Trust* required reading (p. 398).

**From the earlier work.** The working thesis was that candor should not be asked of anyone as a value; it should be a predictable property of a system where every hard thing has a private, recorded destination, nothing difficult is said in a group, and no page holds a sentence about a person the person did not write. Whether that is achievable without any stated expectation was left open.

**Where Sŏn may diverge from the book.** The book's culture is one where people comment on each other's work freely and the founders model it in public. Sŏn's leaning is quieter: the founders ask, the mechanisms carry, and no one is asked to perform candor. The cost is the one the book names: new hires who lack confidence to speak up may never learn that they can, if no one ever says so.

**Options for 5.3.3.** (a) No stated expectation; mechanisms and the founders' asking carry it. (b) One sentence in orientation and the honest description ("you will be asked what could be better, and you can say it"), no principle. (c) A principle in 2.1.5's list, which then has to pass 5.1.4's deliverability test.

## 3. Feedback upward, and the concern you cannot say to your lead

**White paper.** Team members weigh in on management effectiveness (WP p. 19, stated outright). Silent on where a concern about one's own lead goes; 4.5.11 named that as a possible gap.

**The book.** Upward and sideways feedback without being asked is what companies do least well, because leaders set few examples, new people lack confidence, and kindness gets confused with silence (p. 396). Ask on different occasions and forums (p. 397).

**From the earlier work.** Two surfaces: the standing upward question at every check-in, in the person's words, written only if they choose; and the structured channel for anything about the house, a tool, a process, or a space, with an owner and a window (2.3.16). The hole: no destination for a concern specifically about the person's own lead that cannot be said to that lead. The interpreter rule (4.1.23, 4.8) applies to any check-in in a language the lead does not share; the interpreter writes and originates nothing and appears on no record beyond a note that one was used.

**Options for 5.3.4.** For the second destination: (a) the other lead (the floor's concern to the Operations Lead, the kitchen's to the Maitre d), which keeps it inside the leadership team; (b) the partner who holds that lead's monthly conversation (2.3.14), which keeps it out of the lead's peer; (c) a named founder for all, which is simplest and loads one seat; (d) the pulse's free field, which is anonymous and slow. Each commits Sŏn to saying what the reader does with it, and how the person learns it was heard.

## 4. How a lead asks

**White paper.** Silent beyond "feedback runs both directions" (WP p. 19).

**The book's five pointers** (pp. 397 to 398): ask on different occasions and forums, for something specific ("what could I have done differently in that meeting"); thank, don't explain; normalize by mentioning feedback received at the next team meeting; let it sit, repeat it back, come back later; follow up with what you did, even if the answer is "valid, not prioritized."

**From the earlier work.** The standing upward question makes asking a fixture rather than a lead's occasional initiative. "Mentioning it at a team meeting" was refused for anything about a person and kept as a general "here is what we changed" at a gathering. "Let it sit" became the repeat-back with no explaining. "Follow up" became structural: the channel's closing states for anything that went there, and the next check-in for the rest.

**Options for 5.3.5.** (a) As the earlier work translated it. (b) The book's five as written, including the lead mentioning at a team gathering that they received feedback and what they changed, with no person named. (c) (a) plus a fixed ask after every new format or rule ("what could be better about this"), which is the book's own example (p. 397).

## 5. Peer to peer

**White paper.** Peer recognition on a platform (WP p. 19). Silent on peer correction.

**The book.** People speaking up constructively without being asked is the mark of a strong culture; Stripe's was strongest in written comments and weakest sideways (pp. 395 to 396).

**From the earlier work.** Positive recognition is a peer's own words about a specific act, read aloud at a gathering. Corrective peer feedback: during service, only as an operating instruction inside the peer's own task authority (a runner correcting a runner about a table); between shifts, left to colleagues, with no surface, no requirement, and no record, refused only where others can overhear, in writing, or as an expected norm. Reasoning: every constructive thing a peer could say already has a non-peer destination (a capture, the channel, a joint work item), and the house declines to pressure anyone into doing peer-correction work.

**Where Sŏn may diverge from the book.** The book would call this a gap and want leaders to model sideways feedback. The earlier work treats it as a deliberate choice: a small floor cannot afford a grapevine, and "say it to their face" as a house norm becomes a license. 5.3.6 decides.

**Options for 5.3.6.** (a) The earlier work's three refusals and nothing more. (b) The book's culture: peers are asked to offer observations about each other's work directly and privately, and leads model it. (c) A middle: peers are told they may, taught the shape from 5.1.5, and never asked to.

## 6. Praise in public, and the recognition that is not one

**White paper.** Peer recognition through a platform, in the moment, landing differently than management praise (WP p. 19, stated outright).

**The book.** Praise publicly, criticize privately (p. 396).

**From the earlier work.** Public praise is the platform, never a manager's own praise of a named person in a group, to avoid a manager-endorsement dynamic in a room the white paper wants without altitude (WP p. 9). A recognition that is really a correction, a state judgment, or a comparison is not read; the reader tells the writer privately in two sentences; nothing about the writer is written; a pattern of declines from one writer is first a question about the prompt. The count of recognitions is never read for a person (4.3.13, 4.3.11 already refuse it as a signal).

**Options for 5.3.7.** (a) Platform only; no lead praises a named person in a group. (b) Leads may praise a named person in a group for a specific act, on the same rules as a recognition. (c) Leads praise through the platform as peers, never from the chair. Each commits the brief's team-content slot (4.5.13) to carrying recognitions or not.

## 7. The team read

**White paper.** The close captures, the lead reviews, the process is updated (WP p. 13). Post-mortems are process-focused (WP p. 17). Leading indicators are read regularly (WP p. 22).

**The book.** Feedback about the team as a whole can be data-driven, honest, and public: how the quarter or project went, what was learned, what to change; that builds a learning culture (pp. 396 to 397).

**From the earlier work.** A short, evidence-based read on whether a unit's stated goal was met, one sentence with the reasoning, from a record, never a lead's narrative of the team's character, and never a read of a person in a room (86akht2k9). Anything reaching the wider team was a record first (17tn048qc3y, with 2.2.11). The failure-typed process review (5.2.9, built in 5.3.15) is the team read's usual source.

**Options for 5.3.9.** (a) One sentence with reasoning, from a record, at the brief, the leads' review, and the house review. (b) The book's fuller form at the house review only: what happened, what was learned, what changes, as a short written memo (2.3.23). (c) Both: the sentence at the brief, the memo at the house review.

## 8. Testing a change with its users first

**White paper.** Employees are the users; systems are designed for the people who use them, with continuous feedback loops so the tools evolve on real usage (WP p. 17, stated outright).

**The book.** Stripe's surviving practice: test content with a small group before a wide send; the cofounders do it most, which sets the example and signals respect for others' time (pp. 395 to 396).

**Not in the earlier work.** This is new here, and it is the book's practice that most directly matches the white paper's own principle. A change to a brief's content, a module, a page, or a process tried with three of the people who will use it, for a service or two, before it travels the change channel (2.2.11).

**Options for 5.3.10.** (a) Every change of a named kind is tried first; the founders bind themselves first; a tester's word can send it back but not kill it. (b) Only training modules and the brief's fixed content are tried first; everything else travels directly. (c) Not adopted; the channel's feedback loop after the fact is enough.

## 9. Reading the culture

**White paper.** Employee NPS tracked regularly (WP p. 22); engagement across psychological safety and values alignment; exit interviews read for what could have been better (WP p. 23).

**The book.** Examine the feedback culture; if it is weak, the review feels destabilizing because people do not know where they stand (p. 396).

**Candidate signals.** A pulse question on knowing where you stand; one on whether you could raise a concern about your lead; the channel's volume and closure rate (2.3.16); the count of "something new" marks at reviews (5.4); the recognition platform's use in aggregate. None read per person. 4.5.25 decides how psychological safety is read; this adds the feedback-specific questions beside it.

## 10. How others have handled it

General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Danny Meyer's *Setting the Table* describes "constant, gentle pressure" as the manager's daily correction of small things, which is closer to 5.2.6's operating instruction than to feedback about a person.
- Pre-shift lineups in fine-dining rooms commonly carry a short read of the last service from the numbers and the notes, which is the team read in practice; the rooms that do it well keep names out of it.
- Netflix, per *No Rules Rules*, asks for feedback in the open and treats "only say about someone what you would say to them" as the rule; that is the book's culture and the far end of the peer-correction options.
- Bridgewater's recorded meetings and public ratings are the counterexample: candor as a performed norm, which many people experience as the brutal environment the Hallmark line warns about.
- Software teams that "dogfood" a change with a small group before rollout are the general form of Stripe's test-first practice.
