# 5.3 Creating a culture of informal feedback: working session

## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** The everyday flow: how observations move between people at Sŏn outside the check-in and the review. The book says companies neglect this and then wonder why the review feels like a verdict (p. 396). The white paper promises feedback in both directions and a channel that closes the loop (WP pp. 19, 22), and uses one word for four different things. This session names the kinds, gives each a destination, decides what peers and leads may say to whom, and settles what the founders do first to make asking normal.

**Already decided upstream.** The channels a change travels (2.2.11); the check-in's upward question (2.3.9); the pulse (2.3.15); the channel's windows and closing states (2.3.16); rounds in meetings (4.5.6); correcting a person in front of others (4.5.8); the unblocking path (4.5.10); each destination for what a person needs to say (4.5.11); the brief's team-content slot (4.5.13); the moments the house marks (4.5.23); psychological safety's read (4.5.25); live-service speech and the timing of hard feedback (5.2.6, 5.2.7); the failure types (5.2.9).

**Who else must agree.** Both seated founders throughout. The chef partner where the kitchen's team read and peer rule differ. Counsel on whether an upward concern read by a partner is a complaint the house must handle formally (cons. 11).

**Why it sits here.** 5.1 and 5.2 are what a lead says to a person. This is everything else: upward, sideways, and to the room. 5.4 (the formal review) is only "nothing new" if this flow works.

## 3. Briefs

### 5.3.2 Decide the kinds of feedback Sŏn names, and the word and destination for each

**The question:** the white paper says "feedback" for a broken tool, a complaint about the schedule, a view of one's manager, and an observation to a person about their work. Does Sŏn keep one word, or four, and which may travel on the channel?

**Why it matters now:** the destinations page (4.5.17) is being written and the channel (2.3.16) configured. If "feedback" about a person can go on the channel, the person page has a back door.

**Openers:**
- A server types into the channel: "the new runner keeps missing the sequence." Is that a friction report, a read of a person, or both? What does the channel's owner do with it?
- Sideways: think of the last workplace where "I have some feedback" made your stomach drop. What did the word mean there? What would you have needed it to mean?
- If you had to give each kind its own name in floor language, what would the four be?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Four kinds with their own names, one word sorted by destination, or three?
- Nothing about a named person on the channel, ever?
- Which of 4.5.11's destinations does each kind use?

**What the book says:** informal feedback is the one-off advice colleagues offer each other in day-to-day work, distinct from the review (p. 395); feedback on work product and feedback to a person differ (p. 396).

**White-paper default:** systems evolve on feedback from their users (WP p. 17); the team weighs in on management, operations, and culture (WP p. 19); a structured channel closes the loop (WP p. 22). One word throughout.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies with a suggestion or friction channel almost always exclude complaints about named people from it and route those to a person; the sorting is usually done by the reader after the fact, which is what naming the kinds avoids.

**Options:** (a) four kinds, "feedback" reserved for observations to a person; (b) one word, sorted by the destinations page; (c) three kinds. Depth: cons. 1.

**Watch for:** a vocabulary so fussy nobody uses it; a channel that quietly becomes where people talk about each other.

**A finished answer:** the kinds in Brandon's words, each with a destination, and the channel rule stated.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.3.3 Decide whether Sŏn states an expectation of candor, or lets the mechanisms carry it

**The question:** does the house tell people "speak up," or does it build the destinations, have the founders ask, and let that be the expectation?

**Why it matters now:** orientation (3.3.35) and the honest description (2.1.19) are being written. Whatever is said there about speaking up is what a new hire hears first.

**Openers:**
- The Hallmark line: you cannot be brutally honest in a brutal culture (p. 396). What in the building makes honesty safe, concretely? Name three mechanisms. Is that enough, or does someone still have to say the words?
- Sideways: a twenty-two-year-old server six months into the industry (WP p. 17). Nobody has told them they may say the brief was confusing. Will they? What would make them?
- The earlier work's thesis: candor as a property of a system, not a disposition asked of anyone. Do you believe that, or is it an elegant way to avoid asking?

**Narrowing questions:**
- No stated expectation, one sentence in orientation, or a principle?
- If a sentence, what exactly, and where does it appear?

**What the book says:** leaders steer the change; kindness gets confused with silence; an executive solicits feedback in front of the team to model it (pp. 396, 398).

**White-paper default:** mechanisms are stated; no disposition is asked for (WP pp. 17, 19, 22).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Netflix's culture memo states the expectation outright; Bridgewater enforces it; most hospitality groups say nothing and rely on the manager. Sŏn's mechanisms-first leaning is unusual (cons. 10).

**Options:** (a) nothing stated; (b) one sentence in orientation and the honest description; (c) a principle, tested by 5.1.4. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** a stated expectation that the first hard week proves false; no statement, and a floor that never learns it may speak.

**A finished answer:** one of the three, the sentence if any, and where it lives.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.3.4 Decide how a person gives feedback to their own lead, and where it goes when the check-in is not safe

**The question:** the check-in's upward question is asked by the lead. Where does a person go with a concern about that lead?

**Why it matters now:** 4.5.11 named this as a possible hole. The two leads start in hiring and training; the hole is open from their first week.

**Openers:**
- A runner's problem is the Maitre d. The runner's check-in is with the Maitre d. Walk me through the runner's Tuesday.
- Sideways: in your career, where did you take a concern about your own boss? Did it work? What happened to you afterward?
- Four candidate destinations: the other lead, the partner who holds that lead's monthly conversation, a named founder, the pulse's free field. Which would you have used at twenty-two?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The upward question confirmed as the first surface, in the person's words, written only if they choose?
- Which second destination, who reads it, what they do, how the person learns it was heard?
- Same for a concern about a founder?
- Interpreter rule by reference to 4.1.23 and 4.8?

**What the book says:** upward feedback without being asked is what companies do least well; ask on different occasions and forums (pp. 396 to 397).

**White-paper default:** team members weigh in on management effectiveness (WP p. 19, stated outright). No destination for a concern about one's own lead.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Skip-level conversations are the common answer in larger companies; in a two-lead house the equivalent is the partner who holds that lead's monthly conversation.

**Options:** (a) the other lead; (b) the partner holding the lead's monthly conversation; (c) a named founder for all; (d) the pulse's free field. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** a destination that exists on paper and that nobody would use; counsel's view on when such a concern becomes a complaint the house must act on (cons. 11).

**A finished answer:** the first surface confirmed, the second destination with reader, action, and closure, the founder case, and the interpreter reference.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.3.5 Decide how leads and founders ask for feedback, and what they do with it

**The question:** the book's five pointers for asking, translated into what a lead at Sŏn does in a check-in, after a new brief format, and at the leads' review.

**Why it matters now:** the founders will ask the two leads first (5.3.19). What they do with the answer is the norm the leads copy.

**Openers:**
- "How did the brief go?" versus "what could I have done differently in that brief?" Say both to a lead. Which one gets an answer you can use?
- Sideways: someone gives you feedback and you explain why you did it that way. What did they learn about giving you feedback again?
- The book says mention feedback you received at the next team meeting (p. 397). The earlier work refused that for anything about a person and kept "here is what we changed." Where is the line for you?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Where and how often a lead asks?
- The specific form of the ask?
- No explaining; repeat back?
- Mention at a gathering: never, general only, or as the book says?
- Follow-up at the next check-in, and through the channel's closing states?

**What the book says:** the five pointers (pp. 397 to 398); soliciting matters most when a relationship is new (p. 397).

**White-paper default:** feedback runs both directions (WP p. 19). Nothing on how a lead asks.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. "Thank, don't explain" and "repeat it back" are standard in feedback training; the fixed ask after a new format is the book's own example.

**Options:** (a) the earlier work's translation; (b) the book's five as written, no person named; (c) (a) plus a fixed ask after every new format or rule. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** asking that is a ritual with no follow-up, which the white paper names as the source of cynicism (WP p. 22).

**A finished answer:** the five practices in house words, ready for the card (5.3.13).

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.3.6 Decide the bounds on peer-to-peer feedback

**The question:** does the house ask colleagues to tell each other when something is off, leave it to them with limits, or route everything through a non-peer destination?

**Why it matters now:** the module teaches whatever this is; the destinations page says it. And it is where Sŏn most openly diverges from the book.

**Openers:**
- A server watches a peer handle a table badly. Between shifts, does the house want them to say something to that peer? If yes, in what words? If no, where does it go?
- Sideways: the book's Stripe culture, where people comment on each other's work freely, was "almost too strong" and intimidating to new people (pp. 395 to 396). On a floor of thirty, what does "too strong" look like?
- The earlier work's three refusals: never where others can hear, never written, never an expected norm. Which of the three would you drop?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Ask peers to, leave it to them with the three refusals, or route everything?
- During service, operating instruction inside task authority only (5.2.6)?
- Where does a peer take something hard about a peer?

**What the book says:** speaking up constructively without being asked marks a strong culture; leaders model it (p. 396).

**White-paper default:** peer recognition on a platform (WP p. 19). Silent on correction.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Netflix asks it of everyone; most restaurants leave it unmanaged and get a grapevine; the earlier work's position is a deliberate third way (cons. 5).

**Options:** (a) the three refusals only; (b) the book's culture, peers asked and leads modeling; (c) peers told they may, taught the shape, never asked. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** "you may" heard as "you should"; a refusal so total that a peer with a real safety concern stays silent.

**A finished answer:** one of the three, the service rule confirmed, and the peer's destination for something hard.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.3.7 Decide how praise works in public: peer recognition, and whether a lead praises a person publicly

**The question:** the book says praise publicly. The white paper puts public praise on a peer platform. May a lead ever praise a named person from the chair?

**Why it matters now:** the brief's team-content slot (4.5.13) needs to know whether it carries recognitions; the moments the house marks (4.5.23) may already include some.

**Openers:**
- A manager singles someone out in pre-shift: "great night, Ana." What does Ana hear? What does everyone else hear?
- Sideways: the white paper's reason for peer recognition is that it comes from people who understand the work (WP p. 19). Does a lead's praise come from somewhere else? Where?
- Should a recognition ever be counted for anything? The count of who gets recognized most is a tempting number.

**Narrowing questions:**
- A recognition is a peer's own words about a specific act?
- Read where: aloud at a gathering, on the platform only, or in the brief's slot?
- A lead: never from the chair, as a peer through the platform, or allowed for a specific act?
- Counts read only in aggregate, never for a person?

**What the book says:** praise publicly, criticize privately (p. 396).

**White-paper default:** peer recognition through a platform, in the moment, landing differently than management praise (WP p. 19, stated outright).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Peer-recognition tools are common; rooms that read recognitions aloud at lineup report it works when the words are the peer's and fails when it becomes a manager's list.

**Options:** (a) platform only, no lead praises a named person in a group; (b) leads may, for a specific act, same rules as a recognition; (c) leads praise through the platform as peers. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** a lead's praise becoming the altitude the web is built not to have (WP p. 9); recognitions becoming a popularity count.

**A finished answer:** what a recognition is, where it is read, the lead rule, and the count rule.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.3.8 Decide what a recognition that is really a correction gets, and who tells its writer

**The question:** someone writes "shout out to Marco for finally getting the sequence right." It is a correction wearing a compliment. What happens to it, and what does Marco's colleague hear?

**Why it matters now:** the platform's page (5.3.14) needs the rule before the first recognition is written.

**Openers:**
- Read that sentence aloud as Marco. Now as the writer, who meant well. What does each need?
- Sideways: the same person's third declined recognition. Is the problem the person, or the prompt on the screen that invited it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Not read; the reader tells the writer privately in two sentences; nothing written about the writer; stays as unread?
- A pattern of declines is first a question about the prompt?

**What the book says:** praise publicly, criticize privately (p. 396); the rest is the earlier work's.

**White-paper default:** the platform exists (WP p. 19). No rules.

**How others have handled it:** Nothing useful beyond the general observation that unmoderated recognition tools drift toward in-jokes and backhanded compliments.

**Options:** (a) the rule as written; (b) the rule, with the writer offered a chance to rewrite. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** the decline conversation becoming its own correction of the writer.

**A finished answer:** the rule accepted or changed, and the two sentences in house words.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.3.9 Decide how a team's performance is read publicly: the service, the period, the project

**The question:** the book allows honest public feedback about the team as a whole, from data. What does that sound like at the brief, the leads' review, and the house review, and what may it never carry?

**Why it matters now:** the brief and close checklists (2.3.20) and the house review memo (2.3.23) are being written; the failure-typed process review (5.3.15) will be their usual source.

**Openers:**
- Pre-shift tomorrow. Last night the sequence broke at nine twice and the kitchen refired. What does the room hear, in one sentence? Who is in that sentence?
- Sideways: the best debrief you ever heard in a restaurant. What did it name, and what did it leave out?
- A lead wants to say "the floor was flat last night." Is that a read from a record, or a narrative of the team's character?

**Narrowing questions:**
- One sentence with reasoning, from a record, at all three?
- Or the book's fuller memo at the house review only, or both?
- Never a person, never a narrative, record first (2.2.11)?

**What the book says:** feedback about the team can be data-driven, honest, and public: how it went, what was learned, what to change (pp. 396 to 397).

**White-paper default:** the close captures, the process is updated (WP p. 13); post-mortems are process-focused (WP p. 17).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Lineups that read the last service from the numbers and the notes are common in fine dining; the ones that work keep names out (cons. 10).

**Options:** (a) the sentence at all three; (b) the memo at the house review only; (c) both. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** "the team" as a way of reading one person everyone can identify.

**A finished answer:** the form, the three places, and the refusals, ready for 5.3.16.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.3.10 Decide whether a change is tested with the people who use it before it reaches the whole team

**The question:** before a new brief format, module, page, or process travels the change channel, is it tried with a few of the people who will use it?

**Why it matters now:** the change channel (2.2.11) and the communications policy (2.2.12) are being written; this is the one book practice that most directly matches the white paper's "employees are the users" (WP p. 17), and it is not in the earlier work.

**Openers:**
- You have rebuilt the shift brief. Who sees it before the whole floor does? For how long? Can they send it back?
- Sideways: Stripe's cofounders were the heaviest users of test-first, and that set the example (pp. 395 to 396). Would you bind yourself to it for your own changes, first?
- The white paper's one-star app (WP p. 17): the developers say it works fine for them. What is the smallest step that keeps Sŏn from being those developers?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Which kinds of change, how many people, how long?
- The founders first?
- A tester's word sends it back, or stops it?

**What the book says:** test with a small group before a wide send; the leaders do it most (pp. 395 to 396).

**White-paper default:** systems are designed for the people who use them, with continuous feedback loops (WP p. 17, stated outright).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Software teams try a change with a small group before rollout as a matter of course; restaurants rarely do, and it costs a service or two.

**Options:** (a) every change of a named kind, founders first, a tester can send back; (b) modules and the brief's fixed content only; (c) not adopted. Depth: cons. 8.

**Watch for:** a test step that becomes a veto and stalls the build; a test step skipped the first busy week.

**A finished answer:** adopted or not, the kinds, the size and length, the founders' binding, and what a tester's word does.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.3.11 Decide how the feedback culture is read, and when its baseline is set

**The question:** what tells the founders, without naming anyone, whether people know where they stand and whether they could raise a concern about their lead?

**Why it matters now:** the pulse (2.3.15) is being written and the metric register (2.2.25) filled; a baseline set at the first house review needs the questions in before opening.

**Openers:**
- Two questions on the pulse: "I know where I stand" and "I could raise a concern about my lead and it would be heard." Would you answer honestly on a phone in the break room? What would make you?
- Sideways: the book says the sign of a weak feedback culture is a review that surprises (p. 396). What number would show you that before the first reviews?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Which signals: the two pulse questions, the channel's volume and closure, the "something new" count from 5.4, the platform's use?
- Where each is read; baseline at the first house review?
- None per person?

**What the book says:** examine the feedback culture (p. 396).

**White-paper default:** employee NPS regularly; engagement across psychological safety and values alignment (WP pp. 22, 23).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Engagement surveys almost always carry a "my manager gives me useful feedback" item; a "could raise a concern about my manager" item is rarer and more telling.

**Options:** the candidate signal set as written, more, or fewer. Depth: cons. 9.

**Watch for:** a signal that can be traced to a person on a small floor.

**A finished answer:** the signals, their homes, the baseline date, and the per-person refusal.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
