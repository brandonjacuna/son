# 4.3 Team changes and restructuring: working session

## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** What happens after a diagnosis: what is allowed to trigger a change to the structure, in what order fixes are made, how fast a change moves and who hears it when, what is held still during dinner's first quarter, whether the structure ever bends for one person, and how a departure is read and refilled. The book's section (pp. 286 to 294) is about reorganizations: two legitimate triggers, a misused third, three cautions, and a three-phase process. Sŏn has no org chart in the book's sense (WP p. 09), so most of this chunk is about translating "reorg" into the events Sŏn actually has: a period chartered, a line added, a person leaving.

**Already decided upstream.** How a structure change is made and reaches people, and the change entry (4.1.12, 4.1.34); when the structure is reexamined (4.1.33); how a lead's load is relieved and when a line is added (4.1.36); designations and the rotation floor (4.1.20); the internal-succession rule (3.1.40); the internal loop (3.2.44); the shorter plan for a domain change and a change of lead (3.3.29, 3.3.30); what the team is told when a hire is reversed (3.4.3); the channels (2.2.11); what the team hears before and after a decision (1.2.2); the reset (2.3.30). From 4.2 if decided: the read order and the coupling to change (4.2.2, 4.2.11).

**Who else must agree.** Dominic on every founder-level rule. The chef partner on the kitchen's part of the departure read and change plan. Counsel on what is said about a departure and what is recorded (4.3.14).

**Why it sits here.** 4.2 decided how the house reads itself; this chunk decides what the house may do about it, and how. 4.4 follows with what a lead does inside a domain once it is settled: knowing people, delegating. 5.10 holds the exit itself; this chunk holds what the house reads around it.

## 3. Briefs

### 4.3.2 Decide what triggers a structure change at Sŏn, and what never does

**The question:** what events are allowed to change the shape of the house, and which events that feel like they should are not?

**Why it matters now:** candidates will be told the structure will change and when (4.1.33); leads need to know what they may propose. The book's warning is that a struggling team invites a reorg that hides the real problem; the white paper's whole argument is that the two leads stay separate.

**Openers:**
- Month five. The floor is fine, the kitchen is a mess, and the chef partner is stretched. Someone says: put the Operations Lead over the kitchen's ordering for a while. Is that a structure change? Is it allowed?
- Sideways: Coqodaq added lunch and brunch eighteen months in and both stayed flat (WP p. 02). What was the structural decision that was never made there? Would Sŏn's trigger rule have caught it?
- Name an event you can imagine that would make you want to merge the two leads into one. Now say why the white paper says never.

**Narrowing questions:**
- Triggers in Sŏn's terms: a period chartered or closed, a line under 4.1.36, a lead's departure, a repeat finding that resolves to structure. Which stay?
- Non-triggers: a domain's underperformance before its mode is read; one person's departure short of a lead; a retention threat. Which stay?
- Merge two domains under one lead: never, or under a stated emergency?
- Split a domain outside 4.1.36: ever?

**What the book says:** two legitimate triggers, structure no longer matching strategy and a talent change; a misused third, underperformance; reorgs are not a catchall; restructuring can signal growth (pp. 288 to 290).

**White-paper default:** the two leads are parallel; bundling them is the load-bearing model's final failure mode (WP p. 10, stated outright). Growth is outward (WP p. 09) and comes as periods, each on a test (WP p. 36).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Restaurants adding a period usually stretch dinner's leads across it; the white paper's own case says that is the error, so the trigger here is the charter, not the opening.

**Options:** (a) Sŏn's triggers only, no merges, no splits outside 4.1.36; (b) the book's triggers, read by the founders at each reset; (c) (a) plus a stated emergency trigger with a named caller. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** an "emergency" trigger wide enough to be the third trigger in disguise; a trigger list that forgets the kitchen; treating a lead's departure as a rethink of the domain rather than a refill.

**A finished answer:** the trigger list, the non-trigger list, the merge and split answers, each in a sentence Brandon could say to a lead who proposes a change.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.3.3 Decide the sequencing rule after a diagnosis: which fix goes first

**The question:** when a domain has more than one thing wrong, what gets fixed first, and does a people change ever go before a structure fix?

**Why it matters now:** the first reset will arrive with a list (4.3.20); without an order, the loudest problem wins, which is usually the person.

**Openers:**
- The first reset. The list says: the close is not being written on Fridays, the Maitre d is covering the door twice a week, and one server has gone quiet. In what order, and why?
- Sideways: when you have fixed a room for someone else, what did you always do first, before you touched anyone's job?
- The book asks: new leaders first, or vision first? Translate that into Sŏn's terms. Is there a version of "vision first" here at all?

**Narrowing questions:**
- A fixed order among the modes (sequence corruption, node overload, skill invisibility and cooling failure, tension slack), or an effort-against-impact read at the reset, or case by case with the reason on the entry?
- Does a people change ever precede a structure fix in the same domain?
- Who holds the order when the leads disagree (2.2.3)?

**What the book says:** sequence deliberately; sometimes both leaders and vision are needed and the order matters; an effort-impact matrix helps; manage expectations while it runs (pp. 287 to 288).

**White-paper default:** the stack is the nervous system and a degraded one cannot feel the rest (WP p. 12); sequence corruption's fix is system maintenance (WP p. 14). No stated order.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Incident practice fixes the monitoring gap before the underlying fault when both are found, on the reasoning that a fix you cannot observe cannot be confirmed; that is the earlier work's "sequence corruption first."

**Options:** (a) a fixed order among the modes; (b) effort against impact at the reset; (c) case by case, reason recorded. Depth: cons. 1.

**Watch for:** an order so fixed that a real safety or dignity problem waits behind a record fix; "case by case" that means no rule.

**A finished answer:** the rule in one sentence, the people-before-structure question answered, and the holder named.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.3.4 Decide the pace and the order of telling once a change is in motion

**The question:** from the moment a change is proposed, how fast does it move, who knows at each step, and what does the affected person hear before anyone else?

**Why it matters now:** the first changes will happen at the first reset and every departure is a small one; a floor that hears before the person does is the failure the book calls the ice cream on the counter.

**Openers:**
- A seat is being moved from the floor's inward strand to beverage. Walk it: who says it first, where, and when does the person whose seat it is hear?
- Sideways: Phillips's subway announcer (pp. 297 to 298) tells the train plainly that it is changing route and lets people choose to stay. Is there a Sŏn version of that sentence, and who says it?
- The book says two or three conversations for each affected person, more for those who do not come out ahead. Is the check-in that conversation, or is it something else?
- What is the worst way a change has reached a floor you were on?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Window from proposal to decision, in reviews?
- Who knows at each stage: partners, leads' review, the affected person, the floor?
- The person hears it at their own check-in before any record or brief: accepted?
- How many conversations is the person owed?
- The floor hears through what: the brief, a gathering, the team home?
- What counts as a process failure?

**What the book says:** decide slowly, then move fast; design with a very small group; two or three conversations per affected person, listen then be firm, do not rethink the plan for one reaction; tell the most affected first, then everyone within a day (pp. 290 to 294).

**White-paper default:** no surprises (WP p. 19). Silent on telling a change.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies that handle restructuring well tell the directly affected person one-to-one before any group and compress the gap to a day or two; leaks live in the gap.

**Options:** (a) the earlier work's order (leads' review, the person's check-in, the record, the brief) with a fixed window; (b) the book's three phases in miniature; (c) (a) for seats, (b) for a line or a lead. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** a design phase that excludes the leads in a house with two of them; the check-in asked to carry something 2.3.9 said it refuses; the floor hearing a change only when it breaks.

**A finished answer:** the window, the stage list, the person-first rule, the conversation count, the floor's channel, and the failure definition; 4.8 told.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.3.5 Decide what may change during dinner's first quarter, and what waits for the first reset

**The question:** while dinner runs alone for its first quarter, what is the house allowed to change about itself, and what is deliberately held still?

**Why it matters now:** the white paper promises the quarter (WP p. 36); the leads will arrive with fixes on day three. Without a rule, the hold is whoever pushes hardest.

**Openers:**
- Week two of dinner. The Operations Lead wants to move the brief fifteen minutes earlier and change the close's fields. Yes, no, or log it?
- Sideways: what did the first quarter at Coqodaq change about itself, and what should it have left alone?
- What kind of change would you break the hold for, without a second thought?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Held: structure, mechanisms, parameters? Allowed: seat description corrections, designation card fixes, safety or legal changes?
- Everything held is logged with a date?
- Who may break the hold, on what grounds?
- Does the same hold apply to each later period's first quarter?

**What the book says:** after frequent restructuring, delay for stability, especially if the contemplated structure is not itself a steady state (pp. 290 to 291).

**White-paper default:** dinner runs alone for at least a quarter so the heart is solid (WP p. 36, stated outright); 2.3.2 keeps the build's tempo out of the room.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Software teams use a change freeze around a launch and log deferred changes for the first retrospective; the log is what makes the freeze bearable.

**Options:** (a) a hold on structure, mechanisms, and parameters, with corrections and safety allowed and a dated log; (b) structure only; (c) no hold. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** a hold that stops a lead from fixing something that is hurting people; a log nobody owns; a hold that the build's public tempo (2.3.2) pressures.

**A finished answer:** the held classes, the allowed classes, the log, the breaker and grounds, and the later-period answer.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.3.6 Decide whether the structure is ever changed for one person

**The question:** can a seat be created, retitled, kept, or made permanent because of who would hold it?

**Why it matters now:** the pressure arrives with the first valued person who says they are leaving; the answer needs to exist before that conversation, not during it.

**Openers:**
- Your best server, the one whose regulars fill Thursdays, says another room offered them a "service lead" title. What do you say that night?
- Sideways: the book's professor's line, "is this the person you want to break your structure for," was about a senior executive. Does it read the same about a server, or is the floor different?
- The book allows a bridge: an interim seat while there are not yet enough leaders. Sŏn will have exactly that before opening. Is a bridge a break?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Never; the book's two exceptions with a written why; or a bridge rule only?
- Create, retitle, or make permanent from a designation for a person: any of them, ever?
- The pressure case: the pool, 5.5's pay path, or a written no?

**What the book says:** don't restructure around one person; two exceptions, a transparent bridge and a genuine high performer; the test is whether you can explain why (pp. 291 to 292).

**White-paper default:** designations are not permanent elevations (WP p. 10); progression is connections, not altitude (WP p. 11); titles scale-independent (WP p. 15). Silent on a retention threat.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Title-based counter-offers are widely reported to buy a short stay and cost the team's trust; many companies forbid them and route the case to pay or a real open seat.

**Options:** (a) never; (b) the two exceptions, written; (c) bridge only. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** a "bridge" with no end date; a high-performer exception that is really the retention case; an answer that leaves 5.5 as the only tool without telling 5.5.

**A finished answer:** the rule, the three verbs answered (create, retitle, make permanent), and the pressure case's path.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.3.7 Decide how a person moves seats or domains inside a change

**The question:** when a change moves a person, is that a placement or a hire?

**Why it matters now:** 3.2.44 built the internal loop and 3.3.29 the shorter plan; a change that simply reassigns people bypasses both, and a change that runs a loop for every move may take a month.

**Openers:**
- Beverage becomes its own line and two floor servers who have been pouring go with it. Do they interview for it?
- Sideways: you moved strands once yourself, somewhere. Were you asked, told, or offered? Which would you want here?
- Someone does not want the move. What are they owed?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Only through the internal loop and the shorter plan, or placed by the change, and in which cases?
- What a person who declines is owed?
- The move on the change entry, with the person's own words?

**What the book says:** moving people and merging or splitting groups are the mechanics of a reorg; explain the change (p. 294).

**White-paper default:** cross-training is career development (WP p. 19); leadership filled from within by people who have demonstrated the competency (WP p. 19). Silent on reassignment.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Where roles move with their work, people are usually moved with them without a new hiring process; where the role changes in substance, a posting is more common.

**Options:** (a) always the loop; (b) placed when the seat moves unchanged, the loop when the seat's substance changes; (c) placed by the change, with the person's consent recorded. Depth: cons. 3 and 5.

**Watch for:** a "placement" that is a promotion by another name; a loop run for a move nobody else could take.

**A finished answer:** the rule, the decline answer, and the entry's record.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.3.8 Decide the departure read: what is read in the week someone leaves, and when the read closes

**The question:** when someone leaves, what does the house read before it treats the seat as a vacancy, and how does it know the departure is over?

**Why it matters now:** the white paper says a departure's cost is bounded and calculable (WP p. 14) but nothing in it says how; the first departure will happen in training or the first quarter, and it is the first test of "the knowledge stays in the system."

**Openers:**
- The inward-strand server who quietly held the Friday sequence together gives notice. Day one: what do you open, in what order?
- Sideways: the white paper says a server's regulars may follow them (WP p. 25) and that the knowledge does not leave (WP p. 14). Both true? Which one is the departure read measuring?
- "Calculable" without money: what would you count?
- When is a departure finished?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The passes in order: what the seat held; who is compensating; the pool check; the capture check with each gap a finding?
- Closing: adjacent strain falls back after refill, read at the leads' review?
- The count: services of compensation, gaps found?
- The relationship account versus the knowledge account?

**What the book says:** a talent change is a legitimate trigger; the more senior, the wider the ripple (pp. 288 to 289).

**White-paper default:** tension loss, not slot vacancy; the knowledge lives in documented processes, captured preferences, skill maps; the pool surfaces who is furthest along; the cost is bounded, temporary, calculable (WP pp. 8 to 9, 14, stated outright).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Knowledge-transfer checklists at departure are common in engineering and rare in restaurants; the useful ones are short and ask what only the person knew.

**Options:** (a) the three-pass read owned by the domain lead; (b) pool check and coverage only; (c) the book's, assess and hire. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** a read that becomes a judgment of the person who left; a capture check nobody has time for in the week; "calculable" quietly becoming a cost figure.

**A finished answer:** the passes in order, the closing read, the counts, and the two-accounts question answered.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.3.9 Decide what the team hears when someone leaves

**The question:** what does the floor hear when a person is gone, and who says it?

**Why it matters now:** 3.4.3 answered it for the first weeks; the general case has to exist before the opening cohort's first real departure, and the answer sets a tone the floor will read.

**Openers:**
- Someone left last night. The brief is in ten minutes. Say the sentence.
- Sideways: the best departure you ever saw handled on a floor, and the worst. What did the room hear each time?
- A server's regulars ask for them on Friday. What does the host say?
- Does a lead's departure get the same sentence?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Coverage note only; coverage note plus a farewell the person chose; or the book's announcement with context?
- Who says it; is a reason ever given?
- A lead: same rule?
- The regulars: what are they told?
- 3.4.3's first-weeks rule: same, or an exception?

**What the book says:** tell the most affected first; announce broadly within a day; the leader's attitude spreads (pp. 293 to 294).

**White-paper default:** post-mortems are private (WP p. 17); belonging is to people (WP p. 25). Silent on the floor's note.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most employers' guidance is a factual statement that the person has left and who covers, with no reason; farewells are the person's own choice.

**Options:** (a) coverage note alone; (b) coverage note plus the person's chosen farewell; (c) the book's announcement. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** a rule so bare the floor reads it as erasure; a farewell rule that asks something of a person leaving badly; a reason given "just this once."

**A finished answer:** the sentence, the sayer, the reason rule, the lead answer, the regulars answer, and 3.4.3 reconciled; counsel's check queued (4.3.14).

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.3.10 Decide what a lead's departure adds: the handoff, the pool priority, and the record

**The question:** when one of the two leads leaves, what happens beyond the ordinary departure read?

**Why it matters now:** with two leads, one leaving is half the management layer; 3.1.40 says the pool is read first and an outside hire needs a note; the interim has to be named before it happens.

**Openers:**
- The Maitre d gives a month's notice in month seven. Who holds the floor's check-ins next month? Who holds them in month nine if no one internal is ready?
- Sideways: what should a paid handoff month actually contain, if the knowledge is supposed to be in the system already? What does that month prove about the system?
- The white paper says the building rarely hires a manager from outside (WP p. 19). At month seven, is that true yet?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The handoff: what it covers, how long, paid.
- The pool read for a lead seat; what the other lead reads meanwhile (4.1.3).
- The change entry's record.
- Who holds the domain until refilled: a founder, the other lead; for how long?
- A founder's departure: referred to 2.1, same records?

**What the book says:** a leader leaving ripples into strategy and cross-team relationships; run the phases in full (pp. 288 to 294).

**White-paper default:** internal succession is the goal (WP p. 19); the pool-first check and the outside-hire note (3.1.40); a principal's departure is the operating agreement's (2.1.1).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. A paid notice period with a written handoff is standard for managers; the interim holder is usually named the same day the departure is known.

**Options:** (a) the ordinary read plus a handoff block, a pool read, an entry, and a named interim with a date; (b) the book's full phases, treating the domain as reorganized; (c) the ordinary read only. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** an interim that becomes the merge 4.3.2 forbade; a handoff month that reveals the system held nothing, unrecorded as a finding; the founder case left to memory.

**A finished answer:** the handoff, the pool read, the entry, the interim and its limit, and the founder reference.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.3.11 Decide how an open seat is read before it is called a hiring problem, and who decides to cut coverage rather than lower the bar

**The question:** when a seat stays empty, what does the house check before it blames recruiting, and who may cut a service's coverage rather than hire someone who is not ready?

**Why it matters now:** the market is tight (WP p. 16) and the opening cohort will have gaps; the first instinct will be to fill, and the white paper's order is bandwidth first (WP p. 12).

**Openers:**
- The dish seat has been posted for six weeks. Before you call it a recruiting failure, what three things do you check?
- Sideways: the row says "able to lift fifty pounds, close five nights a week, start in two weeks." Who in Austin is that, and is the seat open because of the row?
- A lead holds a seat open rather than hire someone who is not ready, and the people around it are fraying. Who gets to say "cut a section on Tuesdays"?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The order: adjacent strain; double-covering; the row against the labor pool; then hiring quality (3.4.17)?
- The held-open case: coverage or hours cut elsewhere before the bar drops?
- Who cuts: the lead, the partners, the leads' review?

**What the book says:** talent gaps mean real work; sequence deliberately (p. 287).

**White-paper default:** bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results (WP p. 12, stated outright); the tight market (WP p. 16); speed is respect in hiring (WP p. 18).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Restaurants short a server close a section or cap covers rather than seat the full room; the question is only whether the instinct becomes a rule and whose call it is.

**Options for who cuts:** (a) the lead, recorded; (b) the partners on the leads' review's finding; (c) the leads' review jointly under 2.2.3. Depth: cons. 8.

**Watch for:** a coverage cut that lands on the other domain unannounced; "lower the bar" reframed as "adjust the row" without 3.1's process; a read that takes longer than the seat stays open.

**A finished answer:** the order, the held-open rule, and the cutter named.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.3.12 Decide how a domain or a service period that repeats is read, before any hiring question

**The question:** when the same domain keeps producing open seats or departures, or the same period keeps missing its metrics, what is asked first, and what is never concluded from it?

**Why it matters now:** this is the book's misused third trigger in Sŏn's terms; without a fixed order the answer becomes "the lead is the problem," which the white paper calls a design problem (WP p. 17).

**Openers:**
- Three departures from the inward strand in a quarter. What is the first question, and who is it about?
- Sideways: late night opens and stays flat for two months. Before anyone says "the late-night team," what do you read?
- Is there any repeat pattern that is legitimately about a lead's character? How would you know?

**Narrowing questions:**
- For a domain: seat count (a charter problem); the row; the lead's own load; then hiring quality?
- For a period: charter and interface test first (4.1.2, 2.2.36)?
- Repeated departures read as cooling failure first, never as the lead's character?

**What the book says:** underperformance is often misalignment with strategy, or a talent problem a reorg masks; address the real cause (pp. 289 to 290).

**White-paper default:** universal problems are design problems (WP p. 17, stated outright); a period without its own reason becomes a worse version of dinner (WP p. 34).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Manufacturing's root-cause habit asks "why" until the answer is a process, and stops early when the answer is a person; the discipline is the stopping rule.

**Options:** (a) the fixed questions in order for domains and periods; (b) the domain-repeat read only, periods left to 2.2's goal review; (c) the leads' review judges each case. Depth: cons. 8.

**Watch for:** a rule that protects a lead who is the problem, with no path to 5.9; "cooling failure first" becoming "cooling failure only."

**A finished answer:** the two orders, the never-concluded rule, and where the lead's own case goes (5.9) if the order exhausts itself.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.3.13 Decide how a designation held by one person every service for a quarter is read

**The question:** when the same person holds the room, the door, or the sequence every night for a quarter, what does that mean, and what does it never mean?

**Why it matters now:** 4.1.20 set the rotation floor; this is the read that catches the floor being quietly ignored, and the temptation to make the pattern a seat.

**Openers:**
- The same server has held the sequence every Friday and Saturday since opening. Why, in three possible answers?
- Sideways: at Alinea, who held the pass on the nights the chef was out, and was that written anywhere?
- Is there a version of this where the right answer is "make it a seat"? The white paper says no; do you?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The three branches: the lead's hours; only one person trained; preference (the rotation floor answers)?
- Never grounds for a seat; never a performance read on the holder?
- Read at the reset from the brief's designation block?

**What the book says:** a structure bent around one person costs the team's trust (pp. 291 to 292).

**White-paper default:** designations mark accountability in the moment and are not permanent elevations (WP p. 10, stated outright).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Nothing useful beyond the rotation practices already in 4.1.20.

**Options:** (a) the three branches at the reset; (b) weekly surfacing, answered at the leads' review; (c) no read. Depth: cons. 9.

**Watch for:** the "training gap" branch used to avoid the preference branch; the holder learning they were the subject of a read.

**A finished answer:** the branches, the two nevers, and where it runs.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
