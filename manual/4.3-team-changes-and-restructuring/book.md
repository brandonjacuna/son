# 4.3 Team changes and restructuring: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 4, pp. 286 to 293. Full text: `extraction/build/s09/book.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## After the diagnosis

Diagnosing the team (previous section) is only the first step. As with most challenges, about 10 percent of the work is the plan and 90 percent is executing it and making hard choices. If there are talent gaps in skill or will, there is real work ahead (p. 287).

Be smart about sequencing: do you need new leaders and to change out talent first, or do you need to build vision and inspiration first? Sometimes both are needed, and the order matters; an effort-impact matrix is a useful tool for thinking through next steps. Once a plan and sequence are set, manage expectations with stakeholders as the restructuring happens (pp. 287 to 288).

## Reorganizations

Some people treat any reorg as a sign of a problem. She argues restructuring can also signal high growth or a strategic shift, and normalizing that new arrangements will recur is part of leading through growth. That said, too much reorganizing has real costs; the aim is the right call, executed with minimal negative impact (p. 288).

Two legitimate triggers (pp. 288 to 289):

- **Structure no longer matches strategy.** A new product, or newly identified priority user segments, calls for a new team shape. This often coincides with year or fiscal-year planning.
- **A talent change.** A leader leaves, is let go, or changes roles, and their team needs new leadership, which can ripple into how the team's strategy and its relationships to other teams work. The more senior the manager, the bigger the reverberations.

A third trigger she flags as commonly misused: an underperforming team. If underperformance traces to misalignment with strategy, that is really the first trigger. But leaders sometimes reach for a reorg to mask a talent problem instead of addressing it directly, whether that means bringing in a new leader, changing how the team works, or building a shared vision. Reorgs should not be a catchall for a team's problems (pp. 289 to 290; more on underperforming teams at p. 320, and on leadership performance in Chapter 5).

## When to reorganize

Three cautions before executing (pp. 290 to 292):

- **Don't leave the ice cream on the counter.** Her business school professor's image: once an organizational change is out of the freezer, it can't be put back the same way. Take time to decide whether a reorg is needed, but once people beyond an HR partner and your own manager are involved, move fast or risk misinformation and instability.
- **Structure versus stability.** A reorg never feels perfectly timed. If you are aligned that it is needed, push through and accept some short-term instability for longer-term effectiveness, and do it quickly once people know. But if a company has been restructuring often, it may be right to delay for the sake of stability, particularly if the contemplated structure is not itself a steady state (for example, a leadership seat is still unfilled) and another reorg would likely follow soon.
- **Is this the person you want to break your structure for?** A caution against restructuring around a single person, whether out of attachment to them or a retention threat. Teams lose trust if they sense the structure serves one individual, and that person is set up to fail if the resulting structure does not match strategy. She allows two exceptions: bridging toward a future structure when there are not yet enough leaders to fill it (with transparency about the interim state), and a genuine high performer who could take on more; the test is whether you can explain why you are willing to break the structure for them.

## The three phases of a reorg

She sets out three phases (pp. 292 to 294):

**Phase 0, about one month: decide and design.** A very small group, your manager, HR, and maybe directly affected peers, examines the trigger (strategy or talent) and designs the new structure. Direct reports are not yet included. The phase ends with a design that accounts for every person and has buy-in from your manager and peers.

**Phase 1, one to two weeks: get buy-in.** The ice cream is now out of the freezer. Share the plan with each affected individual, focusing on the why: the vision and strategy behind the change, since these people will often need to explain it to others. Expect to need two or three conversations, more for people who do not see themselves coming out ahead; listen empathetically, then be firm about the decision, tying it where possible to the person's own development and career conversations. Avoid a full rethink of the plan because one person reacts badly at first. Once the key people agree, build the communications plan together.

**Phase 2, one to two days: communicate.** Tell the most affected people first, typically those getting a new manager, give them a heads-up and room for questions, then announce broadly within about a day. Move quickly into manager transitions and the new structure. Get the ice cream back in the freezer.

## Closing frame

Reorganizations are not inherently bad; needing one is often a sign of a growing, changing business. The association with distress usually comes from poor execution and poor explanation, not from the reorg itself. Treat it as an opportunity for people to step up, since a leader's own attitude toward the change spreads to the team (p. 294).
