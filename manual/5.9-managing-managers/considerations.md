# 5.9 Managing managers: considerations

What the white paper holds, what the earlier work argued, the options and what each commits Sŏn to, and where Sŏn may diverge from the book. The book's guidance is in `book.md`; the work is in `tasks.md`. "WP p. N" is the white paper, the only Sŏn context.

## 0. Who the "managers" are at Sŏn

The book assumes a manager of managers: someone above a layer of people who each have reports. The white paper builds no such layer. Two leads sit parallel, "co-leads of different systems serving the same center" (WP p. 10); management "is a systems function, not a rank" (WP p. 9); designations below them are "not permanent elevations above anyone" (WP p. 10). The people who manage the leads are the founders, and upstream chunks have already placed that relationship: 2.2.5 names who holds each lead's check-in and review; 2.3.14 names which partner holds each lead's monthly conversation, in the check-in's form, at a closed hour, on the lead's own page; 5.4.7 gives a lead's review to that partner on the same form as anyone's.

So this chunk is about the founders as managers of the two leads, and later of anyone who holds a lead's conversation: the chef partner with the kitchen's station leads (WP p. 11), and eventually a lead in a second property. The chunk carries the book's tactical shift into that relationship without building the layer the book assumes.

One vocabulary note. The white paper uses "manager" for the roles it replaces (WP pp. 10, 22) and for the function (WP pp. 9, 17), and twice for a Sŏn seat: the Maitre d is "the Hospitality Manager in full deployment" (WP p. 10), and "managers carry a nice spirit to pour tableside" (WP p. 26). Whether the word is used at Sŏn is 1.3.2's decision. Both uses are worth flagging to whoever maintains the white paper once 1.3.2 lands. This file says "lead" for the seat and "partner" for the founder who holds the lead's conversation.

## 1. The shift: captain to colonel

**Book.** Managing managers means leaving the weeds and building a system that keeps you involved enough to unblock and coach without doing everyone's job (pp. 449 to 450). The main goal is to be the manager's coach, sounding board, and unblocker.

**White paper.** Close to stated. "The mark of these founders is a company built to run without them" (WP p. 6). The Operations Lead "oversees those systems and is accountable to their outputs" (WP p. 10); the Maitre d is "accountable to experience quality and team health" (WP p. 10). The named failure mode is node overload, "usually a manager doing too much operational work because the parallel structure has blurred," fixed by "restoring the Operations Lead and Maitre d separation" (WP p. 14), and CPU failure, "the General Manager resolving scheduling conflicts" (WP p. 13). Nothing in the white paper has a founder directing a lead's domain.

**The particular risk at Sŏn.** Brandon has run rooms at the level the Maitre d will run this one (WP pp. 3 to 4). The book's captain is a founder who knows the floor better than the person leading it, and the drift from coaching to directing will be quiet and well-meant. The earlier work's worry was the mirror image: a partner becoming "a manager who assigns and reviews" by informal drift, which no single decision prevents and only a standing discipline does.

**Options for 5.9.2.**
- (a) The book's stance as written: the partner asks, unblocks, and supplies; the default reply to a brought problem is "what are you going to do?"; a direct answer only when the lead asks for one and says so. Commits Sŏn to letting a lead make a call the founder would have made differently, and to reading the result at the mechanism, not the person.
- (b) The stance with a named exception for dinner's first quarter, when the founders are in the room every night and the standard is not yet written down. Commits Sŏn to a dated end to the exception and to a structure change entry if it is extended.
- (c) The partner as domain owner one level up, directing where the domain touches their own. Commits Sŏn to the layer the white paper says it does not have, and to reopening 4.1.3 and 2.2.2.

## 2. How to think about it: coach the manager to their own solution

**Book.** "What are you going to do?"; the Five Whys; the meta line, "do you want me to tell you what I think the answer is, or do you want to tell me first?" (p. 450).

**White paper.** Silent on how a founder talks to a lead. "Blame the process, fix the process" (WP p. 17) means the Five Whys already have a home: the incident capture and the process update (WP p. 13). 5.1 built coaching by hypothesis for everyone; 5.1.7 gives the check-in's coaching words. The question here is only whether a lead's conversation uses the same words or the book's.

**Consideration.** The book's meta line works because it names the dynamic out loud. At Sŏn the equivalent honesty is that Brandon often will know the answer. A rule that pretends otherwise will not hold; a rule that says "the partner may answer when asked, and says so" might.

## 3. The 1:1 with a manager: the spine and the page

**Book.** Manager 1:1s move from technical to adaptive problems; Table 10 lists the manager's topics (how do I solve X, what to prioritize, progress on long-term goals, the right systems, are my instincts right, the right team and capabilities); the report proposes the agenda; every 1:1 opens with how the person is doing and the most important item first, even if it takes the whole meeting; a mutual shared document holds action items and links to goals and career notes (p. 451).

**White paper.** "Communication expectations" are on the candidate sheet (WP p. 18), so what a lead can expect monthly is a promise made at the first interview. "Feedback runs both directions" (WP p. 19). The white paper gives the lead's conversation no content, no length, and no document.

**Upstream.** 2.3.9 gave everyone's check-in a fixed spine (three questions and one upward question), fifteen minutes, on the clock, and a list of refusals: no verdict, no pay conversation, no task assignment, no discipline, no post-mortem. 2.3.14 placed the lead's version monthly at a closed hour. 4.4.11 may have put the delegation self-check inside it; 4.2.12 asks where a lead's "what's in my way" lands when the holder is a founder. 2.3.11 decides what a person page holds. 4.4.5 gives the career conversation a block.

**Considerations.**
- Table 10's questions are the lead's, not the partner's. If they become standing prompts, they belong on the lead's page where the lead proposes the agenda, not on a list the partner reads from.
- The book's "most important item first, even if it takes the whole meeting" is in tension with a fixed fifteen-minute check-in. A lead's conversation at a closed hour has room the floor check-in does not; whether it is longer is a real choice.
- The book's action-item document is the one part the earlier work rejected outright, on the ground that a running list of items the partner tracks turns a conversation into a report. The white paper's rule that knowledge lives in the system, not in a person (WP p. 8), cuts the other way: a lead's page that holds nothing is a memory-failure risk when the lead leaves (WP p. 13).
- The earlier work proposed one more line on the lead's page: a monthly "read on the center," in the lead's words, naming a mechanism of the founders' domain and its effect on the lead's domain, with the partner's questions beside it, aggregated at the partners' review as a count and a theme. That is one way the white paper's "team members weigh in on management effectiveness" (WP p. 19) reaches the founders about themselves without a survey. It refuses a founder's name as the subject of any sentence and any reading by the co-lead.

**Options for 5.9.3 and 5.9.4.**
- (a) The same spine as anyone's check-in, unchanged, with Table 10 as optional prompts the lead may bring. Commits Sŏn to one form everywhere and to the fifteen minutes.
- (b) The same opening two items, a longer block, the lead's prompts standing on the page, the lead proposing the agenda, the partner adding only suggestions, and one line a month in the lead's words plus the read-on-the-center line. Commits Sŏn to a lead's page that is richer than a person page and to rules about who reads it.
- (c) The book's form whole: a free agenda, a mutual action-item document, goals and career notes linked. Commits Sŏn to the partner tracking items, which is the drift risk in section 1.

## 4. Managing when you are not the expert

**Book.** Both sides must get value; without functional expertise the manager is a thinking partner on strategy, communication, problem-solving, and management; six other ways to add value (introductions, navigating the company, excitement, resources, unblocking, feedback via skip levels and observation); set expectations up front, in a "Working with Me" document or an early 1:1; her own: "I expect you are making most decisions without me," and she wants to know what is brewing (pp. 452 to 453).

**White paper.** Brandon owns "the concept, the operating systems, and the experience"; Dominic "the capital, the financial architecture, and the technology build" (WP p. 3). The Maitre d owns "the how of the experience" (WP p. 10), which sits inside Brandon's domain. The Operations Lead manages "the architecture of the business" (WP p. 10), which the white paper splits across both founders: the stack is Dominic's build, the operating system Brandon's. So each pairing 2.3.14 chose has a different expertise profile, and the Operations Lead's partner is the non-expert case whichever founder it is.

**Upstream.** 1.1.5 decides what the leads receive of the founders' working-with-me documents; 3.3.24 when a later lead writes their own; 3.3.23 whether a coach is engaged for a lead's first months; 4.4.9 what the founders hand each lead on arrival.

**Considerations.**
- Of the book's six ways, four translate cleanly (introductions to vendors, peers, and the build audience; navigating the founders' own domains; resources; unblocking across the seam). "Generating excitement" is a duty the earlier work declined as performed; the white paper's documented build (WP pp. 29 to 31) does some of it anyway. Feedback via skip levels is section 5.
- The book's expectation about decisions is nearly the white paper's "authority lives in the domain" (WP p. 7) said to a person. Where it is written matters: 1.1.5 governs the document; the first monthly conversation is the alternative.
- Where neither founder has the expertise a lead needs (the Operations Lead's compliance work, the Head of Beverage's program), the book's answer is to find it: an outside mentor or a colleague. That is a cost question, which is Dominic's to weigh; financials are not a source for this work.

## 5. The skip level, and observing the lead's team

**Book.** Skip levels and observing the report's team are how a non-expert manager gets useful feedback into a manager's performance conversations (p. 452).

**White paper.** The front line is "the most sensitive position" (WP p. 9); "team members weigh in on management effectiveness, operational improvements, and culture" (WP p. 19). No meeting between a founder and a lead's people is described.

**Upstream.** 5.4.13 rolls a domain's upward halves into the lead's review by mechanism, never by name; 5.3.4 and 4.5.11 name where a concern about one's own lead goes; 2.3.15 sets the pulse by domain; 4.7.12 has a lead's brief and review observed; 4.7.13 attaches environment reads to a lead's unit.

**The earlier work's position** was that a skip level is incompatible with a flat structure: a founder hearing a lead's people about the lead is a rank in practice. Its function was carried by the mechanisms above. The counter-consideration: those mechanisms are all mediated and aggregated. The book's point is that unmediated contact tells a manager things a roll-up cannot, and the white paper's own founder story rests on that (WP pp. 3 to 4).

**Options for 5.9.6.**
- (a) No skip level; the function is the upward halves, the pulse, the destinations, the observation, and the founder's own time in a seat (section 6). Commits Sŏn to trusting the roll-ups and to the founder-in-a-seat rule as the only unmediated read.
- (b) The book's skip level on a stated rhythm, the lead told first, nothing written about the lead. Commits Sŏn to a meeting the white paper does not describe and to a rule for what the founder does with what they hear.
- (c) A founder available to anyone by request, with what is said carried nowhere except as a question to the lead once. Commits Sŏn to the same rule as (b) without the rhythm.

## 6. Time on the ground

**Book.** Two hours with a risk analyst taught her more than any presentation; share the observations with the team's manager; Collison's "engineerication," three to five days working on a team (pp. 453 to 454).

**White paper.** Brandon "worked the floor" and ran opening service (WP pp. 3 to 4). A designation "marks accountability in the moment, in the domain" and is "not a permanent elevation" (WP p. 10). CPU failure is a founder-level person doing work beneath their capability (WP p. 13). Post-mortems are process-focused (WP p. 17).

**Upstream.** 4.1.19 and 4.1.20 set what a designation is and who holds the room; 4.4.12 names the three cases a lead does the work themselves; 2.2.5 logged the counsel question on whether a founder working a service under a designation is acting as an employee for any purpose; 2.3.8 fixes the shift close.

**The earlier work** landed this as ordinary floor presence with two rules: a founder in a seat is bound by the seat's rules like anyone, and what a founder observes is a capture at the close, never a judgment about a lead. The book adds a third thing the earlier work dropped: telling the lead what you saw afterwards, which she calls bonus points. Those can coexist if what is told is the process and the question, not a person.

**Considerations.**
- The founders will be in the room every night of dinner's first quarter regardless. The rule matters most after that, when presence becomes a choice and its absence a signal.
- A founder in a seat under a designation held by someone in the lead's domain is the sharpest test of section 1: the founder answers to a runner for the service.
- Whether a founder in a seat changes the founders' own employment classification is counsel's (2.2.5); it must be answered before the first such service, not after.

## 7. Beyond the book: what managing the leads needs that the section does not cover

The book's section is short because its structure carries the rest: HR, a ladder, a review process, and a manager's manager who can simply decide. Sŏn has none of those, so several things the earlier work built belong here.

**A concern about a lead (5.9.8).** The white paper says where nothing goes. Upstream has built pieces: the destination for a concern about one's own lead (5.3.4, 4.5.11); the candor count on a review holder (5.4.12), which 5.4 flagged as a supervisory instrument on leads that 5.9 should know exists; the read of a designation held by one person (4.3.13); a domain that repeatedly opens the performance process (4.3.12). The earlier work's position: no partner keeps a file about a lead; a repeated pattern (a one-sided hearing, a personal standard where no written one exists, a range breach) is tallied as a count and raised once, directly, at the lead's conversation, and read first as a missing mechanism. The reasoning was that a case file implies a rank and a paper trail toward termination. The counter-consideration is counsel's: 5.2.4 and 5.8.2 ask whether a record in the person's own words defends a later separation. Whatever 5.8.9 decided for everyone applies to a lead unless 5.9.9 says otherwise.

**A lead's own performance process (5.9.9).** The book's senior path (pp. 458 to 459) skips the formal plan and runs over months, framed on the gap between the role and what is delivered. 5.8 built a process for everyone, opened only by records. The choice is whether a lead gets the same process, the senior path, or a hybrid. The second reader when a lead is the subject was left by 2.2.5 and 2.3.14; the pauses that read the house's failure first have lead-specific forms (the seat load reading, the range 4.4.8 set, 4.1.35's relief order). The earlier work refused any comparison between the two leads' reviews, on the ground that there is nothing alike to compare.

**Keeping the two leads parallel (5.9.10).** The earlier work's six rules, minus the ones upstream now owns: each partner reads the lead they do not hold only through records (the leads' review record, the goal page, the seat load reading); a lead's shortfall is attributed first to what the house left unbuilt; a person informally holding the same responsibility every service becomes a rotation rule (4.3.13, 4.1.19); the chair of the leads' review rotates (2.3.13's). Two leads reaching visibly different conclusions on like situations was read as a calibration item fixed at the mechanism, never one lead correcting the other. The book's alternative is ordinary manager-to-manager alignment run by the manager above; at Sŏn that is the leads' review with the partners reading the record.

**A lead's development (5.9.11).** The book: teach them to steer the boat; find them the help you cannot give. The white paper: cross-training is career development, "a lead who understands both sides of the house leads better," and the aim is internal succession (WP p. 19). The earlier work argued no separate leadership track, no 360, no founder-led mentoring of a lead's character, because a bespoke track is a hierarchy in substance. The counter-consideration is the white paper's own line: a lead who understands both sides. Cross-training a lead into the other lead's domain is either the development path the white paper describes or the blurring 4.1.3 forbids.

**The kitchen (5.9.12).** The chef partner is a founding seat holding station leads (WP p. 11). Whether the same spine, count, and page apply in the kitchen is the chef partner's, pending until the seat is filled (2.1.10).

## 8. Where Sŏn may diverge from the book

- No layer. The book's colonel has captains beneath; Sŏn's partners hold one lead each and the leads hold no one above anyone. The tactical shift survives; the rank does not.
- No skip level, or a constrained one (section 5). The book treats it as a normal source.
- No action-item document, or a page with narrow rules (section 3).
- A lead's process is the same as everyone's, or the book's senior path; the book assumes the senior path.
- Time on the ground under a designation, answering to the room, rather than as a visiting executive.
- Excitement-generation declined as a duty.
