# 5.9 Managing managers: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 5, pp. 449 to 453. Full text: `extraction/build/s15/book.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## The shift managing managers requires

Managing individual contributors and managing managers share fundamentals, but moving to managers requires a major tactical adjustment (p. 449). Early in her own transition, she told a coach she couldn't keep track of her team's work anymore and didn't feel she was contributing meaningfully; her days had become a wall of meetings. His answer: "You have to move from being a captain to a colonel" (p. 449). That meant a mental shift away from being in the weeds with the team's work and toward a system that keeps her involved enough to unblock and coach, without doing everyone's job for them. It also meant moving from managing to leading: setting the division's vision and outlining goals and metrics, not tactically solving every technical problem (pp. 449 to 450).

The main goal when managing managers is to be their coach, sounding board, and unblocker so they can achieve their own goals and their team's. In practice, that means 1:1s oriented toward coaching rather than solving (p. 450).

## How to think about managing managers

When a manager brings a management problem, the instinct to say "here's how you solve it" should be replaced with "what are you going to do?" The aim is to coach managers through solving their own problems: don't just teach them to fish, teach them to steer the fishing boat. One tool: Toyota's Five Whys, asking "why" at least five times to reach the root of an issue. Most people already know the answer; the job is to ask the question, or, if they don't know, to help them identify the problem and then coach them to their own solution (p. 450). When someone is frustrated or she feels impatient with the process, she'll ask, "Do you want me to tell you what I think the answer is, or do you want to tell me first?", a line that gets meta about the dynamic and usually draws a laugh (p. 450).

## Individual 1:1s versus manager 1:1s

Managing managers shifts 1:1 time from solving technical problems ("I don't have enough time to get through my support tickets") to more adaptive ones ("I think we're building the wrong product"). Her table of example topics (p. 451):

| Individual contributor 1:1 | Manager 1:1 |
|---|---|
| Where is my time going? | How do I solve X? |
| What's your feedback on this document I wrote? | What work should I prioritize? |
| What is success in a year? | Are we making progress against our long-term goals? |
| | Do we have the right systems in place? |
| | Are my instincts right on X? |
| | Do I have the right team and capabilities in my organization? |

In both cases, don't skip asking how someone is doing. She treats the 1:1 as the other person's time: a mutual, shared doc tracks action items and links to goals and career notes, and she asks the individual to propose each agenda, only adding her own suggestions to it. Whether or not the other person proposes it, every 1:1 should open with two things: checking in on how someone is doing, and agreeing on the most important item to discuss and taking it first, even if it takes the whole meeting. That's better than a rushed conversation where the truly important thing surfaces in the last five minutes (p. 451).

## Managing when you're not the expert

Managing managers often means overseeing a function she hasn't done day to day herself. A healthy manager-report relationship requires both sides to feel they're getting value from it; when she isn't the functional expert, her value comes not from role-specific knowledge but from being a thinking partner on broader skills: strategy, communication, problem-solving, management. Self-awareness of one's own strengths (her Operating Principle 1) matters here: a manager strong at communicating hard decisions can lean on that (p. 452).

Other ways to add value without functional expertise (p. 452):

- Introducing the report to leaders or other parts of the organization
- Helping them navigate the company to get things done
- Generating excitement around what they're doing
- Getting them resources
- Unblocking them
- Providing useful feedback through skip levels, observing their team, and bringing broader context into performance conversations

If the expertise genuinely isn't there, the job is to find the report the help they need, whether an outside mentor or a colleague, and to set expectations up front about how the manager can add the most value. That can go in a "Working with Me" document or an early 1:1. Her own "Working with Claire" document says: she expects the report is making most decisions without her, and if they come to her she'll usually put it back on them ("what do you want to do?"), but she wants to know about big decisions brewing and is always available to talk them out (pp. 452 to 453).

Finally, spend time on the ground. Even without having worked the front line before becoming a manager, a manager can dedicate a few hours to the team's daily tasks. She describes spending two hours with a member of Stripe's risk operations team and learning more in that time than from any number of presentations; sharing the observations afterward with the team's manager earns bonus points. A more intensive version is what Stripe CEO Patrick Collison calls an "engineerication": blocking three to five days on the calendar, as if on vacation, to work as an engineer on a team, returning energized by what he's learned (pp. 453 to 454).
