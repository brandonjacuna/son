# 5.9 Managing managers: working session

## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** How the founders manage the two leads, and later anyone who holds a lead's conversation: the stance a partner takes in a lead's domain, what the monthly conversation is made of, what the lead's page holds, how a partner adds value where they are not the expert, whether a founder ever talks to a lead's people about the lead, a founder working a seat, what happens with a concern about a lead, how a lead's own performance process runs, how the two leads are kept parallel, and how a lead grows after onboarding. The book's section is five pages (pp. 449 to 454); most of the chunk is what the book leaves to a structure Sŏn does not have.

**Already decided upstream, so not reopened here.** Who holds each lead's check-in and review (2.2.5); which partner holds the monthly conversation, in the check-in's form, at a closed hour, on the lead's page (2.3.14); the check-in's spine and refusals (2.3.9); the leads' review's placement and chair (2.3.13); whether "manager" is a word the house uses (1.3.2); what the leads receive of the founders' working-with-me documents (1.1.5); a lead's onboarding and whether a coach is engaged (3.3.22, 3.3.23); the delegation rule and the handover list (4.4.8, 4.4.9); how the delegation signs are read (4.4.11); where a lead's "what's in my way" lands (4.2.12); how a lead's inclusiveness and environment are read (4.7.12, 4.7.13); who holds a lead's review and how a domain's upward halves roll up (5.4.7, 5.4.13); the candor count (5.4.12); counsel's list of what a lead must know (0.1). If any of these is still open, this session inherits the question and should say so at the start.

**Who else must agree.** Both seated founders on every decision. The chef partner on 5.9.12 and the kitchen half of 5.9.7; until the seat is filled, those are recorded as interim. The two leads, once hired, review 5.9.13 and can change it through 5.9.20.

**Why it sits here.** Chapter 5 has built the instruments for everyone: coaching (5.1), hard feedback (5.2), the review (5.4), pay (5.5), and the cases (5.6 to 5.8). This chunk applies them one level up, where the person being managed manages, and where the founders' own habits are the main risk. 5.10 follows with what happens when any of it ends.

## 2. Agenda

1. The stance (5.9.2): what a partner does in a lead's domain, and never does.
2. The conversation and the page (5.9.3, 5.9.4): the spine, the prompts, what is written, who reads it.
3. Value and expectations (5.9.5): each pairing's expertise, the six ways, what a lead is told to expect.
4. Hearing the lead's people (5.9.6, 5.9.7): the skip level or its substitutes; the founder in a seat.
5. When it is not going well (5.9.8, 5.9.9): a concern about a lead; a lead's own process.
6. Two leads, two partners (5.9.10, 5.9.11): reading the lead you do not hold; divergence; development.
7. The kitchen (5.9.12): what the chef partner will need to decide.
8. Deliverables and kits (5.9.13 to 5.9.19): what the session unlocks.

Start wherever he wants. If he has been on the floor with a lead recently, start with the stance; if a lead is being interviewed soon, start with 5.9.5, because that is what the candidate will ask.

## 3. Briefs

### 5.9.2 Decide the partner's stance toward a lead: what a partner does in a lead's domain, and what a partner never does

**The question:** when the Maitre d runs the floor differently from how Brandon would, what does Brandon do, and what does he never do?

**Why it matters now:** the two leads' candidate sheets name "their direct lead" (WP p. 18); the first interview will ask what that means. The white paper's named failure mode is a founder-level person absorbing a domain's work (WP pp. 13 to 14). Every later decision in this chunk assumes an answer here.

**Openers:**
- It is the third week of dinner. You watch the Maitre d handle a table you would have handled differently, and it goes fine. What do you say afterwards, if anything?
- The book's line for a manager who brings you a problem is "what are you going to do?" Say it out loud to an imagined Operations Lead asking about a vendor. Does it sound like coaching or like dodging?
- Sideways: she told a coach she could not keep track of her team's work anymore and felt she was not contributing. He said "you have to move from being a captain to a colonel" (p. 449). Where are you a captain right now, in the build, that you will have to stop being?
- You ran opening service at a room this ambitious. Name one thing you know about running a floor that the Maitre d might not. Now: how does that reach them without you running the floor?

**Narrowing questions:**
- List what a partner may do inside a lead's domain: ask, unblock, supply, hold the conversation, read the records, work a seat. Anything else?
- List what a partner never does: assign work to the lead's people, review a person in the lead's domain, change a process outside the change channel, answer before asking, decide inside the lead's range (4.4.8). Anything to remove?
- The default reply to a brought problem: the question, or an answer when asked for and said so?
- Is there an exception for dinner's first quarter, and if so, what ends it?

**What the book says:** the move is from being in the weeds to a system that keeps you close enough to unblock and coach; the main goal is to be the manager's coach, sounding board, and unblocker; "what are you going to do?" replaces "here is how"; the meta line names the dynamic and gets a laugh (pp. 449 to 450).

**White-paper default:** the company is "built to run without them" (WP p. 6); the leads are accountable to outputs and to team health (WP p. 10); node overload is fixed by restoring the separation, not by a person above (WP p. 14).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Her own coach's framing (captain to colonel) is the book's; she treats the shift as a learned discipline, not a trait (p. 449).
- Restaurant groups with a founder who came up on the floor commonly struggle with exactly this drift; the usual correction is the founder leaving the building on a schedule, which the white paper's design makes unnecessary if the stance holds.

**Options:** (a) the book's stance as written, the question as default, a direct answer when asked for and said so. Commits Sŏn to letting a lead make a call the founder would have made differently. (b) The stance with a dated first-quarter exception. Commits Sŏn to ending it. (c) The partner as domain owner one level up. Commits Sŏn to the layer the white paper says it does not have, and to reopening 4.1.3 and 2.2.2. Depth: cons. 1.

**Watch for:** a "never" list Brandon agrees to in principle and has already broken in the build; an exception without an end date; the stance stated for Dominic's pairing and assumed for Brandon's.

**A finished answer:** the two lists, the default reply, the first-quarter question answered either way, and one sentence about how Brandon's floor knowledge reaches the Maitre d.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.9.3 Decide the lead's monthly conversation's spine: how the lead is doing, the most important thing first, and the lead's questions

**The question:** is a lead's monthly conversation the same fifteen-minute check-in anyone gets, or something with more room, and what is in it?

**Why it matters now:** 2.3.14 placed it and 2.3.9 gave it a form; this is the last chance to say what is different about it before the guide (5.9.13) and the page (5.9.15) are built. "Communication expectations" are on the candidate sheet (WP p. 18).

**Openers:**
- Picture the Operations Lead's third monthly conversation. It is a closed hour. What is on the table?
- The book's manager brings questions like "are my instincts right on X" and "do I have the right team" (p. 451). Which of those do you want a lead to feel free to ask you, and which would you rather they never had to?
- Sideways: she says the worst version of a 1:1 is the rushed one where the important thing surfaces in the last five minutes (p. 451). Think of a conversation in the build that went that way. What would have put the important thing first?
- Who proposes the agenda: the lead, on the page, ahead of time? Or you?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Same spine and length as anyone's, or the same opening two items and a longer block?
- Which of Table 10's six questions become standing prompts on the page?
- Where do the delegation self-check (4.4.11) and "what's in my way" (4.2.12) sit?
- Is an action item ever written, and by whom? The book says yes; the earlier work said no.
- The refusals: the check-in's list (no verdict, no pay, no task, no post-mortem) plus no reading of the lead's people?

**What the book says:** manager 1:1s shift from technical to adaptive problems; the report proposes the agenda and the manager only adds; open with how the person is doing and the most important item first, even if it takes the whole meeting (p. 451).

**White-paper default:** none for content; the check-in's spine (2.3.9) and the monthly placement (2.3.14) are the nearest thing.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Her practice was a shared document, the report's agenda, and the two fixed openers (p. 451).
- Many operators run a manager's 1:1 longer than a line 1:1 and less often; monthly at a closed hour is already that shape.

**Options:** (a) the same spine, Table 10 optional. Commits Sŏn to one form and fifteen minutes. (b) The same two openers, a longer block, standing prompts, the lead's agenda, one line a month in the lead's words. Commits Sŏn to a richer page and rules for it (5.9.4). (c) The book's form whole, action items included. Commits Sŏn to the partner tracking items, which is the drift 5.9.2 guards against. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** prompts that are really the partner's questions dressed as the lead's; a "most important item first" rule that cannot survive a fifteen-minute block.

**A finished answer:** the spine named, the openers confirmed or declined, the prompts chosen, the agenda's author, the action-item question answered, the refusals listed.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.9.4 Decide what the lead's page holds, and who may read each part

**The question:** what does a lead's own page carry beyond a person page, and who can see each part?

**Why it matters now:** 5.9.15 builds it; 5.9.9 may put a window block on it; 4.3.10 puts a handoff block on it. The white paper's rule is that knowledge lives in the system (WP p. 8), and a lead's page that holds nothing is a memory failure waiting for the lead's departure (WP p. 13).

**Openers:**
- The Maitre d leaves after two years. What do you wish had been on their page?
- The earlier work proposed a monthly line in the lead's words naming one mechanism of the founders' domain and what it does to the lead's domain. Read that back. Is that a thing you want to receive every month, in writing, from a lead?
- Sideways: the book's shared document tracks action items and links to goals and career notes, and she calls it the report's document, not hers (p. 451). Whose document is the lead's page?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Beyond the person page: the monthly line, the prompts and proposed agenda, the goal link, the career block (4.4.5), the read-on-the-center line, the window block, the handoff block. Which stay?
- Who reads each: the lead and their partner; the other partner only through aggregate; never the co-lead; counsel by rule?
- What is never on it: a rating of a partner, a founder's name as subject, a survey form, anything a founder writes about the lead outside the read-back?
- Do the read-on-the-center lines aggregate at the partners' review as a count and theme (feeding 4.2.12)?

**What the book says:** a mutual, shared document; the other person's time and document (p. 451).

**White-paper default:** knowledge in the system, not the person (WP p. 8); "feedback runs both directions" (WP p. 19); 2.3.11 decides what a person page holds.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. A shared running 1:1 document is common practice in technology companies and is exactly what the book describes; hospitality rarely keeps one for a manager at all.

**Options:** (a) a person page and nothing more. Commits Sŏn to the conversation carrying everything. (b) A person page plus the lead's prompts, the monthly line, and the read-on-the-center line, with the reader rules above. Commits Sŏn to building and policing a richer page. (c) The book's document with action items. Commits Sŏn to the partner tracking items. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** a read-on-the-center line that becomes a rating of a founder by another name; a page the co-lead can see.

**A finished answer:** the parts kept, the reader for each, the never list, the aggregation question answered.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.9.5 Decide how a partner adds value where they are not the expert, and what each lead is told to expect

**The question:** for each pairing 2.3.14 chose, where is the partner not the expert, what do they bring instead, and what does the lead hear about decisions on day one?

**Why it matters now:** the first lead interview will ask "who do I answer to and what do they do for me." The Operations Lead's partner is the non-expert case whichever founder it is, because the seat's work sits across both domains (WP pp. 3, 10).

**Openers:**
- The Operations Lead brings a compliance question neither of you can answer. What happens next, and how long does it take?
- Her working-with-me document said: I expect you are making most decisions without me; if you come to me I will usually put it back to you; I want to know about big decisions brewing (pp. 452 to 453). Which of those three sentences is already yours, and which is not?
- Sideways: the book lists "generating excitement around what they are doing" as a way to add value (p. 452). Does that land as real, or as the thing the documented build already does for you?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Per pairing: expert or not, and in what?
- Of the six ways, which does each pairing use, in Sŏn's form?
- The decision expectation: written into the partner's working-with-me document under 1.1.5's rule, or said at the first monthly conversation?
- When neither founder has it: an outside mentor the house pays for (3.3.23), a peer lead elsewhere, the book's colleague?

**What the book says:** both sides must get value; the non-expert is a thinking partner on broader skills; six other ways; set expectations up front (pp. 452 to 453).

**White-paper default:** Brandon owns the experience, so the Maitre d's pairing is near-stated; the Operations Lead's is open (WP pp. 3, 10). The candidate sheet names the direct lead (WP p. 18).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Her own document is the book's example (pp. 452 to 453). Operators who pair a finance founder with an operations lead commonly buy the missing expertise as a retained advisor rather than hire it.

**Options:** (a) the six ways trimmed to four, excitement declined, the decision expectation in the working-with-me document. Commits Sŏn to 1.1.5's rule doing the work. (b) The same, said at the first conversation instead. Commits Sŏn to the guide (5.9.13) carrying it. (c) The book's list whole. Commits Sŏn to excitement as a duty. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** an expertise gap papered over with "Brandon knows restaurants"; the cost question decided here instead of by Dominic.

**A finished answer:** a line per pairing, the ways chosen, where the expectation is written, and the answer for the gap neither founder fills.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.9.6 Decide whether Sŏn has a skip level, or what carries its function

**The question:** does a founder ever talk to a lead's people about the lead's domain, and if not, what tells the founder what a skip level would?

**Why it matters now:** the roll-ups (5.4.13, 2.3.15) are built on the assumption that they are enough. If they are not, the first sign will be a lead's domain going wrong with no founder having heard it. The earlier work declined the skip level outright; that is now a choice.

**Openers:**
- A server tells you at the pass, quietly, that the Maitre d plays favorites on sections. What do you do with that in the next five minutes, and in the next month?
- Sideways: the book's manager learns from "skip levels, observing their team, and bringing broader context" (p. 452). The white paper says the front line feels things "before anyone else" (WP p. 9). How does what the front line feels reach you without you asking them about their lead?
- If you never hold a skip level, what would you be giving up that you have relied on in every room you have worked in?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Never, and the function is the upward halves, the pulse, the destinations, the observation, and your own time in a seat? Or the book's, on a rhythm, the lead told first? Or by request only?
- How does what you hear reach the lead: a question at the monthly conversation, once, after their account; never a count?
- What do you say to the person who asks you to keep it from their lead?

**What the book says:** skip levels and observing the team are how a non-expert manager gets useful feedback into a manager's performance conversations (p. 452).

**White-paper default:** management is a systems function, not a rank (WP p. 9); team members weigh in on management effectiveness (WP p. 19); no founder-to-team meeting is described.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Skip-level 1:1s are standard in technology companies and rare in restaurants, where the owner walking the floor does the same job informally and without rules.

**Options:** (a) no skip level; the mechanisms and the founder in a seat. Commits Sŏn to trusting roll-ups. (b) The book's, on a rhythm, the lead told first, nothing written. Commits Sŏn to a meeting the white paper does not have and a rule for what is heard. (c) By request only, carried nowhere but one question to the lead. Commits Sŏn to the same rule without the rhythm. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** "never" as policy and "at the pass" as practice; a rule that makes a person's concern about their lead reach the lead with the person's name attached.

**A finished answer:** the choice, how what is heard reaches the lead, and the sentence for "please keep this from them."

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.9.7 Decide the founder in a seat: time on the ground, its rules, and what a founder may do with what they see

**The question:** does each founder work seats after dinner's first quarter, on what rhythm, under whose authority for the service, and what happens to what they see?

**Why it matters now:** the counsel question from 2.2.5 (a founder under a designation as an employee for any purpose) has to be answered before the first such service. The card (5.9.16) is written from this.

**Openers:**
- It is month five. You take a runner's seat for a Tuesday. The room designation is held by a server hired in March. They tell you to reset table twelve. What happens?
- Sideways: she spent two hours next to a risk analyst and learned more than from any number of presentations, then told the team's manager what she saw and calls that bonus points (pp. 453 to 454). What did you learn in a seat that no report could have told you? Is there a seat here you would take to learn the same way?
- Dominic has not worked a floor. Does he take a seat too, and which one?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Rhythm: a stated number of services per period; only when short-crewed (4.4.12); a block once a year; never in the first quarter?
- Bound by the seat's range card and the room's designation: yes?
- What you do with what you see: a capture at the close as process, never a name; a question to the lead once; nothing written about a person?
- Do you tell the lead afterwards, as the book advises, and in what form?
- Who answers the counsel question, and by when?

**What the book says:** dedicate a few hours to the team's daily tasks; share the observations with the team's manager; Collison's multi-day version (pp. 453 to 454).

**White-paper default:** Brandon worked the floor (WP pp. 3 to 4); a designation marks accountability in the moment and is not a permanent elevation (WP p. 10); CPU failure is a founder-level person doing work beneath their capability (WP p. 13).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Collison's "engineerication" is the book's example (p. 454). Chef-owners working the line nightly is the industry norm and the white paper's counterexample: it is the dependency, not the practice.

**Options:** (a) a stated rhythm, bound by the seat and the room, a capture at the close, a question to the lead once. Commits Sŏn to founders answering to their own team for a service and to the counsel answer first. (b) Only when short-crewed. Commits Sŏn to presence as a fix, which is CPU failure by another name. (c) The book's block once a year. Commits Sŏn to a scheduled absence from the founder's own domain. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** a founder in a seat who is really running the floor; an observation about a person carried to the lead as "just so you know."

**A finished answer:** the rhythm per founder, the authority rule, the three things done with what is seen, the tell-the-lead form, and who owns the counsel answer.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen.

### 5.9.8 Decide how a concern about a lead is held: the destinations, the count, and what is never kept

**The question:** when something about a lead keeps happening, who notices, how, what is counted, how it reaches the lead, and what is never written?

**Why it matters now:** 5.4.12's candor count already exists as a supervisory instrument on leads, and 5.4 asked that 5.9 name it as one. 5.3.4 and 4.5.11 route a concern about one's own lead somewhere. Without this decision, those pieces reach a founder with no rule for what the founder does next.

**Openers:**
- Three people in two months say, at their check-ins, that the Operations Lead decided a dispute after hearing one side. Walk me through what happens, in order.
- The earlier work's rule was that no partner keeps a file on a lead; a pattern is a count, raised once, read first as a missing written standard. Say the counter-argument yourself: what does a count miss that a file would hold?
- Sideways: the white paper says "management absorbs blame from staff" (WP p. 17). When the lead is the one who erred, who absorbs it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Which patterns are counted: the one-sided hearing, the personal standard where no written one exists, a range breach, the candor count, a domain that repeatedly opens 5.8 (4.3.12)?
- Counted by whom, from which records, never from one person's word (5.1.3)?
- Threshold as a reset parameter; raised once at the monthly conversation after the lead's account; read first as a mechanism?
- Written on the lead's page, in whose words, or not at all?
- Confirm: no partner keeps a file, case notes, or narrative about a lead outside the page.
- Confirm: 5.2.2's breach boundary applies to a lead unchanged; what follows is 5.10's.

**What the book says:** a manager's manager brings broader context into performance conversations, drawn from skip levels and observation (p. 452); coach the manager to their own solution (p. 450).

**White-paper default:** team members weigh in on management effectiveness (WP p. 19); management absorbs blame (WP p. 17); silent on any record about a lead.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most companies keep manager-authored notes on managers as on anyone; the alternative the earlier work proposed, a count raised once and unwritten, is unusual, and counsel's view on it is in 5.2.4 and 5.8.2.

**Options:** (a) the earlier work's rule: counts, once, mechanism first, nothing kept. Commits Sŏn to counsel accepting a lead's own words as the only record. (b) Counts, raised once, written on the lead's page in the lead's words at the read-back. Commits Sŏn to the page being the record. (c) The book's practice: the partner's notes, shared with the lead. Commits Sŏn to a partner writing about a lead. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** a count that reaches a founder without the lead ever hearing it; a "mechanism first" rule that never gets past the mechanism.

**A finished answer:** the patterns, the counter and sources, the threshold, the raising rule, the record answer, the two confirmations.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.9.9 Decide how a lead's own performance process runs: the holder, the second reader, and what differs from anyone's

**The question:** when a lead's work is not holding, does 5.8's process apply unchanged, or the book's senior path, and who is the second reader?

**Why it matters now:** 5.8 built the process for everyone and named the lead as its holder; a lead has no lead. 2.2.5 and 2.3.14 said who reads the other lead when a partner is the subject; this is where it is used. A lead's candidate sheet is read by someone who will ask.

**Openers:**
- The Maitre d's domain has opened three performance windows in a quarter and the pulse for the floor is down. Before anyone says the Maitre d's name: what does the house check about itself?
- Sideways: the book says senior departures usually skip the formal plan and run over months, framed on "the gap between the role and the company's needs and observed performance," and half the time the person sees it coming and says so first (pp. 458 to 459). Is that dignity, or is it the "no surprises" rule broken for the people at the top?
- Would you want your own process, if you had one, to look like everyone's?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Unchanged; the senior path; or a hybrid (a window exists, its rows are the seat's outcomes)?
- Second reader: the other partner, the other lead, counsel by rule?
- The pauses that read the house first: the seat load reading (4.1.37), the range (4.4.8), the interval drifted, a promised system unbuilt, 4.1.36's relief order not exhausted?
- Who judges the close, on what: the seat's outcomes (2.2.22), an assessor from outside the domain?
- Are the two leads' windows or reviews ever compared? The earlier work said never.
- Confirm the hand-off to 5.10 is the same closed package.

**What the book says:** senior conversations take two or three sessions, center on whether the role's needs are met, and move to timing and transition when the two sides do not see eye to eye (pp. 458 to 459); a manager's manager brings broader context (p. 452).

**White-paper default:** the leads are accountable to outcomes (WP p. 10); reviews are the path forward rather than a verdict; no surprises (WP p. 19).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most companies do run a separate, quieter path for senior people, which is what the book describes; the earlier work's position was that one process for everyone is the flat structure made real.

**Options:** (a) unchanged: records only, the partner holds it, the same window and close. Commits Sŏn to one policy a candidate can read. (b) The book's senior path. Commits Sŏn to a different policy for leads and to saying so on the sheet. (c) A hybrid. Commits Sŏn to defining what differs and why. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** a second reader who is the co-lead, which makes each lead the other's evaluator; pauses that become a permanent shield for a lead who is the problem (4.3's warning).

**A finished answer:** the path, the second reader, the pauses, the close, the comparison rule, the hand-off confirmed.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.9.10 Decide how a partner reads the lead they do not hold, and how divergence between the two domains is read

**The question:** how does Dominic know how the floor is doing, and Brandon the operations side, without either becoming a second reader over a lead they do not hold, and what happens when the two leads run like cases differently?

**Why it matters now:** two founders, two leads, one relationship each: the other three relationships need a rule or they will form on their own. 2.3.4 gives the partners a written monthly read of each other's domains; this decides what that read may draw on.

**Openers:**
- You notice something about the Operations Lead's domain that worries you. Dominic holds that conversation. What do you do that day, and what do you not do?
- Sideways: the two leads are handling lateness differently, the kitchen's way and the floor's way, and people are comparing notes. Is that two leads out of line, or one missing written standard?
- The earlier work's rule: a partner reads the other lead only through records. Which records would actually tell you what you want to know?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Through records only: the leads' review record, the goal page, the seat load reading, the pulse by domain?
- A concern about the lead you do not hold: a line in the monthly domain read (2.3.4); a question at the weekly review; never a conversation with that lead about it?
- Divergence between the leads: a calibration item on the leads' review fixed at the mechanism, never one lead correcting the other; or manager-to-manager alignment run by the partners?
- The permanent informal room holder: confirm it is 4.3.13's and 4.1.20's, a rotation rule at the reset.

**What the book says:** the system keeps the manager involved enough to unblock and coach without doing everyone's job (pp. 449 to 450).

**White-paper default:** authority lives in the domain (WP p. 7); co-leads serving the same center (WP p. 10); node overload fixed by restoring the separation (WP p. 14).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Two-founder companies commonly split executive relationships one each and read the other through the leadership meeting; the failure is the founder who reads "their" lead's peer directly and creates a shadow line.

**Options:** (a) records only, concern to the domain read, divergence fixed at the mechanism. Commits Sŏn to the leads' review record being good enough to read by. (b) Records plus a standing question at the partners' weekly review. Commits Sŏn to the founders talking about both leads weekly. (c) Either partner may speak to either lead about the domain. Commits Sŏn to two readers per lead and the drift that brings. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** "records only" with records that hold nothing; a divergence rule that quietly makes one lead the standard.

**A finished answer:** the record list, the concern path, the divergence rule, the room-holder confirmation.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.9.11 Decide how a lead develops after onboarding: the house's mechanisms, an outside mentor, or a leadership program

**The question:** after the first months, how does a lead get better, and who decides when they need help the founders cannot give?

**Why it matters now:** 3.3.22 and 3.3.23 covered onboarding and the first months; nothing covers year two. The white paper's aim is internal succession (WP p. 19), and the leads are who the next leads learn from.

**Openers:**
- The Operations Lead in month nine is competent and plateaued. What does the house have for them that is not a promotion?
- The white paper says "a lead who understands both sides of the house leads better" (WP p. 19). Does the Maitre d spend time in the operations seat? Is that development or the blurring 4.1.3 forbids?
- Sideways: the book says teach them to steer the fishing boat (p. 450). Who taught you? Was it a program, a person, or the floor?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The mechanisms everyone has, plus peer mentoring from a lead in another house; an outside mentor by seat or by need; a 360 once a year; a written program?
- Who decides when outside help is needed, and how it is found?
- Cross-training into the other lead's domain: a path, or a risk?
- The founders' own development on the same footing (5.1.12, 6.3)?

**What the book says:** coach them to their own solutions; find them the help you cannot give, an outside mentor or a colleague; set expectations about where you add value (pp. 450, 452 to 453).

**White-paper default:** cross-training is career development; internal succession is the goal; the platform is how anyone learns (WP p. 19). No leadership program.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Hospitality groups that grow leads from within commonly rotate them across properties or functions rather than run a classroom program; outside coaching for a first-time senior lead is common in technology and rare in restaurants.

**Options:** (a) the mechanisms everyone has, plus a peer lead elsewhere. Commits Sŏn to the platform and the conversation carrying it. (b) An outside mentor or coach by need, the house paying. Commits Sŏn to a cost Dominic weighs and a rule for who asks. (c) A 360 or program. Commits Sŏn to instruments the white paper does not have. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** a development answer that is really a title; a cross-training path that puts one lead's hands in the other's domain.

**A finished answer:** the path, the decider for outside help, the cross-training answer, the founders' footing.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
