# 1.2 Say the thing you think you cannot say: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 1, principle 2, pp. 52 to 56. Full text: `extraction/build/s01/book.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## The principle

Her opening story: in an early quarterly business review at Stripe, people were talking around a significant blocker, a dependency on another team. She stopped the review and asked whether there was or was not a problem with the other team. The rest of the meeting went to the real issue, and an engineer on the way out said, "Well, that was refreshing" (p. 52).

Her question: why don't managers say what is actually on their minds? People think good management is about having a lot of filters, and much does feel risky or like a personal judgment. Her rule: be wary of over-filtering. Fine-tune the filters and push yourself to name the observation constructively, and the conversation becomes honest enough to work on a solution (p. 52).

## Why it is hard: the it, the we, and the I

From Fred Kofman's *Conscious Business*. Every conversation has three components (pp. 52 to 53):
- **The "it":** the task being discussed. Disagreement about what happened, unspoken beliefs about who is at fault.
- **The "we":** the relationship between the people in the room. Do you respect, like, trust each other?
- **The "I":** your own stance. Am I stupid for thinking this? What will people think of me if I say it?

Each can add unspoken dynamics. The framework helps to work through blockers and "give air to the voices in your head" (p. 53).

## Three tactics

1. **Share your feelings.** Emotions are a management tool because everyone understands them. "We didn't hit our targets" says nothing about gravity; "We didn't hit our targets, and I'm worried about the impact" tells the team there is a problem to fix (pp. 53 to 54).
2. **Be measured.** "I'm freaking out because we didn't hit our targets" panics the team. Eli (the transparency story in 1.1) was honest about what was going on but not measured, and destabilized his team (p. 54).
3. **Separate the person from the idea or task.** Keep the discussion on the "it." Criticism feels oppositional, two people facing off; it is more powerful to stand next to someone and look at the same thing together. Not "that presentation was terrible" but "what did you think of the presentation? I was disappointed in aspects of it." Not "the team is performing terribly" but "my impression is that the team isn't communicating well right now, and I think deadlines are being missed as a result. Do you agree, or could it be something else?" (pp. 54 to 55).

## The balancing act, and the payoff

Management can feel like gymnastics: a step right and you have shared too much, a step left and you are unapproachable. Learning to express the closest thing to your truth constructively keeps your balance, and the dismount is trust (p. 55). Teams where people can say what is on their minds raise and resolve problems faster and are happier; being open, direct, and constructive earns the one-to-one trust that makes a manager-report relationship work. She cites *Radical Candor* for why open, caring communication is the foundation of high-functioning teams (p. 55).

## Practice

You will sometimes say it badly. Her example: she told someone they seemed nervous in meetings and got a blank stare and a defensive remark. The next time she asked what they thought of the meeting first, let them speak, then offered "To me, you seem a bit nervous. What do you think?" and, when asked why, named the clues. The conversation turned to how to mitigate the pattern, which was the conversation they needed (pp. 55 to 56). More on comments that can be heard as personal judgments in Chapter 5; the templates on coaching, feedback, meetings, and offsites are practice (p. 56).
