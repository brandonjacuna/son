# 2.3 Operating cadence: working session

The guide for the session (or sessions) that decides this chunk. Task numbers are `tasks.md`'s; "cons. N" is a section of `considerations.md`; "WP p. N" is the white paper. Nothing here is a decision. Every brief ends with what a finished answer looks like, so the conversation knows when to stop. The plain terms (shift brief, shift close, check-in, person page, leads' review, partners' review, house review, gate review, mechanism reset, cadence calendar) are defined at the top of `tasks.md`; use them as written so the calendar reads the same everywhere.

## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** The last section of the book's Chapter 2 (pp. 135 to 143, with the QBR guidelines at pp. 156 to 165): the schedule on which everything 2.2 built gets set, reviewed, and communicated. Her list is annual planning, quarterly reviews, monthly all-hands, biweekly 1:1s, weekly meetings with metrics, daily standups. Sŏn's version has to hold two tempos at once: a build that runs on proof gates and does not end at opening, and a service day whose unit is the shift (cons. 1). The chunk ends with one table, the cadence calendar, every rhythm with its owner, its record, and its cost in bandwidth, and one page of it for the team.

**Where it sits.** After 2.2, which decided that each mechanism exists, who is in it, and what it reads first. This chunk decides when, where, how long, and in what order, and adds the rhythms 2.2 could not: the partners' own, the check-in, the closed day, the reset. Where 2.2 is still open on a mechanism, this chunk can still place its slot; note the dependency and go on. It hands back to 2.2 the cadence rows for the readiness test.

**What is already decided upstream.** Check `manual/2.1-founding-documents/decisions.md` and `manual/2.2-the-operating-system/decisions.md` before opening. This chunk leans on: how the partners decide and the reserved classes (2.1.13); the seat descriptions (2.1.8, 2.1.9) and who holds culinary direction before the chef seat is filled (2.1.10); dinner's charter and its seasonal shape (2.1.15); who may change the founding documents and when (2.1.18); and from 2.2: the units (2.2.1), who holds each lead's check-in and review (2.2.5), the mechanism set (2.2.29), the goal form and the baseline rule (2.2.17, 2.2.18), the metrics and their owners (2.2.19 to 2.2.22), per-service ownership in the brief (2.2.31) and the brief's and close's operating-system fields (2.2.32), "dinner is steady" (2.2.38), and the readiness test (2.2.39). Brandon's one-seat ruling (the floor manager and the Maitre d are one seat, 2026-09-13) means the owner of the room, and so the runner of the brief, is the Maitre d on every service they work.

**Who else must agree.** Most decisions are "agreed by both seated founders": the partners' review, the closed day, the leads' review, the house review, the gate review, and the reset are the founders' shared machinery. The chef partner owns 2.3.29 outright (the menu turn and its training window) and writes the kitchen's readiness block, close checklist, house-review memo, and check-in holder once seated; until then those are marked as theirs. Each lead confirms the structures they own in 2.3.32. Where Brandon has a position and Dominic has not weighed in, record it and mark it "pending agreement from Dominic."

**How to run it.** Twenty decisions and four choices inside deliverables. The agenda below groups them into three sittings by phase. Take them in the order he wants, but 2.3.1 (the shape) and 2.3.3 (the entry test) shape everything after them, and 2.3.12 (the closed day) has to come before anything is placed on a day.

## 3. Briefs

### 2.3.1 Decide whether Sŏn runs one cadence with two phases or two rhythms that coexist

**The question:** do the pre-opening build and the running restaurant share one calendar whose agenda changes at the gate, or run two rhythms that coexist until the last period opens, and either way, what do they share and what do they not?

**Why it matters now:** the build does not end at opening; each later period is built while the one before it runs (WP p. 36), so the answer holds for a year or more. It decides whether 2.3.2's seam rules are needed as rules or as habits, and it is the first thing the cadence calendar (2.3.31) has to show.

**Openers:**
- Dinner has been open six weeks. The early morning's buildout is behind. Where does the Tuesday build meeting happen, and who is in it who also worked Monday's close?
- Sideways: describe the meeting you and Dominic have now about the build. Then imagine it the week after dinner opens. What in it points at the room, and should it?
- The book says start from what already happens (p. 137). What already happens: the brand's daily pulse, weekly read, monthly review (WP p. 31), and your build meeting. Is that one rhythm or two, today?

**Narrowing questions:**
- One calendar with two phases, two rhythms that coexist, or start with what runs now and decide at the first reset?
- If two: what is shared (the partners' review, the goal cycle, the gate review, the brand cadence) and what is not (the daily unit, the leads' review, the season)?
- If one: what protects the room from the build's tempo other than self-restraint?
- Name the cost: two rhythms is more machinery for two founders; one is a habit carried across the seam by construction (cons. 1).

**What the book says:** the cadence mirrors the company cadence and is agreed up front; have one but do not get stuck in it (p. 136); if none exists, observe what is happening organically and build from it (p. 137).

**White-paper default:** two tempos described and never named: milestones and proof for the build; the shift for the day (WP pp. 05, 26, 36). No default on whether that is one cadence or two.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Restaurant groups opening a second unit while the first runs commonly keep an "openings" track with its own weekly meeting, separate from the operating review of the open rooms, precisely so the open rooms' meeting does not become the openings meeting.
- Software companies with a platform team and a product team often run the same split: a delivery cadence and a run cadence, sharing the quarterly planning cycle.

**Options:** (a) two rhythms that coexist, with named shared members and a seam rule. Commits Sŏn to naming what is shared and to a rule about build meetings in service hours. (b) one calendar with two phases. Commits Sŏn to less machinery and to the founders' self-restraint as the protection. (c) start with what runs now and decide the shape at the first reset. Commits Sŏn to designing the service rhythm with the leads, at the cost of a thinner candidate sheet (WP p. 18). Depth: cons. 1.

**Watch for:** choosing two rhythms and then scheduling both in the same hour with the same people; choosing one and calling it two.

**A finished answer:** the shape stated, the shared and unshared members listed, and the cost named aloud.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 2.3.2 Decide the rules that keep the build's tempo out of the room

**The question:** which rules, if any, keep the founders' build work from running the room at the build's pace during dinner's first quarter, and does the same protection repeat at each later opening?

**Why it matters now:** the founders will have run at speed for a year and then walk into a room that runs on the table (cons. 1); "dinner is what everything protects" (WP p. 33) needs a mechanical form or it is a wish; the first reset (2.3.34) is when the freeze lifts.

**Openers:**
- It is week three. The brief's spine is visibly wrong in one place. Do you fix it Thursday, or at the reset? Say why.
- Sideways: name a room where the owners' project meetings happened at the bar during service. What did the floor learn from watching?
- Which of these would you break first: no build meeting in the building during service hours, or the room's rhythms held fixed for a quarter?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Rule one: no build meeting in the building from the first brief to the last close. Accept, change, decline.
- Rule two: the room's rhythms held fixed through dinner's first quarter, except safety, a non-negotiable, a promise to customers, and the close's continuous process updates. Accept, change, decline.
- Rule three: first-quarter goals are baselines (2.2.18 applied). Confirm it applies here.
- The founders' floor presence on the service's tempo belongs to 6.1; note it and do not decide it here.
- Does the protection repeat for the early morning's first quarter and late night's, or is that the reset's to decide?

**What the book says:** stability and predictability most of the time; do not change processes too quickly (pp. 136, 141).

**White-paper default:** bandwidth first (WP p. 12); dinner is what everything protects (WP p. 33); no operating rule for the months when the founders build the next period while the room runs (cons. 2).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. "Change freezes" around a launch are standard in operations: no configuration changes for a stated window after go-live except for safety and incident fixes. The form is the same as rule two.

**Options:** (a) all three rules, repeating at each later opening. Commits the founders to a fixed quarter after every gate. (b) rules one and three only, with the rhythms adjustable at the leads' review. Commits Sŏn to a cadence the crew may have to relearn monthly. (c) no rules; the founders hold the line by judgment. Commits Sŏn to self-restraint. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** a freeze so tight the close's process updates stop; a freeze the founders exempt themselves from.

**A finished answer:** each rule accepted, changed, or declined by name; the set written in one paragraph; the repeat question answered or handed to the reset.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 2.3.3 Set the test a rhythm must pass before it enters the calendar

**The question:** what five or six lines must any proposed meeting, report, or check satisfy before it goes on the calendar, and does every new one enter as an experiment?

**Why it matters now:** every rhythm costs bandwidth and bandwidth is first (WP p. 12); the test is the first thing the cadence calendar cites (2.3.31) and the standard the reset (2.3.30) reads every rhythm against; without it the calendar grows by default.

**Openers:**
- Someone proposes a Monday managers' meeting in month four. What is the first question you ask, and the last?
- Sideways: the book says a good process should subtract from the number of meetings (p. 142). Name one meeting at a past room that a written line would have replaced.
- What is the one rhythm you would add that fails your own test? That is the test's calibration.

**Narrowing questions:**
- The candidate lines: it states its cost in bandwidth and what it buys; it has one owner; it runs on the system, not on a memory; it sits in the pre-service window or after the close, never mid-service; it fixes a process, not a person; it enters as an experiment with a duration, a check-in date, and criteria. Add, cut, reword?
- Her question attached: could this be a written update or a line in the tracker (p. 142)?
- Who applies the test to a proposal: the owner of the calendar, the leads' review, the partners?

**What the book says:** one owner per structure; defensive and stale processes; experiments state their duration, check-in, and criteria; a good process subtracts meetings (pp. 139 to 142).

**White-paper default:** bandwidth is first (WP p. 12); the company runs on its systems (WP pp. 05 to 06, 21); blame the process (WP pp. 13, 17). The mid-service line is the realist's, not the white paper's.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Amazon's rule that a meeting needs a written narrative rather than slides is a de facto entry test: a meeting that cannot produce a document does not happen. Agile teams' "definition of done" is the same device pointed at work rather than meetings.

**Options:** (a) the six lines plus her question, applied by the calendar's owner. Commits every proposer to writing cost and buy before asking. (b) the experiment rule alone. Commits Sŏn to trying anything for a stated period. (c) no test; the partners decide each proposal. Commits the partners to being the filter. Depth: cons. 8.

**Watch for:** a test so strict the brief itself fails it (it should pass); a test with no owner.

**A finished answer:** the lines written, the experiment rule attached, the applier named, and the brief and the leads' review both pass it out loud.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 2.3.4 Decide the partners' weekly review and monthly domain read

**The question:** on what day, for how long, and on what agenda do the two partners meet each week, how does each read the other's domain each month, and where does the chef partner sit in both once seated?

**Why it matters now:** it is the one meeting that exists today in some form and has no fixed shape; the reserved-class decisions (2.1.13) need a place to be taken; the partners' page (2.3.6) is written by it; the gate review (2.3.26) sits inside it pre-opening.

**Openers:**
- Describe last week's conversation with Dominic about the build. When was it, how long, what got decided, and where is that written?
- Sideways: equal partners with non-overlapping domains do not need a meeting to assign each other work. What do you need one for? Say it in one sentence.
- Would you rather read Dominic's domain page and write three questions, or hear him talk for twenty minutes? Which would he rather?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Day, length, fixed. Which?
- The agenda: each domain's state; decisions in each partner's own range; reserved-class decisions under 2.1.13; cross-domain asks with owners and dates; one thing each carries into the week. Accept, or reorder?
- The monthly domain read: each partner reads the other's page and writes questions before the month's first review. Accept?
- In service: does it move to a closed hour and read the leads' review's record rather than repeat it?
- The chef partner's seat, once seated: the culinary domain's page joins; the read runs three ways; the reserved classes run on the three-partner rule.

**What the book says:** a cadence for decisions and progress; one owner per structure (pp. 136, 139).

**White-paper default:** the brand's cadence is the one complete cadence in the white paper (a daily pulse, a weekly read, a monthly strategic review, WP p. 31), the shape to borrow; silent on how the partners meet (WP pp. 03, 05 to 06).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Co-founder pairs who last commonly report a fixed weekly meeting with a written agenda and a decision log; the log matters more than the meeting because it is what settles "I thought we agreed."
- The monthly read of a peer's area in writing, with questions ahead, is how boards read management: the memo comes first, the meeting is the questions.

**Options:** (a) weekly, fixed, the five-part agenda, the monthly read in writing, moving to a closed hour in service. Commits both partners to writing a domain page monthly. (b) weekly with a loose agenda and no domain read. Commits Sŏn to a meeting that becomes a status call. (c) the brand's cadence extended: a daily check, a weekly review, a monthly strategic review. Commits the partners to a daily touchpoint through the build. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** a meeting with no record; the reserved classes decided in passing; the chef partner's seat left undescribed.

**A finished answer:** day, length, agenda fixed; the monthly read stated; the in-service move stated; the chef partner's seat described.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 2.3.5 Assign the owning partner for each pre-opening workstream

**The question:** which partner owns each pre-opening workstream, including the two the white paper is silent on, construction and hiring?

**Why it matters now:** 3.1 cannot start without a hiring owner; the partners' page carries the owners (2.3.6); the workstream owners are the first row of the calendar's owner column.

**Openers:**
- List the workstreams: capital, construction, the technology build, the operating systems, the experience, hiring, the documented build. Say a name after each without pausing. Where did you pause?
- Sideways: the hires run the operating systems and the experience. The capital pays for the building. Which argument decides hiring and which decides construction, or is it the same argument?
- Who answers the general contractor's call at seven in the morning?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The five the white paper assigns (WP pp. 03, 05): confirm.
- Hiring: Brandon's seat (the old work's reasoning: the hires run his domains), Dominic's, or split by role (leads by both, hourly by the domain lead once seated)?
- Construction: Dominic's (capital in built form), Brandon's (the experience is the room), or both with one named?
- Whether recruiting help is engaged for the pre-opening burst is 3.1's question; note it.

**What the book says:** be clear which leader owns which structure; match structures to strengths (p. 139).

**White-paper default:** Brandon owns the concept, the operating systems, and the experience; Dominic the capital, the financial architecture, the data architecture, and the technology build (WP pp. 03, 05). Silent on construction and hiring.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Founding pairs typically give construction to whoever holds the capital relationship, because the draw schedule and the contractor's invoices run through the same person; hiring tends to sit with whoever will manage the hires. Neither is a rule.

**Options:** (a) hiring to Brandon's seat, construction to Dominic's. Commits Brandon's seat to 3.1 and Dominic's to the build site. (b) both to Brandon. Commits Brandon's seat to the two heaviest pre-opening loads at once. (c) hiring split by role. Commits Sŏn to two hiring processes. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** an owner named for a workstream the other partner is actually running; two names on one line.

**A finished answer:** every workstream with one owning partner on the partners' page; 3.1 told who the hiring partner is.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 2.3.7 Decide the shift brief's fixed content and order

**The question:** what is the spine of the brief, in what order, what survives a thirty-second version, is it spoken in every roster language, and is the spine the same at every period's brief?

**Why it matters now:** the brief already exists and is a named failure when it is missing (WP p. 14); it is the one place her daily standup already lives at Sŏn (cons. 3); 2.2.31 and 2.2.32 decided what the brief must name for the operating system, and this decides the order it is said in and what the rush may not delete.

**Openers:**
- It is five twenty, two people called out, and the first table is at five thirty. Run the brief out loud. What did you say, and in what order?
- Sideways: the best pre-shift you ever stood in. What did the person running it do first? What did they never skip?
- Which is worse: a brief that runs long and eats the pre-service window, or a brief that skips the period's goal for a month?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The candidate spine: a readiness block the rush cannot delete (the 86 list; allergies routed by station; recognized customers on the book; staffing gaps; station checks), the period's goal in one sentence, the one thing from the week's leads' review, recognitions since the last brief, then team content bounded by the time left. Accept, reorder, cut?
- The thirty-second version: the readiness block only? Say what survives.
- Spoken in every roster language: accept, or a different rule (4.8 sets the list)?
- The same spine at every open period's brief: yes, or may the owner of the room reorder?
- The kitchen's readiness content is the chef partner's; the team content is 4.5's.

**What the book says:** daily standups; predictable, common structures as touchstones (pp. 136 to 137).

**White-paper default:** "beginning of shift, prepare for the customer" (WP p. 26); the brief's absence is sequence corruption (WP p. 14); the pre-shift briefing is as important as the signature dish (WP p. 20); recognition starts at the brief (WP p. 22). No spine given.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Aviation's pre-flight briefing has a fixed order and a "challenge and response" form so that a rushed briefing still covers the safety items; the readiness block is that idea for a dining room.
- Danny Meyer's rooms run a daily pre-meal that covers the menu, the book, and one teaching point; the "one thing" line is the same device.

**Options:** (a) the five-part spine fixed in order, the readiness block also on the OS surface. Commits Sŏn to a brief that survives compression and to building the block into the surface. (b) the readiness block only, the rest at the lead's discretion. Commits Sŏn to a brief that never carries the week's one thing. (c) the spine as a default the owner of the room may reorder. Commits Sŏn to variation by period and lead, against p. 137. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** team content placed before readiness; a spine that needs a lead's memory to run.

**A finished answer:** the spine in order, the thirty-second version stated, the language rule ruled, the same-at-every-period rule ruled, the kitchen's block marked for the chef partner.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen's block.

### 2.3.8 Decide the shift close's fixed content

**The question:** what does the close carry, which of its two demands yields on a short night, and what does it refuse?

**Why it matters now:** the white paper funds the close twice, the thank-you note (WP p. 26) and the capture (WP p. 13), and does not say how they share one window (cons. 3); 2.2.32's fields have to fit inside whatever this decides; the close is where the cultural labor score's line may live (2.2.20).

**Openers:**
- Eleven forty on a Saturday, a server has six notes to write and one recovery to capture. Which do they do first, and who decided that?
- Sideways: the old work's rule is that a capture that takes longer than the note is a system failure, not a server failure. Do you agree? What would you build differently if you did?
- Where does the lead's one line on how hard the night ran on the team go, and who reads it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The candidate close: the capture (incidents, preferences learned, what the next server inherits); the notes; the lead's one line on the night, with one line on how hard it ran; the station close checklist. Accept, add, cut?
- Which yields on a short night, the capture or the notes? State the rule.
- The refusals: nothing about a person (that is the check-in's); no post-mortem (private and off the floor, WP p. 17); no cleaning (the overnight crew's, WP p. 26). Confirm.
- The kitchen's close checklist is the chef partner's.

**What the book says:** written updates and measures of progress within each cadence (p. 137).

**White-paper default:** the note by every server to every customer, with cleaning handed to an overnight crew (WP p. 26); the capture in the shift close that the lead reviews and the process absorbs (WP p. 13); post-mortems private and process-focused (WP p. 17).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Hospital shift handoffs use a fixed structured form (SBAR is the best known) so the outgoing shift's capture takes minutes and the incoming shift inherits it without a conversation; the "what the next server inherits" field is the same purpose.

**Options:** (a) the four-part close, the capture yields to nothing but takes less time than the notes, refusals as stated. Commits the stack to a capture that takes minutes. (b) notes only on a short night, capture the next day. Commits Sŏn to a capture written from memory. (c) capture only, notes optional. Commits Sŏn against WP p. 26. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** a close that needs a lead to type it; a field that names a person; the cultural labor score line if 2.2.20 put it elsewhere.

**A finished answer:** the content written, the yield rule stated, the refusals stated, the kitchen's checklist marked.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen's checklist.

### 2.3.9 Decide the check-in as Sŏn's form of the 1:1

**The question:** is the 1:1's function carried by an on-the-clock check-in placed by the schedule, and if so, how long, in what window, held by whom per domain, with what spine, and refusing what?

**Why it matters now:** her 1:1 is the load-bearing member of her cadence and every assumption in it is written for salaried people with desks (cons. 4); the white paper's "no surprises" rule (WP p. 19) is a wish without something between reviews; the alternative most restaurants choose is nothing. The candidate sheet's "communication expectations" (WP p. 18) has to describe it.

**Openers:**
- A runner, four months in, is doing fine and quietly looking elsewhere. In the white paper's building, when does anyone find out, and how?
- Sideways: the best conversation a manager ever had with you about how you were doing. Where was it, how long, and was anyone paying you for it?
- Fifteen minutes on the clock as the first minutes of a shift, placed by the system. Say what you like about that and what you do not.

**Narrowing questions:**
- The function carried by an on-the-clock check-in placed by the schedule, folded into the brief, or by the ninety-day plan's check-ins alone?
- Length. Window (pre-service, never mid-service, never at the close).
- Holder per domain: the Maitre d for the floor; the kitchen's marked for the chef partner.
- The spine: what is working; what is in the way (heard as a system question); what changed; the person's read on the lead and the house; one line written, read back, next date. Accept, or change?
- The refusals: a verdict, a pay conversation, a task assignment, discipline, a post-mortem, a scheduling negotiation. Confirm.
- The range: may move it to the next shared shift when the window is short, may not cancel it; twice moved is a load reading at the leads' review; the optional early placement once per interval. Accept?
- The interpreter question is 4.8's; hand it over.

**What the book says:** biweekly 1:1s as a member of the cadence (p. 136).

**White-paper default:** the ninety-day plan promises regular check-ins (WP p. 18); the review never contains new information (WP p. 19); the Maitre d owns team development for the floor (WP p. 10). Nothing on form, pay, or record.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Retail and hospitality operators that run anything like a 1:1 for hourly staff almost always do it on the clock and short; "stay interviews" (a scheduled conversation about what would make someone stay) are the closest named practice, usually quarterly.
- Hospitals' charge nurses do a brief check with each nurse at the start of shift; it is the same "first minutes of the shift" placement.

**Options:** (a) the on-the-clock check-in as proposed. Commits Sŏn to early starts on check-in days, to the lead's windows carrying the rotation, and to a page the person can read. (b) folded into the brief. Commits Sŏn to a public conversation where a private one was needed. (c) no standing check-in after the ninety-day plan. Commits the review to being the first time a person hears how they are doing, against WP p. 19. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** a form that only works for a full-time roster; a spine that turns into a review; a check-in a lead can cancel.

**A finished answer:** carried or not, and if so length, window, holder per domain, spine, refusals, and range; the interpreter question handed to 4.8.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen's holder.

### 2.3.10 Set the check-in's intervals

**The question:** how often inside the ninety-day plan, how often after it, and what happens when a lead's windows cannot carry the roster?

**Why it matters now:** the review every three to six months sets a ceiling (WP p. 19); the Maitre d's pre-service windows are the one cost in the cadence that can exceed its budget (cons. 8); both numbers are parameters the reset revisits.
