# 2.1 Founding documents: working-session guide

For the facilitator. Reference depth is in `considerations.md` (sections cited as "cons. N") and `book.md`; the task list is `tasks.md`; the record goes in `decisions.md`. "WP p. N" is the white paper (`extraction/s01/record.md`).

Working rules for the room: Brandon decides, the facilitator never does. Options and defaults are starting points. Nothing he says about his own strengths, working style, or failure modes gets recorded here; that is chunk 6.x. If he reaches for the old program's vocabulary ("canon," "the charter," "daypart charter"), keep the plain word. Brain dumps go to `notes/YYYY-MM-DD-<topic>.md` in his words before they are funneled.

## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** The book's four founding documents: a mission, long-term goals, principles, and team charters (pp. 71 to 83). Sŏn adds three things the book does not ask for at this level: descriptions of the three founding seats, a rule for how the partners decide together and break deadlocks, and an honest description of working here for candidates. Everything is assembled into one short founding document that a new hire can read before a first shift.

**What it depends on upstream.** Only the operating agreement Brandon and Dominic have worked on. It governs ownership, capital, votes, and what happens when a principal joins or leaves. The founding documents defer to it by reference; they must not restate or contradict it. Nothing in this manual has read it (2.1.1).

**Who else has to agree.** Nearly every decision here binds Dominic, so his agreement is needed on all of them; the record marks his items "pending agreement from Dominic" until he has. Three decisions also reach the chef partner once seated: the decisions about their seat and its description (2.1.10, 2.1.11), any rule about building or removing a service period (2.1.13, 2.1.15), and their voice in changing the documents (2.1.18). Until then, the two seated founders decide for the interim and mark what is held open.

**Why this chunk comes first.** Three reasons in the record. The white paper commits to orienting every new person to the mission, values, and behavioral standard before operational training (WP p. 18); that orientation needs documents that exist. The company is meant to run without its founders and without depending on either founder's memory (WP pp. 05 to 06); a mission and principles that live only in two heads are the memory failure the white paper diagnoses (WP p. 13). And the chef partner is being searched for now (WP p. 29) and will join a company that has already decided a great deal; the founding document is how they see what was settled before them and what was held for them.

**The book's frame in one line.** Founding documents are the foundations of the house; the operating system (2.2) and the cadence (2.3) are built on top of them (pp. 71, 73).

## 3. Briefs

### 2.1.1 Read the operating agreement and note what it already settles

**The question:** what does the agreement already fix on votes, deadlock, admitting a principal, a principal's departure or incapacity, and ownership of domains?

**Why it matters now:** 2.1.7 to 2.1.11, 2.1.13, and 2.1.18 all either restate the agreement, contradict it, or defer to it. Only deferring is safe (cons. 1). If this is skipped, the deadlock rule chosen in 2.1.13 may already be overruled by a clause neither founder remembers.

**Openers:**
- Have you and Dominic read the current draft end to end together, or has each of you read the parts you cared about?
- What does it say happens if the two of you cannot agree? Say it from memory, then check.
- Is the chef partner's admission in it already, or is that a future amendment?
- Sideways: what did you assume it said that turned out not to be there?

**Narrowing questions:**
- For each of the five topics, does the agreement speak, stay silent, or half-speak?
- Which of those does Brandon want the founding documents to cite, and which should stay in the agreement only?

**What the book says:** founding documents give every system a common purpose and hold in chaos (p. 72); they cannot contradict the legal instrument beneath them (pp. 71 to 72).

**White-paper default:** none; the white paper is silent on the operating agreement.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Equal-partner operating agreements usually carry a deadlock mechanism of some kind (a named tiebreaker, mediation, or a buy-sell trigger) and provisions for a partner's departure or incapacity; culture documents in well-run companies stay silent on those and point to the legal instrument instead.

**Options:** none to choose; this is a read and a note. The note lists, per topic, what the agreement holds.

**Watch for:** answering from memory and moving on. The note must come from the text.

**A finished answer:** a short note exists (a page at most) with the five topics and what the agreement says on each, including "silent" where it is, and Dominic has confirmed the read.

**Needs agreement from:** Brandon alone writes the note; both seated founders confirm it.

### 2.1.2 Decide Sŏn's mission and the entity it belongs to

**The question:** what is the one sentence that says why this company exists, and is it the company's sentence or the restaurant's?

**Why it matters now:** the mission is the parent of the long-term goals (2.1.3), the principles (2.1.5), every charter (2.1.15), and the orientation every new person gets first (2.1.20, WP p. 18). Vague here and every metric in 2.2 has no parent. Rushed here and the sentence names the cuisine or the horizon before the founders have chosen to (cons. 2).

**Openers:**
- A line cook on their third week asks you at the pass why this place exists. What do you say in one breath?
- The white paper has five sentences that sound like a mission (cons. 2 lists them). Read them aloud. Which one do you actually say to people?
- Sideways: you have been telling the story to the build audience on camera for months. What line do they repeat back to you? Stripe's mission was a phrase people kept quoting until it was simply accepted (pp. 73 to 74).
- Is this a Korean company, or a company whose first restaurant is Korean? Say it both ways and notice which one you flinch at.
- Picture the morning the layer holds something that is not Sŏn. Does the sentence still read true?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Whose sentence is it: the parent company's or the restaurant's? If the company's, what sentence does Sŏn the restaurant hold beneath it?
- Is the cuisine in the company's sentence, one level down, or not written yet?
- Run the four tests on the candidate: uniquely specific, never fully achieved, never asserts the unproven (WP p. 36), not a description of the restaurant alone (WP p. 34). Which does it fail?
- Could a competitor described in the white paper (a portfolio group, a chef's restaurant) hold this sentence? If yes, it is not specific enough.

**What the book says:** a mission states why you exist; it must be descriptive (no other organization could hold it) and aspirational (unlikely ever to be fully achieved); every layer of the company holds a mission that ladders up (pp. 73 to 76).

**White-paper default:** the mission belongs to the company, whose asset is the connective layer, not the restaurant alone (WP p. 34, stated outright); Sŏn is the only brand the customer meets and the parent stays in the background (WP p. 36, stated outright). No default for the sentence itself: the white paper holds a thesis, not a mission (cons. 2).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Stripe's mission was a phrase from an early About page that people kept quoting until it became the mission; she treats that as the normal path (pp. 73 to 74).
- Hospitality groups with a customer-facing brand and a background parent commonly keep the parent's purpose out of the customer's sight; the white paper's own peer example runs this way (WP p. 02).
- Missions that survive years tend to describe an effect on the world rather than the product: Microsoft's "a computer on every desk and in every home" is her example (pp. 74 to 75).

**Options:** (a) the company holds the mission, without the cuisine; the restaurant holds its own sentence beneath it, with the cuisine. Commits Sŏn to two sentences and a ladder, and keeps the company's sentence true when the layer holds more. (b) the company holds the mission and names the cuisine. Commits the company to Korean food across whatever it later holds. (c) Sŏn the restaurant holds the only mission for now; the company's is written when the layer holds a second thing. Commits Sŏn to reopening the sentence at the first expansion. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** a sentence that is really the thesis ("a restaurant is not a room that serves food"), which any reader of the argument could hold; a sentence that asserts the horizon; a sentence Brandon likes but has never said out loud to anyone.

**A finished answer:** one sentence, the entity named, the cuisine question answered either way, all four tests passed out loud, and Brandon can say the restaurant's sentence beneath it (or has parked that as an open item with a closer).

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 2.1.3 Choose the long-term goals

**The question:** which few ambitions, over years, is the company building toward, stated without figures or lagging indicators?

**Why it matters now:** every quarterly and annual goal (2.2, 2.3) ladders to these; she calls them a necessary precursor to goal-setting (p. 77). Every metric 2.2 sets must be able to name its parent here (tasks.md). Skipped, and 2.2 invents goals to hang metrics on. Answered vaguely, and the list is the white paper's thesis restated.

**Openers:**
- Ten years on, the company is exactly what you hoped. Describe that morning. What is true then that is not true today?
- Which of the white paper's big claims would you be embarrassed to still be chasing in year eight? Which would you be proud to still be chasing?
- Sideways: Stripe's goals were written on two pages and unchanged eight years later (p. 77). Of everything in the white paper, what is structure and what is fashion?
- The proof the building exists to deliver, moving a customer from the morning to late night (WP p. 35): is that a goal, or a test of a goal?
- Do you want the concepts on the horizon on this page at all, or should the first year earn the right to write them?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Of the candidates in cons. 3, which are goals, which are metrics that serve a goal (send to 2.2), and which are structural design (send to 4.1)?
- Is each one stated as an outcome rather than a number or a lagging indicator (WP p. 22)?
- Does it fit on one page? If not, what comes off?
- Is the horizon in category terms only, as the white paper does it (WP p. 36), or off the page?

**What the book says:** long-term goals are the bigger ambitions over years; they bolster the mission and are the parent of every shorter goal; Stripe's fit on two pages and did not change (pp. 76 to 77).

**White-paper default:** the three compounding engines (WP p. 06); a company built to run without its founders (WP pp. 05 to 06); one restaurant coherent across the day with dinner protected (WP p. 33); further concepts by category only, gated on proof (WP pp. 36, 38). All stated outright, none labeled as long-term goals; the selection is inferred (cons. 3). The proof that the layer moves a customer from the morning to late night (WP p. 35) is listed in tasks.md as a candidate test of the goals, not a goal; if Brandon wants it as a goal, that is a choice to make on purpose.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Companies that hold long-horizon goals in writing tend to keep them short and stable and let annual plans do the changing; Stripe's two pages are her worked example (p. 77).
- Well-run hospitality companies with an explicit ordering of who comes first (staff, then customers, then community, then suppliers, then investors is the best-known version) treat that ordering as a standing goal rather than a metric. Named example withheld; the pattern is verifiable in a founder's own published account.
- Goals stated as leading conditions (what the organization can do) rather than lagging results (what it earned) are the exception in the restaurant segment, which the white paper says outright (WP p. 22).

**Options:** (a) the white paper's five, selected as listed above, with the horizon in category terms. Commits Sŏn to a goal it cannot yet name concretely and must not assert externally. (b) the same list with the horizon removed; the building's proof opens it later. Commits Sŏn to rewriting the goals at the first expansion. (c) a shorter list: the three engines and the company that runs without its founders, with the day and the proof treated as Sŏn the restaurant's goals one level down. Commits Sŏn to the same ladder chosen in 2.1.2. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** a metric dressed as a goal ("high employee NPS"); a revenue or cover target sneaking in; a list long enough that nobody can recite it.

**A finished answer:** a list Brandon can say from memory, on one page, with no figures and no lagging indicators, each item an outcome, and the horizon either on it in category terms or explicitly off it.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 2.1.4 Decide whether "bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results" is a rule or a description

**The question:** on a real night, when bandwidth and results conflict, does this sequence decide the trade-off?

**Why it matters now:** 2.1.5 depends on it: a rule is a principle with teeth, a description is a belief. It reaches scheduling and covers (4.1), the cadence (2.3), and every conversation about a service period that makes money and consumes the team (2.1.15). Inherited rather than decided, it will be quoted as a rule by staff and treated as a description by the founders, which is the worst of both.

**Openers:**
- Tell me about a night on a floor somewhere where the results won and the people paid for it. What did you do, and what did you wish you had done?
- Saturday, two servers out, a full book. Under this rule, what happens to the book?
- A late-night period is making money and eating the team. Who says stop, and what does stop look like?
- Would you let a lead quote this back at you on a night when you are the one pushing for results?
- Sideways: what would make you regret having made it a rule?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Rule or description? Say which, then say the sentence a lead could use on a Saturday.
- If a rule: are the two named consequences accepted (reduce covers rather than thin the floor; restructure a period that consumes bandwidth before defending it on results), or only one, or neither?
- Who holds the authority to invoke it in the moment, and what does invoking it look like?
- What would count as bandwidth being gone, before the metric layer exists to measure it?

**What the book says:** principles must be deliverable and connected to how the company actually operates; a value with no connection to operation does not resonate (pp. 77 to 78).

**White-paper default:** it is a rule: "This is mechanical, not aspirational" (WP p. 17, stated outright; the sequence itself WP p. 12). As a rule it decides trade-offs: bandwidth wins over results when they conflict (cons. 4).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Some well-known companies hold an explicit ordering (employees first, then customers, then shareholders) and say outright that it is a decision rule, not a slogan; a founder of a major airline and a founder of a large New York restaurant group have each said this in print. The test they apply is whether the ordering has ever cost them something.
- Restaurants that protect the team on a short night most often do it by capping covers or closing a section, not by lowering the standard; the mechanism is decided in advance, not on the night.

**Options:** (a) a rule, with both consequences accepted. Commits Sŏn to sometimes turning away revenue and to restructuring a profitable period. (b) a rule for staffing (reduce covers rather than thin the floor) and a description for periods (a period's bandwidth cost is weighed, not decisive). Commits Sŏn to a partial rule with a named boundary. (c) a description of how health produces results. Commits Sŏn to nothing on the night; the sentence is a belief. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** choosing the rule because it sounds right, without saying the Saturday sentence; choosing the description and then writing the principles as if it were a rule.

**A finished answer:** Brandon has said which reading holds, has said the sentence a lead uses on the night, has accepted or declined each consequence by name, and can name who invokes it.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 2.1.5 Write the principles, version one

**The question:** which of the white paper's tenets become Sŏn's principles for version one, which are commitments, which are operating rules, and what does each ask of a person?

**Why it matters now:** hiring scores against the principles and feedback references them (3.x, 5.x); the commitments are what recruiting states before the mechanics are set (3.x, 5.5); the honest description for candidates (2.1.19) pairs each with its cost. Answered vaguely, the list is a set of beliefs nobody can hire or give feedback against. Skipped, and 5.5 builds compensation mechanics against commitments nobody has written down.

**Openers:**
- Which of these tenets (cons. 4 lists them) have you already had to enforce during the build, on yourself or on Dominic?
- Pick one and tell me a moment from a room you worked in where you saw it done, or broken.
- If a lead had to give hard feedback using only this list, which items would they reach for?
- Which of these could a customer see from their chair, without being told?
- Sideways: what would you defend to a skeptical chef, and what would you let them cut?

**Narrowing questions:**
- For each tenet: principle (a behavior anyone can observe in a service), commitment (something Sŏn pays for or refuses to do), or operating rule (a mechanism)? Cons. 4 gives the test.
- Which three or four would Brandon keep if he could keep only those?
- Which decision in the record does each trace to? If none, is it a decision the founders are making now?
- Is the people and compensation commitment list complete enough for Dominic to review before 5.5 builds against it?

**What the book says:** principles establish the culture; they must be relevant, believable, enduring, and deliverable; draft from notable moments, seek input, have the founder review, revisit every year or two; the test that matters is whether they show in how the company hires, rewards, and leads (pp. 77 to 79). Stripe's set as a worked example (pp. 145 to 147).

**White-paper default:** version one is drawn from the white paper's stated tenets (cons. 4, by page), because Sŏn has decisions and no moments. "No hierarchy of importance" is called an operating principle in the white paper's own words (WP p. 20, stated outright). People and compensation commitments (labor as asset, pay for what matters, compensation in the menu price shared with the kitchen, benefits from the start; WP pp. 16, 19 to 20) are stated outright; treating them as commitments here and leaving every mechanism to 5.5 is inferred.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Amazon's leadership principles are each a short name plus a sentence on the behavior, and they are used directly in interviews; Stripe's follow the same form (pp. 145 to 147).
- Netflix's culture document is written to say what the company will not do and what it costs to work there, and has been publicly rewritten more than once.
- One large New York restaurant group hires on a stated split between emotional skills and technical skills; the principle is expressed as a hiring rule, which is her deliverability test in practice.
- Zappos published its values with unedited employee commentary; the pattern is that the founder's draft was tested against what employees actually said.

**Options:** (a) a short set of principles (five to seven behaviors), a separate commitment list, and operating rules left to the operating system (2.2). Commits Sŏn to three registers and to keeping each in its place. (b) one flat list of everything the white paper holds. Commits Sŏn to a list hiring cannot score against. (c) principles only, with commitments folded into the founding document's people section and no rules at all. Commits Sŏn to restating the commitments wherever recruiting needs them. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** aspirational principles ("we value humility") that name no behavior; a list so long it becomes the white paper again; mechanics (a percentage, a point weight) leaking into a commitment.

**A finished answer:** Brandon has sorted every candidate tenet into one of the three registers or out, named the principles he would keep, traced each to a decision, and named what each asks of a person in one line; the commitment list is ready for Dominic; version one is marked pre-opening with the chef partner invited to add.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders on the list; Dominic specifically on the commitment list, which is his own action (2.1.6) and does not need Brandon in the room; the chef partner invited once seated.

### 2.1.7 Decide who owns the OS surface

**The question:** who owns what the OS surface must do, and who owns how it is built and run?

**Why it matters now:** three assignments in the white paper land on one object (Brandon's "operating systems," Dominic's "technology build" and "data architecture," and the proprietary "OS surface and agent layer"; WP pp. 03, 05, 21 to 22). Both seat descriptions (2.1.8, 2.1.9) need the seam drawn or marked open; the leads need to know who to call when the surface is wrong (4.1). This is exactly the both-own-it failure the white paper predicts (WP p. 11), findable in its own text.

**Openers:**
- A lead sees something on the OS surface that is wrong at the start of a shift. Who do they call, and who is allowed to change it?
- What have you and Dominic argued about during the build that turned out to be a seam nobody had drawn? Where did it land?
- Sideways: name a request you would make of the surface that Dominic would say is not what it is for. Who wins that, and how?
- Where does "what the surface must do" stop and "how it is built" begin, for something like what a lead sees at the start of a shift?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Option (a), (b), or open with a closer and a date?
- If (a): what is the path for a request the specification did not anticipate, and who decides whether it is specification or build?
- Does the agent layer follow the same rule as the surface, or is it its own seam?

**What the book says:** the charter question is who is responsible for what work, and to what end (p. 83).

**White-paper default:** none stated. The white paper's own pattern, authority with the closest competent owner (WP p. 08), fits a split between specification and build (cons. 5); that fit is inferred.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Product-versus-engineering seams in software companies are usually resolved by giving one seat the specification (what it must do) and the other the build, with a stated path for requests the specification did not anticipate; that is cons. 5 option (a).
- Two-founder companies that last tend to write the domain split down early and name the tiebreak per domain; the founder who owns the domain decides after consulting the other.

**Options:** (a) Brandon owns the specification, Dominic owns the build and its operation, with a request path. Commits each half to its closest owner and pre-states the boundary. (b) one seat owns it outright. Commits the other seat to asking for everything. (c) marked open with a closer and a date. Commits nothing yet. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** resolving it by goodwill ("we'll work it out"); a request path that is "ask Brandon"; treating the ruling as Dominic's alone because the object is software.

**A finished answer:** a sentence for each half (what it must do; how it is built and run) with an owner, plus the request path; or an explicit "open" with a closer and a date.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
