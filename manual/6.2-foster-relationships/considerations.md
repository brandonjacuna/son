# 6.2 Foster relationships: considerations

Context for the working session. The book's section (pp. 491 to 498) is written for a manager with a manager above, peers beside, and founders somewhere up the chain. At Sŏn the founders are the top of the structure, the leads are the only people who hold a founder as "their direct lead," and there is no chain. White paper cited as WP p. N. Nothing here describes how any founder relates to anyone as a person; the digest's rulings on that hold, and the earlier work's proposals appear as options.

## 1. What the white paper holds, and where it is silent

The structure: authority lives in the domain, anchor points are "small, specific, explicitly defined, interlocking," and there is no top or bottom, only a center where information converges and an edge where it is most sensitive (WP pp. 07 to 09). The two leads are parallel co-leads "serving the same center, the culinary and conceptual vision," and neither reports to the other (WP p. 10). The candidate sheet names "their direct lead" (WP p. 18), which for a lead is a partner.

The founders: two own everything outside the kitchen; the chef partner owns the culinary vision as an equal founding partner (WP p. 03). The white paper describes them as a founding team whose domains are complementary by design, and says nothing about how they work together day to day.

Feedback and change: "feedback runs both directions" and team members weigh in on management effectiveness (WP p. 19); employees are the users and the tools evolve on feedback loops (WP p. 17); the feedback channel syncs to the execution system and the loop is closed (WP p. 22); "Sŏn has done it too, which is how the team knows the trap" (WP p. 17).

Outside relationships: the document is "a diligence document, not an offer" (WP p. 02); the build is documented and the audience is invited into real decisions (WP p. 29); crisis communication is transparency first (WP p. 31); creators are offered access over cash (WP p. 31); the founders "would rather hand over the machine in full" than ask for faith (WP p. 39).

Silent on: how the partners treat each other; what a lead may expect from a founder; how an idea reaches a founder; whether a lead asks a founder what matters to them; a founder as the subject of a concern; a founder's peers, mentors, or coach; whether the team hears a public statement before the public.

## 2. The partners with each other (6.2.2)

The book's advice to a report about their manager (never surprise them with late bad news; help them be their best; think about what you appreciate in a report and apply it upward; get to know each other personally, pp. 491 to 492) has no vertical at Sŏn to run on, so it runs sideways between partners. Its peer advice (honor commitments, listen, share information, raise a problem directly before it escalates, p. 494) applies as written.

The earlier work turned each into a mechanism rather than a conversation: the working-with-me exchange (1.1.10) as a seam document between people who rarely watch each other work; the domain read (2.3.4) as monthly written questions on each other's pages; "never surprise" as the domain page's weekly state and the live-risk line's daily state (4.1.31); "help your manager be their best" as a prompt naming what each partner needs from the other's domain, feeding the asks list. The consideration for Brandon is whether mechanisms are the whole of it or whether the partners also owe each other something the house does not record.

Options:
- (a) The weekly state and the daily state are the whole no-surprises rule. Commits Sŏn to the pages being kept, and to nothing else.
- (b) In addition, a partner hears a problem inside the other's domain from that partner first, in writing, within 4.6.3's response time, before it reaches a lead, the team, or the public. Commits Sŏn to a rule about order, which 1.2.1's hard-things rule and 4.8.6's record rule should be checked against.
- (c) In addition, a standing personal check between the partners that the house never records. Commits the partners to time the cadence does not show, which is the book's "get to know each other personally" and is theirs.

How others have handled it: General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Co-founder pairs that last commonly keep a standing meeting that is only about the two of them, separate from the operating review; the ones that fail commonly describe the relationship as having been left to proximity.

## 3. Conduct inside the weekly review (6.2.3) and the edge of a domain (6.2.4)

**Conduct.** 2.3.4 fixed the review's day, length, and agenda. The earlier work identified four things the agenda does not fix and that a partner's working style plausibly should: whether a domain page is read before the review or presented in it; whether a required consultation happens in writing or in the room; whether a dissent on a reserved-class decision is written or said; and who holds the clock. Its proposal was to set these from each partner's working-with-me document at each gate review, from documents the team never reads. The alternative is to set them once by agreement, or leave them to the chair. The annual block (2.3.30) already has the founders' documents on the table; the gate review (2.3.26) is the more frequent point.

**The edge.** The book's "two and two" (know the roles two levels above and below; check that incentives are aligned up and down, pp. 492 to 493) has no levels to count. Its Sŏn form is "two domains out": each founder knows the decisions nearest the edge of their own domain that belong to another seat. The seat descriptions' seams field (2.1.8, 2.1.9, 2.1.11) and the decision-rights register (2.2.4) already hold the edges; the question is whether a founder studies the far side of theirs.

Options for the map: (a) the seams field is the map. Commits Sŏn to the edges as written once. (b) Each founder writes the handful of decisions nearest their edge and whose they are, and the two maps are compared, with the register updated where they disagree. Commits Sŏn to finding the disagreements before a lead does. (c) Made once at the mutual-awareness conversation (1.1.10) and not kept.

The incentives half is separate and mostly counsel's: whether the founders' incentives (the operating agreement) and the team's (the pool, 5.5.7; advancement, 4.1.11) pull the same way at the seams. The white paper's transparency design puts seat wages and weights in the open (5.5.20); founder pay is outside this program entirely and stays there. Any misalignment found is a counsel question or a parameters-register row, never a figure here.

## 4. The paired relationship between a founder and a lead (6.2.5)

The book lists what a good manager gives a report: unblocking, resource advocacy, context, help with priorities, and development; and it says leverage runs both ways, with "managing up" meaning getting the best results for the team, not politics (pp. 491 to 492). The white paper gives every hire "their direct lead" on the sheet (WP p. 18), and for a lead that is a partner. 1.3.2 decided whether "manager" is a word the house uses; 1.3's session parked the question of what the paired relationship is called.

The earlier work held that this is the only manager-shaped relationship a lead has, avoided naming it, and gave each of the book's five gifts a mechanism: unblocking through the path 4.5.10 set; resource advocacy through the partners' asks list; context through 2.2.11's channels; priorities through the goal pages (2.2.27); development through 2.2.14 and the career conversation (4.4.3). What runs up, in its reading, is the lead's own written account of how the house is running, "naming mechanisms and never a person," which it treated as the whole of managing up.

Options for the name: (a) none; the house says "the partner who holds your check-in." Commits Sŏn to a phrase where a word would do, and to a candidate reading it on the sheet. (b) A plain word the founders choose. Commits Sŏn to using it consistently from the first posting. (c) The book's "manager," if 1.3.2 allowed it. Commits Sŏn to the word the white paper avoids.

What the lead can count on, and what runs up, are lists to confirm rather than options; the consideration is whether every item on the book's list has a real home, and whether "the lead's own read on how the house is running" is a thing the house asks for (in what form, how often) or a thing a lead may offer.

How others have handled it: General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Restaurants with two co-equal heads (a general manager and an executive chef reporting separately to an owner) are common, and the owner-to-head relationship there is usually unnamed and unwritten; that is the gap the lead's page (6.2.13) fills.

## 5. How an idea reaches the founders (6.2.6)

The book's three principles for a manager working with founders are about patience and form: check the fruit (test an idea's ripeness; expect many to stay in the bag; it is usually better to wait than to force something the company will reject); build the Camry (ask for the lightest version; start with the tools the team already uses; her own mistake was proposing two systems by acronym instead of explaining the need); and propose a change as a pilot with what is changing, how success is assessed, and when permanence is decided (pp. 495 to 498). It also says the manager's job is to judge what rate of change the founders and the organization can absorb (p. 495).

At Sŏn most of that is already mechanism: the change channel (2.2.11); the first-quarter freeze and the hold log (4.3.5, 4.3.20); the test-first step, if 5.3.10 adopted it; the entry test a rhythm must pass (2.3.3). The earlier work went further: a lead's idea to change a mechanism enters the change log and, during the first quarter, is held for the reset with a date; an idea to add a mechanism must pass a four-part entry test (bandwidth cost, benefit, owner, record), which is the Camry rule made a gate; and a lead never brings an idea to a founder in a room, because nothing is assigned or escalated in a room and the founder speaks last if present (4.5.7). It read "check the fruit" as right in patience and wrong in form: a lead should not have to judge a founder's readiness, because the rate of change is written down (4.3.5, 2.3.30).

Options:
- (a) The channel and the hold log are the whole path; a founder never receives an idea in a room. Commits Sŏn to every idea having a written answer within a window, and to founders who may hear about a lead's idea only by reading.
- (b) The book's path: the lead judges ripeness and brings it to their partner at the check-in as a pilot with the three parts. Commits Sŏn to the check-in carrying ideas, which 2.3.9 may have refused, and to a lead reading a founder's mood.
- (c) Both: the check-in for the conversation, the channel for the record. Commits Sŏn to the record being made even when the conversation went well.

What a founder owes back is the part the book leaves to the founder's character: a written answer within a window; a reason when the answer is no; never a silent bag. The white paper's line that the loop gets closed because "feedback without follow-through breeds cynicism" (WP p. 22) is the default.

## 6. How a lead learns what matters to the founders (6.2.7)

The book: understand founders' values the way you would a report's, including the trade-offs they will make; if they have not written their values down, asking them to could start the founding-documents work; offsites and 1:1s both work (pp. 497 to 498). At Sŏn the founding documents exist or are in progress (2.1.5, 2.1.17), the trade-offs list is 2.2.9, and the why session (3.3.35) is spoken by a founder to every cohort.

The earlier work drew a line worth putting to Brandon plainly: the company's values are not the founders' personal values and are not derived from them; the company's intent lives in the founding document; a lead never asks a founder about their values directly, in a check-in or an offsite; where the document is silent on a trade-off the house is about to make, that becomes a written question, not a conversation; and the founders' personal values stay in a private prompt exchanged among the partners only. That is a strong refusal of the book's most personal advice, and it is presented as one option.

Options:
- (a) The founding document and the trade-offs list are the answer; a lead's question about a silent trade-off becomes a written question on the partners' page. Commits Sŏn to keeping 2.2.9 current, and to a lead never hearing a founder say what matters to them except through the document and the why session.
- (b) The why session plus a founder-and-lead conversation inside lead onboarding (3.3.45), unrecorded. Commits Sŏn to the book's path and to a conversation the house cannot check happened well.
- (c) Each founder's issued working-with-me document (1.1.5) carries what matters to them at work, and the lead reads it. Commits Sŏn to 1.1.5's issued version holding a values prompt, which 1.1.2 may have kept private.

## 7. The founders and the whole team (6.2.8)

The white paper gives the team's relationships to the people closest to them: the Maitre d owns team development (WP p. 10), the server owns the table (WP p. 25), the mentor is a cultural steward (WP p. 18). The founders' standing relationship with every person is the why session (3.3.6), which the earlier work described as delivering the concept's voice and not a founder's personality. Everything else about a founder and a team member is governed by rules already set: the destinations (4.5.11, 4.5.17), where feedback about your own lead goes (5.3.4), what is never a source for a hypothesis (5.1.3), the floor rule (6.1.8).

The earlier work's refusal: a founder holds no person's check-in except a lead's by default, reads no page but a lead's, and is never the destination for a concern about a person; any observation about a person goes to that person's check-in holder. Its reason is the white paper's: belonging is to people, and the people are the ones at the edge, not at the center (WP pp. 09, 25).

Options:
- (a) The refusal as written. Commits Sŏn to founders who cannot be asked about a person, and to leads who hear everything about their own people.
- (b) Through dinner's first quarter a founder holds more, by name, with hand-over dates on 4.4.18. Commits Sŏn to a stated exception, and to the read in 6.1.4 seeing a founder holding past the date.
- (c) A founder is a standing destination on the destinations page for a named class (candidate: a concern about a lead, if 5.3.4 sent it there). Commits Sŏn to the class being short and public.

## 8. A concern about a founder's conduct (6.2.9)

The book's peer advice is to raise a problem with a fellow manager directly before it escalates (p. 494). The harder case is the one the book does not reach: a concern about a founder's conduct in a house where no one stands above the founders. 5.2.2 and 5.2.3 define the serious breach; 5.8.3 names the record custodian, the second reader, and counsel's line; 5.3.4 names the destination when the check-in is not safe. None of them was written with a founder as the subject.

The earlier work's position: the non-negotiables bind a founder exactly as they bind anyone, because a house whose founders were exempt would make everyone else disposable at the founders' hands. Its path: a concern naming a founder goes the same day to both leads and every non-subject partner; the read is held jointly by the two leads, with counsel where required, and by no partner, since any other partner is the subject's peer in every reserved-class decision; the founder hears what was raised from the other partner, with a reader present, before anything is written; if the finding touches a stated non-negotiable, the two leads write a closed-form record, since a founder has no personal file; everything past that point (staying, terms) belongs to the operating agreement and counsel. It offered, as an option and not a rule, that while a read is open the founder is off the floor and out of any room the concern would reach, as the house's protection for everyone else in it.

Options:
- (a) The path above. Commits Sŏn to two leads reading their own principals, which is a heavy thing to ask of an employee, and to counsel being close.
- (b) A destination outside the house (counsel, or a named outside party), with the house designing nothing beyond the destination. Commits Sŏn to the leads having somewhere to go that is not a founder, and to the process being counsel's.
- (c) The other partner holds it alone. Commits Sŏn to a peer reading a peer, which the earlier work refused because the two are tied in every reserved decision.

Counsel, for 6.3.7: how a founder as the subject of a concern is handled beyond what the house designs; whether the operating agreement holds removal provisions this path must defer to; what record, if any, may be kept and by whom.

How others have handled it: General practice, not Sŏn-specific. In companies with a board, a concern about a founder goes to the board or its committee; in small partnerships without one, it goes to the operating agreement's provisions and to counsel, and the usual failure is that nothing is written down until it is a dispute. Sŏn's position is the second, with a path drawn in advance.

## 9. The founders' outside circle (6.2.10)

The book: identify leaders you admire and compare notes; some become the people you call to pressure-test an idea or practice a hard conversation (pp. 493 to 494); the friendships from hard moments are what she still draws on (p. 491). The white paper's founders name the operators they admire and treat the best rooms as a university (WP pp. 02, 04). 5.1.12 decides who tests hypotheses about each founder's work; 3.3.23 decided whether a lead gets a coach and sent the founders' own question here.

The earlier work said these relationships are the founders' own and the house records none of them, and it declined a coach for the founders on the same reasoning it declined one for a lead. Both are options here.

Options:
- (a) The founders' own, unrecorded; 5.1.12's answer stands for coaching. Commits Sŏn to nothing, and each founder to finding their own circle.
- (b) A coach per founder. Commits Sŏn to a relationship that reads a founder from outside; who pays is Dominic's and the operating agreement's, since financials are not a source here.
- (c) A standing peer relationship with other operators, named on the knowledge-transfer list only where the house depends on it (the vendor who returns one founder's call is 1.1.12's example). Commits Sŏn to distinguishing a founder's friend from a house dependency.

How others have handled it: General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Executive coaching for founders is common in venture-backed companies and uncommon in independent restaurants; peer groups of operators (formal and informal) are common in both.

## 10. Outside relationships the house depends on (6.2.11)

Four relationships sit on the founders' seats and touch the team: the raise (Dominic's domain; only its public-voice component is Brandon's seat's; nothing about amount, terms, investors, or dates is a source here, WP p. 02); the public voice under the content framework (WP pp. 29 to 31); creator and brand partners, offered access over cash (WP p. 31); and the chef partner search (3.1.19). The book's advice to share your team's mission with key partners and to seek out those who can create or prevent obstacles (pp. 493 to 494) is about a manager's stakeholders; at Sŏn these are the founders' outside faces, and the question is what each owes the team first.

The earlier work proposed an inward-first rule: a public statement about the house reaches the internal team before or at the moment it reaches the public, and a founder speaking a crisis aloud to the team before it is written is itself a breach of 4.8.6's record rule. 1.2.2 decided what the team hears before and after a decision; 4.8.20 built the whole-team statement form; 3.1.16 decided what the documented build may show of a hiring search.

Options:
- (a) Inward-first for every public statement about the house. Commits Sŏn to the content framework's speed (WP p. 31) meeting a hold whenever the team has not yet been told.
- (b) The content framework's tiers are the whole rule; the team learns as the public does. Commits Sŏn to a team that may see a statement on a phone before hearing it at a brief.
- (c) Inward-first for a named class only (a crisis, a structure change, a departure, a change in the plan for a service period). Commits Sŏn to keeping the class explicit.

Two open rows for 6.1.17: who holds the founder consultation and the leadership review of content (WP p. 31); and whether these relationships are dependencies on the knowledge-transfer list (1.1.12).

## 11. Counsel

Logged for 6.3.7: how a founder as the subject of a concern is handled beyond what the house designs; whether the operating agreement's removal, deadlock, and incapacity provisions constrain 6.2.9's path; what record may be kept of a finding about a founder and by whom; whether the founders' incentives under the agreement and the team's under the pool are aligned in any way counsel would flag (6.2.4).

## 12. Where Sŏn may diverge from the book

- The book's relationships are vertical. Sŏn's are between domains, and every one the book describes as a conversation is, in the earlier work's reading, a mechanism first. Brandon may keep some as conversations (6.2.2 option c, 6.2.7 option b).
- The book's "check the fruit" asks a manager to read a founder's readiness. Sŏn writes the rate of change down (4.3.5, 2.3.30) so no one has to.
- The book's manager learns founders' values by asking. Sŏn's earlier work refused the asking and pointed at the founding document. That refusal is strong enough to deserve Brandon's own ruling (6.2.7).
- The book has no case for a concern about a founder. Sŏn draws the path in advance (6.2.9), because the alternative is nothing until it is a dispute.
- The book's outside relationships are the manager's to cultivate. Sŏn distinguishes the founders' own (unrecorded) from the ones the house depends on (on a list, with a rule).
