# 6.2 Foster relationships: working session

## 1. Orientation

This chunk covers the founders' relationships: with each other, with the two leads who hold a partner as their direct lead, with the whole team, and with the people outside the house the business depends on. The book's section (pp. 491 to 498) is written for a manager with a manager above and founders up the chain. At Sŏn the founders are the top, so every relationship the book describes has to be rebuilt sideways or downward, and most of them already have a mechanism doing part of the work. The session decides what is mechanism, what is conversation, and what the house never records.

Working rules for the room: Brandon decides, the facilitator never does. Options and defaults are starting points. Nothing he says about how he and Dominic get on, or about either of them as people, gets recorded; the note that a topic came up is enough. If he starts describing what he needs from Dominic personally, that is material for the mutual-awareness conversation (1.1.10) and his own document, not this record. Brain dumps go to `notes/YYYY-MM-DD-<topic>.md` in his words before they are funneled.

Upstream, already decided or in progress: the working-with-me exchange and the mutual-awareness conversation (1.1.5, 1.1.10); the partners' rule for hard things (1.2.1); what the team hears before and after a decision (1.2.2); whether "manager" is a word (1.3.2); how the partners decide (2.1.13); the weekly review, domain read, and partners' page (2.3.4, 2.3.6); who holds each lead's check-in and monthly conversation (2.2.5, 2.3.14); the founders' norms for working apart (4.6.3); the unblocking path and the destinations (4.5.10, 4.5.11, 4.5.17); the senior person speaks last (4.5.7); the change channel and the first-quarter freeze (2.2.11, 4.3.5, 4.3.20); the serious-breach definition and the record custodian (5.2.3, 5.8.3); the floor rule (6.1.8); the founders' training gates; who coaches each founder by hypothesis (5.1.12).

Who else must agree: both seated founders on every decision; the chef partner once seated on 6.2.2, 6.2.3, and 6.2.9; both leads review the lead's page (6.2.13) once they exist; counsel on 6.2.9 and 6.2.12.

Why it sits here: the book puts relationships after time and energy because the second half of "manage yourself" is not doing it alone (p. 491). At Sŏn the order also runs the other way: the founders' relationships with the leads are what the handover list (4.4.18) and the floor rule (6.1.8) depend on to hold.

## 3. Briefs

### 6.2.2 Decide what the partners owe each other beyond the weekly review: no surprises, and the ask

**The question:** the weekly review and the domain read exist. Beyond them, is there a rule between the partners about hearing bad news first, and is there anything the partners owe each other that the house does not record?

**Why it matters now:** the first surprise between partners will happen before the first hire, in the build. The book's "never surprise your manager" (p. 492) has no manager to run to; it runs sideways or nowhere. And 1.2.1 set how a hard thing is raised; this is about the order in which a problem travels.

**Openers:**
- The book says think about what you appreciate in a report and apply it upward (p. 492). There is no upward. What do you appreciate in a partner, and is any of it a mechanism?
- Sideways: the white paper calls a settled-looking answer to an open question "the tell" (WP p. 22). Between the two of you, what is the tell that something is being held back?
- Picture a live risk in Dominic's domain that a lead hears about before you do. Is that a failure? Of what?
- Counterexample: two partners who only ever meet at the weekly review, and it works. What would they be missing, if anything?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The weekly state and the daily state as the whole rule; plus an order rule (the other partner first, in writing, within 4.6.3's time); plus a personal check the house never records?
- Does the order rule sit inside 1.2.1's hard-things rule or beside it?
- Is "what I need from your domain" an ask with an owner and a date on the partners' page (2.3.4), or a conversation?

**What the book says:** never surprise your manager with late bad news; help them be their best; get to know each other personally; a good peer honors commitments, listens, shares information, and raises a problem directly before it escalates (pp. 491 to 494).

**White-paper default:** non-overlapping domains (WP p. 03); open items named rather than hidden (WP pp. 15, 22). Silent on the partners day to day.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Co-founder pairs that last commonly keep a standing meeting that is only about the two of them, separate from the operating review.

**Options:** (a) the pages are the rule; (b) plus an order rule; (c) plus an unrecorded personal check. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** an order rule that quietly becomes a rule about tone; a personal check that becomes an agenda item; anything about how the two of them actually get on.

**A finished answer:** the rule in one sentence; the ask confirmed as an entry; the extension to the chef partner named.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner once seated.

### 6.2.3 Decide how the partners' working-with-me documents change how the weekly review is run

**The question:** the review's agenda is fixed (2.3.4). Four things inside it are not: read-before or present-in; consultation in writing or in the room; dissent written or said; who holds the clock. Are these set from the partners' documents, set once, or left to the chair?

**Why it matters now:** the review starts before the first hire and its habits set fast. The documents exist or will (1.1.7, 1.1.8); this is the one place they are allowed to change something the house runs, and the earlier work wanted it stated so the agenda itself never moves.

**Openers:**
- Read the four variables. For each, do you already know what you would want? Do you know what Dominic would?
- Sideways: the white paper's one full cadence (a daily pulse, a weekly read, a monthly review, WP p. 31) is fixed in rhythm and content and silent on conduct. Is conduct a thing to fix at all?
- If the two of you disagree on a reserved-class decision, is the dissent better on the page or in the room? Whose preference wins if you differ on that?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Per variable: from the documents, set once, or the chair's?
- Revisited when: each gate review (2.3.26), the annual block (2.3.30), or not?
- Confirm the agenda is unchanged and versioned.

**What the book says:** offer constructive feedback on how the two of you could work better together (p. 492).

**White-paper default:** a fixed cadence with fixed content (WP p. 31). Silent on conduct.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Written meeting norms that include "read the pre-read before, do not present it" are a common practice in technology companies; letting participants' stated working styles set the norm is less common and is what makes this Sŏn's own.

**Options:** per variable, from the documents, set once, or the chair's; no packaged set. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** the agenda changing under the name of conduct; a variable set from one partner's document over the other's; Brandon stating his own preference in the room, which is his document's.

**A finished answer:** the four variables each with a source; the revisit point; the agenda confirmed.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 6.2.4 Decide whether each founder maps the decisions nearest the edge of their domain, and checks the incentives along it

**The question:** the book's "two and two" has no levels here. Does each founder write down the handful of decisions nearest their domain's edge that belong to another seat, and does anyone check that the founders' incentives and the team's pull the same way at the seams?

**Why it matters now:** the seams are where a lead first meets a disagreement between founders (2.2.3). The seat descriptions hold the edges as each founder drew them; whether the two drawings match is the thing to find before a lead does.

**Openers:**
- Name the decision nearest the edge of your domain that is Dominic's. Now name the one nearest his edge that is yours. Would he name the same two?
- Sideways: the white paper says the anchor points are "small, specific, explicitly defined, interlocking" (WP p. 09). Interlocking is a claim about the edges. Where has it been tested?
- Ron Williams's second half (pp. 492 to 493) is incentives. The team's pay is on a transparent surface (5.5.20); the founders' is under the agreement. Where could those pull apart?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The seams field is the map; each founder writes the edge decisions and the maps are compared; or once at 1.1.10 and not kept?
- Where the two maps disagree: 2.2.4 updated, or 2.2.3's cross-domain rule handles it live?
- The incentives check: a counsel question, a parameters-register row, or nothing?

**What the book says:** know the roles two levels above and below you; check whether incentives up and down the chain are aligned (pp. 492 to 493).

**White-paper default:** authority lives in the domain; anchor points interlock (WP pp. 07 to 09). The seams field (2.1.8) and the register (2.2.4) hold the edges.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Partnerships commonly discover seam disagreements in the first dispute rather than in advance; a written decision-rights register is the standard remedy and Sŏn has one.

**Options:** (a) the seams field; (b) the edge map, compared; (c) once and not kept. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** the incentives check drifting into founder pay, which is never recorded here; a map that grows into a second register.

**A finished answer:** the map's form or its absence; where disagreements go; the incentives question placed.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 6.2.5 Decide the paired relationship between a founder and a lead: its name, what the lead can count on, and what runs up

**The question:** each lead holds a partner as their direct lead (WP p. 18). What is that relationship called, what does the lead get from it, and what does the partner get back?

**Why it matters now:** a lead candidate reads it on the sheet at the first interview (3.1.27). 1.3 parked the name. The book's five things a good manager gives (unblocking, advocacy, context, priorities, development, pp. 491 to 492) each have a candidate mechanism; whether every one has a real home is what a lead will test in their first month.

**Openers:**
- A candidate for Operations Lead asks at the first interview, "who do I report to?" What is the answer in one sentence?
- Sideways: the white paper avoids "manager" and gives the leads "their direct lead" (WP p. 18). For the leads themselves the direct lead is you. Does the word you chose in 1.3.2 fit here?
- Run the book's list: unblocking, resources, context, priorities, development. For each, where does a lead go at Sŏn? If the answer is "to me," is that a mechanism or a person?
- What does the lead owe back that is not politics? The book says "the best results for your team" (p. 492). What form does that take here?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The name: none, a plain word, or the book's word if 1.3.2 allowed it?
- The five promises, each with a mechanism and a home; any without one is sent back to its chunk.
- What runs up: the upward half (5.4.13), the "what's in my way" (4.2.12), and whether a lead's own read on how the house is running is asked for, in what form, how often.
- 5.9 told; the sheet (3.1.27) can state it.

**What the book says:** a good manager unblocks, advocates, provides context, helps set priorities, and develops you; leverage runs both ways; managing up is getting the best results, not being political (pp. 491 to 492).

**White-paper default:** parallel co-leads serving the same center; "their direct lead" on the sheet (WP pp. 10, 18).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Rooms with a general manager and an executive chef each reporting to an owner usually leave the owner relationship unnamed; the ambiguity is where the "who is really in charge" politics the white paper names (WP p. 11) tend to start.

**Options:** for the name: (a) none; (b) a plain word; (c) the book's word. The promises and what runs up are lists to confirm. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** a name that implies reporting in the load-bearing sense; a promise on the list whose only home is a founder; "what runs up" becoming a report on the founder.

**A finished answer:** the name; the five promises with homes; what runs up and in what form; the sheet's sentence; 5.9 told.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 6.2.6 Decide how a lead brings an idea to change or add a mechanism, and how the founders answer

**The question:** when a lead thinks a mechanism should change, or a new one should exist, how does that reach the founders, and what do the founders owe back?

**Why it matters now:** the leads arrive before opening into a house whose mechanisms were built without them. Their first ideas will come fast. The channel exists (2.2.11), the first quarter is frozen (4.3.5), and the hold log exists (4.3.20); this decides whether the founders are reached through those or around them.

**Openers:**
- The book's Stripe story: she proposed two systems by acronym, and Patrick Collison asked why (pp. 496 to 497). Which side of that conversation are you on now?
- Sideways: the white paper says the tools evolve on continuous feedback loops and "Sŏn has done it too" (WP p. 17). A lead's idea is that loop. What does it hit first at Sŏn: a channel, a check-in, or you in a hallway?
- "Check the fruit" asks a lead to judge whether you are ready to hear something (p. 496). Do you want a lead reading your readiness, or would you rather the calendar said when?
- A lead asks for the Escalade. What is the Camry, and who decides it is enough?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The channel and the hold log only; the check-in as a pilot proposal; or both?
- The entry test for a new mechanism: bandwidth cost, benefit, owner, record? Confirm or amend.
- What the founders owe back: a written answer in a window; a reason for a no; never a silent bag?
- The rate of change: 4.3.5 and 2.3.30 answer it, not a founder's mood?

**What the book says:** check the fruit; build the Camry; propose a pilot with what changes, how success is assessed, and when permanence is decided; founders question assumptions by nature and the manager judges the rate of change (pp. 495 to 498).

**White-paper default:** employees are the users; the loop is closed (WP pp. 17, 22).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. A written change proposal with a pilot, a success measure, and a decision date is standard practice in operations-heavy companies; in restaurants the same idea usually travels by conversation and dies there.

**Options:** (a) the channel is the whole path; (b) the book's path at the check-in; (c) both. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** ideas that reach a founder in a room and get a yes there; a window with no owner; the first quarter's freeze used as a reason to never answer.

**A finished answer:** the path; the entry test; the founders' obligation back; the rate-of-change answer pointed at the calendar.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 6.2.7 Decide how a lead learns what matters to the founders

**The question:** the book says understand the founders' values the way you would a report's, and ask them if they have not written them down (pp. 497 to 498). At Sŏn, does a lead learn what matters from the founding document and the trade-offs list, from a conversation, or from the founders' issued documents?

**Why it matters now:** the leads' first hard call will be a trade-off the founding document does not cover. Whether they call a founder or write a question is set by this decision. The earlier work refused the conversation outright; that refusal needs Brandon's own ruling.

**Openers:**
- A lead has to choose tonight between a slower service and a table turned; the principles are silent. What should they do: call you, decide, or write it down for the review?
- Sideways: the white paper puts the mission and values before operational training for every hire (WP p. 18). Is that enough for a lead, or does a lead need more than the cohort gets?
- The earlier work said the company's values are not your personal values and are not derived from them. Do you agree? Say why either way.
- Counterexample: the book's own advice is that asking founders to write their values down can start the founding documents (p. 498). Sŏn's are being written. Did that come from asking?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The document and the trade-offs list, with silent trade-offs becoming written questions; the why session plus a conversation in lead onboarding; or the issued working-with-me document?
- Confirm or decline the refusal: company values are not the founders' personal values.
- If a conversation: recorded anywhere? By whom?

**What the book says:** understand founders' values and the trade-offs they will make; ask them to write them down; offsites and 1:1s both work (pp. 497 to 498).

**White-paper default:** why before how (WP p. 18); the founding document (2.1.17) and the trade-offs list (2.2.9) exist for this.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies with written principles commonly still find that leads learn "what the founders actually want" from watching them; the written version wins only where the founders defer to it out loud.

**Options:** (a) the document and written questions; (b) the why session plus an unrecorded conversation; (c) the issued working-with-me document. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** a "written question" path that no lead uses because the founder is in the building; the issued document carrying values 1.1.2 kept private; Brandon stating what matters to him, which is not recorded here.

**A finished answer:** the path; the refusal confirmed or declined; where a silent trade-off goes.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 6.2.8 Decide the founders' standing with the whole team beyond the leads: check-ins, destinations, and observations about a person

**The question:** beyond the leads, does a founder ever hold a person's check-in, sit on the destinations page for a concern about a person, or act on something they noticed about a person?

**Why it matters now:** the opening cohort meets the founders in the why session and on the floor before the leads have been there long. The team will bring things to a founder if a founder will take them. The destinations page (4.5.17) is written before the first cohort; this decides whether a founder is on it.

**Openers:**
- A server from the first cohort catches you after a shift with something about their lead. What do you say, and where does it go?
- Sideways: the white paper says belonging is to people, and the people are the ones at the edge (WP pp. 09, 25). Where is a founder in that picture: a person, or the center?
- You notice, over three services, something about one cook. What happens to that observation?
- Counterexample: a founder who is genuinely unreachable about people can look cold to a small team. Is that a cost you accept?

**Narrowing questions:**
- No check-in but a lead's, no page but a lead's, never a destination, observations to the check-in holder only; a first-quarter exception with dates; or a named class on the destinations page?
- The founders' one standing relationship with everyone: the why session (3.3.6), as the concept's voice?
- Anything a founder is allowed to say to a team member about their work, outside the floor rule's live speech (6.1.8)?

**What the book says:** build formal and informal connections; raise a problem with a fellow manager's team directly with them (pp. 493 to 494).

**White-paper default:** the Maitre d owns team development (WP p. 10); the mentor is the early relationship (WP p. 18); belonging is to people (WP p. 25).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Owners who take concerns about managers directly from staff are common in restaurants and are the usual source of the manager being undermined; the standard remedy is a stated path that does not run through the owner.

**Options:** (a) the refusal; (b) a first-quarter exception with dates; (c) a named class on the destinations page. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** an exception with no end; a founder as the destination for concerns about a lead when 5.3.4 sent them elsewhere; anything about a founder's warmth or distance being written down.

**A finished answer:** the rule; the why session confirmed as the standing relationship; the destinations page's founder line, if any.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 6.2.9 Decide how a concern about a founder's conduct is raised and read, when no one stands above

**The question:** the non-negotiables are being written (5.2.3). If a concern names a founder, where does it go, who reads it, what is written, and what does the house do while it is open?

**Why it matters now:** the conduct policy is on the candidate's sheet before the first hire. A policy that exempts its authors is read as such by every candidate. And there is no one above the founders, so the path has to be drawn in advance or it is nothing until it is a dispute.

**Openers:**
- The white paper says a house whose value lives in one person is a dependency, not a business (WP p. 37). What is a house whose rules stop at its founders?
- Sideways: 5.8.3 named a second reader for anyone's performance record. Who is the second reader when the subject is you?
- Picture the lead who has to raise it. What would make them able to?
- The earlier work offered that while a read is open the founder is off the floor. Say that aloud about yourself. Does it hold?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The same path as anyone's, read by the two leads with counsel, the other partner told the same day; a destination outside the house; or the other partner alone?
- While open: the off-floor option, stated as an option?
- If a finding touches a non-negotiable: what is written, by whom, who may read it?
- Everything past a finding: the operating agreement's and counsel's? Confirm.

**What the book says:** raise it directly before it escalates (p. 494); the manager protects the team (pp. 491 to 492). Nothing on a founder as the subject.

**White-paper default:** no one is above the founders in the structure (WP pp. 07 to 08); management absorbs blame from staff (WP p. 17). Silent on the case.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. With a board, a concern about a founder goes to the board or its committee. Without one, it goes to the operating agreement and counsel, and the common failure is that nothing is written until it is a dispute.

**Options:** (a) the path above; (b) an outside destination; (c) the other partner. Depth: cons. 8.

**Watch for:** a path that asks two employees to judge their principals with no counsel beside them; a record that becomes a founder's file; the decision drifting into what happens after a finding, which is the agreement's.

**A finished answer:** the path; the while-open option stated; the record rule; the counsel questions logged for 6.3.7; the policy sentence 6.2.12 will carry.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner once seated; counsel.

### 6.2.10 Decide the founders' outside circle: peers, mentors, a coach, and what the house records

**The question:** the book says find the leaders you admire and compare notes, and call them to pressure-test an idea or practice a hard conversation (pp. 493 to 494). Do the founders have that circle, is a coach part of it, and does the house record any of it?

**Why it matters now:** 3.3.23 decided the coach question for a lead and sent the founders' own question here; 5.1.12 decides who tests hypotheses about a founder's work. The first hard conversation a founder has to practice is with a lead in the first quarter.

**Openers:**
- Who would you call to practice a hard conversation before having it? If no one, is that a gap or a preference?
- Sideways: the white paper treats the best rooms as a university and names the operators it admires (WP pp. 02, 04). Are any of those people a call away, and should they be?
- The earlier work said no coach for the founders on the same reasoning as no coach for a lead. Does the reasoning transfer?
- What must the house never know about who you talk to?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The founders' own, unrecorded; a coach per founder; or a standing peer relationship named on 1.1.12 only where the house depends on it?
- If a coach: who pays is Dominic's and the agreement's. Confirm that financials are not a source here.
- Confirm: no outside relationship of a founder appears in a house record except as a dependency on 1.1.12 or a contract.

**What the book says:** identify leaders you admire; compare management notes; some become the people you call (pp. 493 to 494); the friendships built in hard moments last (p. 491).

**White-paper default:** the operators the founders admire, named (WP pp. 02, 04). Nothing on a circle or a coach.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Executive coaching for founders is common in venture-backed companies and uncommon in independent restaurants; peer groups of operators are common in both.

**Options:** (a) the founders' own; (b) a coach; (c) a peer relationship on the list where the house depends on it. Depth: cons. 9.

**Watch for:** the house recording a founder's friendships; a coach who becomes the read on the center that 6.1.4 refused to be a person; the decision made from cost.

**A finished answer:** the choice, each founder for their own; the recording rule confirmed.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders, each for their own.

### 6.2.11 Decide the founders' outside relationships the house depends on, and the inward-first rule

**The question:** the raise, the public voice, creator partners, and the chef search all sit on the founders' seats and touch the team. What does each owe the team first, and does a public statement about the house reach the team before the public?

**Why it matters now:** the documented build is already public and the content framework moves at the speed of the moment (WP p. 31). The first time the team sees something about the house on a phone before hearing it at a brief is the first time the inward-first question is answered by default.

**Openers:**
- A structure change is decided on Tuesday. The content framework would post it Tuesday night. When does the team hear?
- Sideways: crisis communication is "transparency first" toward the customer (WP p. 31). Toward the team, what is it?
- The raise is Dominic's domain. What is the one thing about it the team should hear from a founder, and when? Nothing about amount, terms, or dates is a source here.
- Who holds the founder consultation and the leadership review of content (WP p. 31) today? Whose seat is that on?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Inward-first for every public statement about the house; the framework's tiers as the whole rule; or inward-first for a named class?
- The whole-team statement form (4.8.20) as the vehicle?
- The content review roles named or placed on 6.1.17?
- Which of these relationships are dependencies on 1.1.12?

**What the book says:** share your team's mission with key partners; seek out those who can create or prevent obstacles; do not let a peer hear news late (pp. 493 to 494).

**White-paper default:** a diligence document, not an offer (WP p. 02); transparency first (WP p. 31); access over cash (WP p. 31); hand over the machine rather than ask for faith (WP p. 39). Silent on the team hearing first.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. "Internal before external" is a standard communications rule in companies with a public voice; restaurants with a documented build are new enough that the rule is rarely written.

**Options:** (a) inward-first for everything; (b) the framework's tiers only; (c) inward-first for a named class. Depth: cons. 10.

**Watch for:** a rule the content framework's speed cannot keep; the raise creeping into the record beyond its public-voice component; a class that is never written down.

**A finished answer:** the rule; the vehicle; the content review roles placed; the dependencies named.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
