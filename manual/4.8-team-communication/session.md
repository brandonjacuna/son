## 3. Briefs

### 4.8.2 Decide the house's language list: how it is built, when a language enters and leaves, and what must exist in every listed language before it is published

**The question:** which languages does the house work in, how does a language get on and off the list, and what must exist in every one of them before it counts as published?

**Why it matters now:** the posting states the languages the process runs in, so this is on the first candidate's screen. The promise at the interview creates the obligation after it, and the cost of that obligation depends on how the list is defined.

**Openers:**
- A cook interviews in Spanish, is hired, and on day one receives the founding document in English. What did the interview promise mean?
- Sideways: the white paper says language shapes culture, as the reason for "customer." What does it say about a house where the culture is written in a language a third of the team reads slowly?
- What languages do you and Dominic speak well enough to translate a pay rule? That is the list you can keep before the first hire.

**Narrowing questions:**
- Built from postings' requirements plus each hire's chosen language, or from postings only?
- Enters when a posting states it or a hire chooses it; leaves when no one who chose it remains. Both?
- The complete-before-publishing set: the founding documents, the candidate sheet, safety and policy notices, the brief's written form, the team home's pages. Which of these, and what may lag with a window?
- Who owns the list?

**What the book says:** share the same information with everyone; as a team scales, information-sharing has to be formalized or people learn things at different times and read it as politics (p. 361).

**White-paper default:** the candidate chooses the interview language; language choice protects dignity (WP p. 17). Nothing after the interview.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Safety regulators require training in a language workers understand; multilingual employers commonly version translations with their source.

**Options:** (a) the full list with a complete set and a window for the rest. (b) postings' languages only; individuals served by the interpreter and personal translation. (c) English plus one now, reopened at the third. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** a list the founders cannot serve before the first hire; a promise that quietly narrows after hire.

**A finished answer:** the build rule, the enter and leave rules, the complete set and the lag window, the owner; Dominic agrees.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.8.3 Decide how translation is done, checked, paid, and disclosed

**The question:** who translates, who checks, is it paid, and may a machine draft it?

**Why it matters now:** the founding set has to exist in every listed language before the first cohort, and someone has to do it. Bilingual staff translating "as a favor" is unpaid work; machine drafts are wrong where it matters.

**Openers:**
- Who translates the first pay rule into Spanish, and who catches the mistake?
- Sideways: the white paper pays anyone qualified to write a training module. Is a translation a module?
- A machine drafts a safety notice. Who reads it before it goes on the wall, and does the wall say a machine drafted it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Before opening: the founders and early leads, or an outside translator?
- After opening: bilingual staff, paid on the clock, always?
- The second-speaker check: always?
- Machine drafts: allowed with a human check on the complete set and a disclosure line elsewhere; or not at all?
- The version rule: a translation carries its source's version.

**What the book says:** share the same information with everyone (p. 361).

**White-paper default:** deterministic where load-bearing, AI on top only (WP p. 21); module authorship is paid (WP p. 19). Silent on translation.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Employers running in several languages commonly keep one source document and have a second speaker check any translation a person relies on.

**Options:** (a) founders and leads first, paid staff after, second check, machine drafts disclosed. (b) an outside translator for the founding set. (c) machine drafts by default with a human check on the named set only. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** the bilingual server who becomes the house's unpaid translator; a translated page one version behind its source.

**A finished answer:** who, the check, the pay rule, the machine rule and its disclosure, the version rule.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.8.4 Decide the interpreter designation's rule and its reach

**The question:** when a shift's crew includes a language the shift lead does not speak, who interprets, on what terms, and does that reach into the check-in, orientation, and a review?

**Why it matters now:** the card exists (4.1.23); 2.3.9 handed the check-in's case here. A private, upward conversation through a peer is not private; the answer has to exist before the first check-in with someone the lead cannot talk to.

**Openers:**
- Friday's brief, a new dishwasher who speaks only Spanish, a lead who does not. Who says the 86 list to him, and does anyone write down that it happened?
- Sideways: hospitals let a bilingual colleague interpret directions but not a diagnosis. What is the Sŏn version of the diagnosis?
- The same server interprets every shift for three months. What has she become, and did anyone decide it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The trigger and the terms: paid, on the clock, floor-only, rotated, logged. All five?
- The check-in: a speaking lead holds it, a written check-in in the person's language, or the interpreter with consent each time under the card's confidentiality rule?
- First-day orientation: the interpreter?
- A review conversation (5.4): never through a peer?
- The kitchen: the chef partner's.

**What the book says:** share the same information with everyone (p. 361).

**White-paper default:** designations mark accountability in the moment and are not elevations (WP p. 10); the interview-language promise (WP p. 17). No interpreter.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. The hospital distinction between a trained interpreter and an ad hoc bilingual colleague is the useful analogy.

**Options:** (a) the designation for the brief and orientation; the check-in and the review to a speaking lead or a written form. (b) the designation covers the check-in with consent. (c) no designation; the written brief in every language and the spoken brief in the lead's language. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** the interpreter becoming the channel; the log becoming a mark on the person who needed one.

**A finished answer:** the trigger, the five terms, the reach for each of the three conversations, the confidentiality rule confirmed, the kitchen marked pending.

**Needs agreement from:** no one beyond Brandon for the floor; the chef partner for the kitchen.

### 4.8.5 Decide the record rule: one home per kind of message, written before spoken for a change, and a chat message is never the record

**The question:** where does each kind of message live, may a change ever be spoken before it is written, and what is never the record?

**Why it matters now:** this is the bus. Two shifts hearing two versions of a change is the white paper's named failure. The destinations page lists where things go; this decides that the list is the rule, and settles the old conflict over spoken versus written.

**Openers:**
- The Maitre d changes the seating rule at Tuesday's brief. Thursday's lead heard it from a server. Which version is true?
- Sideways: the book's engineers move any work conversation into a channel everyone can see. What is the restaurant's version of "everyone can see" when half the team is asleep?
- An 86 cannot wait for a page. Where does the exception end?

**Narrowing questions:**
- One home per kind, and the destinations page (4.5.17) is where the list lives?
- Written before spoken: always; or a service-critical exception with the write-up owed by the close?
- Chat, text, and speech are never the record: written.
- A meeting slot reads records aloud and originates nothing unrecorded: kept?

**What the book says:** share notes and decisions; formalize or people learn things at different times and read it as politics (p. 361); chat retention loses things (p. 336).

**White-paper default:** verbal handoffs and tribal knowledge are a system failure (WP p. 13); the system holds the knowledge (WP p. 07). Nothing on what counts as the record.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Hotels and hospitals read the spoken handover from the written one, not the reverse.

**Options:** (a) written before spoken always. (b) the service-critical exception with the close as the deadline. (c) spoken first allowed, written by the close. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** an exception that swallows the rule; a "record" that is a photo of a whiteboard in a group text.

**A finished answer:** the one-home rule, the order rule with its exception if any, the never-the-record sentence, the meeting-slot rule; Dominic agrees.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.8.6 Decide the pass-down: how what leadership knows reaches each shift, and how it is framed

**The question:** what does the brief's pass-down line carry, who frames it, and what may framing never do?

**Why it matters now:** 2.3.7's spine has "the one thing from the week's leads' review." The book's manager adds "the right way to think about this is." That is where a lead earns trust or turns a decision into a rumor, and no one has said what the line may and may not do.

**Openers:**
- The leads' review decided to move the close's cutoff. Say it as the line at Wednesday's brief, with the reason, in one breath.
- Sideways: Sandberg would share a leadership decision and then say how to think about it. When does that become spin?
- The morning has no lead present. Who says the line?

**Narrowing questions:**
- What the slot carries: the one thing, a decision that reached the house, a change's reason.
- Written in the brief's form before it is spoken: always?
- Framed by the lead who was in the review, in their own words, with the reason.
- Never: soften a decision into a rumor, attribute it to a person, add a figure.
- A shift with no lead: the written brief and the designation holder.

**What the book says:** a standing pass-downs item; framing adds context to a decision already made (pp. 361 to 362).

**White-paper default:** the brief is part of the nervous system (WP p. 14) and as important as the signature dish (WP p. 20). No framer named.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. The book's Google example is the reference; in restaurants the pre-shift is already where managers pass things down, usually without the reason.

**Options:** (a) the one line, framed by the lead who was there, with the reason. (b) the line plus a written why on the team home. (c) no framing. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** "leadership decided" as the framing; the reason left out because it is uncomfortable.

**A finished answer:** what the slot carries, the written-first rule, the framer, the three nevers, the no-lead case.

**Needs agreement from:** no one beyond Brandon.

### 4.8.7 Decide whether every person writes a weekly line, and whether leadership writes a note to the house

**The question:** does everyone write a weekly snippet, and does leadership write a note to the whole house on a rhythm?

**Why it matters now:** a written line from everyone is paid time or unpaid work; a house note is the one place leadership can say in writing, in every language, what changed and why and what it is working on. 4.6.16's "what leadership is working on" line needs a home if it was adopted.

**Openers:**
- Month three. What does a late-night server know about what dinner's crew is dealing with? Should they?
- Sideways: the book's leader writes a weekly note with wins, challenges, and a photo of the dog. What is the honest Sŏn version, and would you write it every month for five years?
- The lead already writes one line at the close. Is that the book's end-of-day habit, or something smaller?

**Narrowing questions:**
- A weekly line from everyone: where (the person page, the close), how long, on the clock, in their language, read by whom? Or no?
- A house note: rhythm; fixed sections (what changed and why, what leadership is working on, wins as facts, one open question with a channel); refusals (no figure, nothing about a person, no code name); owner.
- The close's line as the end-of-day habit: yes?

**What the book says:** team snippets weekly; a leader's shared update for a larger group, personal notes welcome; a personal habit of deciding what to tell people each day (p. 362).

**White-paper default:** feedback runs both directions (WP p. 19); the lead's weekly note (2.2.33) and the close's line (2.3.8) exist. No snippet, no house note.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Founder notes to the whole company are common in small companies; restaurants use a printed sheet by the time clock for the same purpose.

**Options:** (a) no snippet; a monthly house note; the close's line is the habit. (b) a weekly line on the person page plus the note. (c) neither. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** a note that becomes a place for conviction rather than facts; a weekly line that is unpaid.

**A finished answer:** yes or no to each, with the terms; the note's sections, refusals, rhythm, and owner if yes.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.8.8 Decide whether the house has a chat tool, what it may carry, and the off-shift rule

**The question:** does the house provide a chat tool, what may it carry, and what does a message off the clock oblige?

**Why it matters now:** communication expectations are on the candidate sheet (WP p. 18), so this precedes the first interview. Staff will message each other regardless; the choice is a place with rules or a group text without them. And the off-shift rule is a wage-and-hour matter.

**Openers:**
- Every restaurant you have worked in had a group text nobody created. What went wrong in it?
- Sideways: a message at midnight asking who can cover tomorrow. Is reading it work? Is answering it?
- A porter has a phone with no data plan. How does Thursday's brief reach him on Wednesday?

**Narrowing questions:**
- A chat tool at all?
- If yes: schedule notices, swaps, links to the written brief; never a decision, a change, anything about a person, a customer's details.
- Off shift: available to read, never owed a response; nothing sent off shift requires an act before the next paid hour. Counsel confirms.
- The person without a phone or data: the paper brief at the building, a shared device.
- On a phone, in every listed language.

**What the book says:** a shared channel levels the playing field; chat retention limits (pp. 334 to 336).

**White-paper default:** communication expectations are on the candidate sheet (WP p. 18); systems are built for their users (WP p. 17). No chat tool named.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Restaurant scheduling tools commonly include messaging; many operators state in writing that off-shift messages need no response until the next shift.

**Options:** (a) a narrow chat tool with the off-shift rule on the candidate sheet. (b) no chat tool; the team home and the schedule tool carry it. (c) a broad chat tool. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** the tool becoming the record by habit; a rule counsel has not seen.

**A finished answer:** yes or no, the scope, the off-shift sentence, the no-phone case, the language and device rule; counsel's confirmation pending.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; counsel on the off-shift rule.

### 4.8.9 Decide the peer recognition rule

**The question:** what is a recognition at Sŏn, what is it never, where is it read, and what happens when one is declined?

**Why it matters now:** the white paper promises a platform; platforms sell points and leaderboards, which are rankings of people, which the brief refuses. The rule has to exist before the tool is configured (4.8.18).

**Openers:**
- A server writes: "Marco caught table twelve's allergy before the ticket went in." A second writes: "Marco is the best runner we have." Which one goes up, and why?
- Sideways: the white paper says peer praise lands differently because it comes from people who understand the work. What happens to that the first time a manager's praise is posted in the same feed?
- Someone gets recognized every week; someone never. Is that information, and for whom?

**Narrowing questions:**
- A peer's own words, a specific action, one service: the whole definition?
- Never: a rating, a comparison, a count, manager praise as peer praise, a review input, an off-shift prompt.
- Read at the brief's slot (4.5.13) and the team home; does it accumulate anywhere?
- Declines: who, and the author told privately; repeated declines prompt a look at the prompts.
- A peer's words carry the customer word only (4.5.19), or more?

**What the book says:** celebrate wins in shared updates (p. 362); recognition without criteria drifts to whoever the manager sees (pp. 357 to 358).

**White-paper default:** stated outright, peer recognition in the moment through a platform, landing differently than management praise (WP p. 19).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Office recognition tools commonly ship with points and public feeds; the pre-shift shout-out is the industry's version with no record.

**Options:** (a) the old work's rule whole. (b) read at the brief only, never accumulating. (c) light points, no ranking. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** a count creeping in through the tool's defaults; the writing card rewriting a peer's words.

**A finished answer:** the definition, the nevers, where it is read, the decline rule, the writing rule.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.8.10 Decide the feedback channel's rules

**The question:** what is the channel for, what does it refuse, may an item be anonymous, and who sees what?

**Why it matters now:** the windows and closing states exist (2.3.16); the destination for a concern about a lead exists (4.5.11). What is missing is the rule that keeps the channel about process and tools, and the anonymity choice, before the tool is configured (4.8.19).

**Openers:**
- The first item in the channel is "the Maitre d plays favorites." What happens to it, and what does the submitter see happen?
- Sideways: the white paper says feedback without follow-through breeds cynicism. What breeds it faster: no reply, or a reply that says "declined" with no reason?
- Anonymous items cannot be answered. Is that a price you want people to be able to pay?

**Narrowing questions:**
- For: a friction with a tool or process, an idea, a failure report, a question. Refuses: a named coworker or lead (to 4.5.11), a pay question (to 5.5).
- Anonymous allowed, with no reply as the cost?
- Who triages, inside 2.3.16's windows.
- The submitter sees the state; do other staff see open items?
- Every listed language.

**What the book says:** build an environment for open exchange; sharing decisions builds trust (pp. 360 to 361).

**White-paper default:** stated outright, a dedicated channel synced to the execution system, the loop closed (WP p. 22); team members weigh in on management effectiveness (WP p. 19).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Working suggestion channels publish each item's state; the ones that die go quiet.

**Options:** (a) process only, anonymity allowed, states visible, open items visible to all. (b) open items visible to the submitter and leads only. (c) no anonymity. Depth: cons. 8.

**Watch for:** "management effectiveness" being refused along with named people; a triage owner who is the subject of most items.

**A finished answer:** the for and refuses lists, the anonymity rule and its cost, the triager, the visibility rules, the language rule.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.8.11 Decide whether software may answer the phone by voice, and what it must disclose

**The question:** may a customer be answered by a synthesized voice, and if so, does it say so?

**Why it matters now:** the phone is in the stack's plan (WP p. 22) and the white paper treats how the phone is answered as equal to the plating. Until decided, a person answers. 3.3.19 decided the same question for training media; the two should agree or the difference should be stated.

**Openers:**
- A regular calls to move her table. A voice answers in perfect Spanish and never says it is software. Is anything wrong? Would she think so if she found out?
- Sideways: the white paper keeps AI off anything load-bearing. Is the phone load-bearing?
- What did 3.3.19 decide for the why session, and does the phone deserve the same answer?

**Narrowing questions:**
- A person always; software with disclosure in the first sentence; software without disclosure.
- Which languages it must handle; what happens in a listed language it cannot.
- How a caller reaches a person.
- Consistent with 3.3.19, or the difference stated.
- Counsel on disclosure law.

**What the book says:** nothing directly; communication builds trust (p. 361).

**White-paper default:** the phone is as important as the plating (WP p. 20); the phone runs in software (WP p. 22); AI is never load-bearing (WP p. 21); never assert the unproven to a customer (WP p. 36). No rule on a synthesized voice.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Several states have enacted or proposed automated-system disclosure requirements of varying scope; restaurants using automated answering commonly disclose it at the start of the call.

**Options:** (a) a person always. (b) software with disclosure. (c) software without. Depth: cons. 9.

**Watch for:** deciding the build here (it is Dominic's); a rule that leaves a Spanish-speaking caller with no path to a person.

**A finished answer:** the rule, the language rule, the path to a person, the 3.3.19 consistency line; counsel pending.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; counsel.

### 4.8.12 Decide where a phone message lives

**The question:** when someone or something answers the phone, where does what they heard go?

**Why it matters now:** a message relayed verbally is the white paper's failure in miniature; a message in phone software that never reaches the customer record is a preference the building forgot.

**Openers:**
- A caller says she is allergic to shellfish and will be in Saturday. Trace that sentence to the pass.
- Sideways: the white paper says memory leaves when the server does. Where does a phone message live when the host who took it goes home?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Reservation changes and notes to the customer record; complaints to the incident log; service-relevant notes to the brief's written form.
- A message for a staff member: passed on, not recorded.
- A message only in phone software is a gap the reach review reads.
- Staff-directed calls on and off shift: routing.

**What the book says:** the record, not the relay (p. 361, in substance).

**White-paper default:** preferences live in the system (WP p. 27); the incident is captured in the close (WP p. 13). No phone rule.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Reservation systems commonly carry a notes field precisely so the phone's content does not stay with whoever answered.

**Options:** (a) the three destinations and the no-relay rule. (b) everything to the customer record, including complaints. (c) the host's judgment. Commits Sŏn to the relay. Depth: cons. 9.

**Watch for:** a customer's personal details in a chat tool; a staff message becoming a record about the staff member.

**A finished answer:** the three destinations, the staff-message rule, the gap rule, the routing.

**Needs agreement from:** no one beyond Brandon; the build is Dominic's.

### 4.8.13 Decide the exit conversation and contact with former employees

**The question:** is a leaving person offered a conversation about what could have been better, who holds it, what happens to what they say, what does the house confirm outside, and does it stay in touch?

**Why it matters now:** the white paper names exit interviews and growth beyond the building as culture measures; the first departure will come before anyone has designed this. 4.3.9 decided what the team hears; 5.10 holds the exit itself.

**Openers:**
- A good server leaves for a better-paying room. On her last shift, who sits with her, and what do they ask?
- Sideways: the white paper's longest culture measure is how people grow beyond the building. How would you ever know, and would they want you to?
- She is leaving because of her lead. Who does she get to talk to, and does she have to say why?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Optional, last scheduled shift, paid, in their language: all four?
- Never the lead the person is leaving, when that is the reason, without the person having to say so.
- Asks what could have been better; nothing evaluative about the person.
- Read at the house review as process; never on the person's record.
- Outside: dates and seat only, unless the person asks for more.
- Contact: an annual opt-in note, aggregate only, counsel on consent.

**What the book says:** nothing directly; keep communicating through uncertainty (p. 361).

**White-paper default:** stated outright, exit interviews read for what could have been better; how people grow beyond the building is the longest measure (WP p. 23); post-mortems private (WP p. 17).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Alumni networks are common in consulting and technology and rare in restaurants, though a chef's former cooks are the industry's oldest version.

**Options:** (a) the old work's design whole. (b) the conversation only. (c) neither; the departure read and the pulse carry it. Depth: cons. 10.

**Watch for:** the conversation becoming a reference; outreach without consent.

**A finished answer:** the four terms, the holder rule, the questions' scope, the process-only use, the outside rule, the contact rule; Dominic agrees; counsel pending.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; counsel.

### 4.8.14 Decide the reach review: how the house checks that a change reached every shift, language, and person, and reads a miss as a process gap

**The question:** who reads the change-reach record, how often, and what may they change when it shows a miss?

**Why it matters now:** 2.2.11 defined "reached" and handed the record to Dominic. A record no one reads is a build for its own sake; a record read for culprits is worse.

**Openers:**
- A change to the close's checklist reached dinner's brief and never reached late night's. Who notices, and when?
- Sideways: the old work noticed that a staffing change reaching the floor before the affected person is itself a signal. A signal of what?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Weekly at the leads' review from the system's record; the quarter's pattern at the house review.
- A miss is a process step, never a name.
- Floor-before-person is a logged signal.
- What the review may change: the channel, the window, the translation step.

**What the book says:** formalize information-sharing or people learn things at different times and read it as politics (p. 361).

**White-paper default:** sequence corruption is a system failure; the fix is system maintenance, not people management (WP p. 14).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Nothing verifiable beyond the book; most operators learn a change did not land when someone acts on the old rule.

**Options:** (a) weekly and quarterly from the record. (b) quarterly only. (c) only on report. Depth: cons. 11.

**Watch for:** the review becoming a search for who did not tell whom.

**A finished answer:** reader, rhythm, the step-not-name rule, the signal rule, the three things it may change.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
