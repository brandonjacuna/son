# 4.8 Team communication: considerations

Topics follow the book's order (pp. 360 to 362), with the routed matters (the language list, the interpreter, the phone, the exit conversation) placed where they fit. "WP" is the Sŏn investor white paper, September 2026. "Book" is *Scaling People*. Where the white paper is silent, it says so.

## 1. The premise the book assumes, and Sŏn's

**What the book holds.** Its team advice reduces to three things: set expectations well, share the same information with everyone, and build an environment for open exchange (Book pp. 360 to 361). Its team is desk-based, reached by meeting, email, and chat, with a layer above passing information down to the manager.

**What the white paper holds.** The bus fails when information runs on "verbal handoffs, tribal knowledge, and informal relationships between individuals" (WP p. 13). The two ends of a service are the brief and the close (WP pp. 14, 26). "Language shapes culture" (WP p. 25), the reason for "customer." Communication expectations are on the sheet a candidate receives at the first interview (WP p. 18), which is why several of this chunk's decisions sit before the first hire. The white paper never describes a team chat, a memo, or a staff meeting other than the brief.

**Structural facts, from the digest, that every decision below assumes.**
- Sŏn's version of "distributed" is time, not geography.
- A shift team exists from the brief to the close and then dissolves.
- Staff will work in more than one language, because the interview-language promise (WP p. 17) becomes a fact the workplace keeps serving after hire.
- Most staff are not at a desk. Anything meant to reach the team is read in short bursts, on a phone, before or after a shift.
- A message sent to a personal device off shift must not create an expectation of a response; off-the-clock work raises wage-and-hour questions. Whatever is chosen must distinguish "available to read" from "owed a response."

**Already decided upstream.** The transparency line (2.2.6); the three channels and what "reached" means (2.2.11); the internal communications policy (2.2.12); the team home (2.2.13, 2.2.15); the brief and the close (2.3.7, 2.3.8); the check-in (2.3.9); the pulse and the channel's windows (2.3.15, 2.3.16); the destinations page (4.5.11, 4.5.17); the writing card (4.5.19); how a structure change reaches people (4.1.12); the interpreter card (4.1.23); what the team hears when someone leaves (4.3.9).

## 2. The language list and translation

**What the white paper holds.** "Language choice protects dignity: a candidate whose first language is not English can interview with someone who speaks it" (WP p. 17). Nothing past the interview. 3.1.16 and 3.2.15 decided which languages hiring offers at launch and what happens before a trained speaker exists; 2.3.7 carries a rule that the brief is spoken in every roster language; 2.2.15 puts the team home in every house language; 2.3.33 depends on this chunk for the list.

**From the digest.** The old work proposed a living list built from open-position requirements plus each employee's chosen language, a rule that internal documents exist in every listed language before publishing, and that a language stays listed while an employee who chose it remains. Translation as paid work checked by a second speaker, disclosed if machine-drafted. The founders' and early leads' own languages are a real constraint on the hiring calendar. The old brand-guidelines questions (register, untranslated terminology, whether a posting is a formal surface) are dropped; they rest on excluded material.

**Considerations.**
- The promise at the interview creates the obligation after it. A person interviewed in Spanish who then receives the founding document only in English has been told, in effect, that the promise was for the door.
- "Every document in every language before publishing" is expensive at a house that versions everything. The honest rule names which documents must be complete before publishing (the founding documents, the candidate sheet, safety and policy notices, the brief's written form) and which may lag with a window.
- Machine-drafted translation is cheap and often wrong in exactly the places that matter (a safety notice, a pay rule). The white paper's own ratio, deterministic where load-bearing (WP p. 21), argues for a human check on anything a person relies on, and a disclosure line otherwise.
- Bilingual staff translating "as a favor" is unpaid work and a hidden tax on the people the list is meant to serve.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. US workplace-safety regulators require safety training to be delivered in a language and vocabulary workers understand; many restaurants meet that for training and nothing else. Employers that run in more than one language commonly keep one source document and version its translations together.

**Options for 4.8.2.** (a) The list as the old work had it, with a complete-before-publishing rule for a named set and a window for the rest. (b) A shorter list: only the languages a posting requires, so an individual's chosen language does not add to it; the individual is served by the interpreter and a personal translation. Commits Sŏn to a smaller list and a promise that narrows after hire. (c) English plus one, decided now, with the rule reopened when a hire chooses a third. Commits Sŏn to saying so on the posting.

**Options for 4.8.3.** (a) Founders and early leads translate before opening; bilingual staff after, paid on the clock, second-speaker check, machine drafts allowed with a disclosure line. (b) An outside translator for the founding set, staff for the rest. (c) Machine drafts as the default with a human check on the named set only. Each commits Sŏn to the version rule: a translation carries its source's version.

## 3. The interpreter designation

**What the white paper holds.** Designations "mark accountability in the moment, in the domain. They are not permanent elevations above anyone" (WP p. 10). No interpreter. 4.1.23 wrote the card (never customer-facing, confidentiality-bound); 4.1.20 set the rotation floor; 2.3.9 handed the check-in's interpreter question here; 2.3.7 says the brief runs in every roster language.

**From the digest.** The old work: on a shift whose crew includes a language the shift lead does not speak, a bilingual crew member is named interpreter for that shift, paid, on the clock, floor-only, rotated, with whether the brief ran in every needed language logged. Kitchen needs are a separate decision. First-day orientation runs with an interpreter if needed.

**Considerations.**
- The check-in is the hard case. A private, upward conversation through a peer is not private. The options are a lead who speaks the language holding the check-in for that person, a written check-in in the person's language, or the interpreter under the card's confidentiality rule with the person's consent each time.
- A review conversation (5.4) has the same problem, more so.
- Rotation matters more than it looks: the same person interpreting every shift becomes the informal channel the white paper warns about, and stops being read as a peer.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Hospitals distinguish a trained interpreter from an ad hoc bilingual staff member and restrict the second to non-clinical matters; the analogy for Sŏn is the brief versus the check-in.

**Options for 4.8.4.** (a) The designation for the brief and orientation; the check-in and the review go to a speaking lead or a written form, never through a peer. (b) The designation covers the check-in too, with consent each time and the card's confidentiality rule. (c) No designation; the brief's written form in every language does the work and the spoken brief runs in the lead's language. Each commits Sŏn to paying for the time.

## 4. The record rule and the pass-down

**What the white paper holds.** "The system holds the knowledge" (WP p. 07); bus failure (WP p. 13); the shift brief is as important as the signature dish (WP p. 20); recognizing customers starts at the brief (WP p. 22). The white paper does not say what counts as the record of a change, or who frames leadership's news for the floor. 2.2.11 named the channels and "reached"; 4.5.17 lists the destinations; 4.1.12 says how a structure change reaches people, never as an announcement.

**From the digest.** The old work proposed that every kind of internal message has one home record, that a team-meeting slot reads records aloud rather than originating unscripted content, and flagged an unresolved conflict over whether verbal may precede written. The book's pass-down is a standing agenda item carrying information from leadership, with framing, "I think the right way to think about this is" (Book pp. 361 to 362). The book's caution that chat retention loses things (Book p. 336) carries.

**Considerations.**
- "Written before spoken" is clean and slow. A service-critical change (an 86, a closure, a safety matter) cannot wait for a page. The workable rule is a narrow exception with the write-up owed by the close, so the record exists before the next shift.
- Framing is where the book's manager earns their keep, and where a lead can soften a decision into a rumor. The pass-down at Sŏn is one line in the brief (2.3.7), spoken by the lead who was in the review, in their own words, with the reason. What the framing may never do is as important as what it may.
- A shift with no lead present (possible at the morning once it opens) receives the pass-down through the written brief and the designation holder.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Hotels and hospitals run on a written handover that the spoken handover reads from; the book's own Sandberg example is a manager adding "how to think about this" to a decision already made elsewhere (Book pp. 361 to 362).

**Options for 4.8.5.** (a) One home per kind, written before spoken always, chat never the record, meeting slots read records. (b) The same with the service-critical exception and the write-up owed by the close. (c) Spoken first is allowed for any change, written by the close. Commits Sŏn to different shifts hearing different versions until the close.

**Options for 4.8.6.** (a) The one line from the leads' review, framed by the lead who was there, with the reason. (b) The line plus a written "why" on the team home for anyone who wants it. (c) No framing; the line is read as written. Commits Sŏn to the floor hearing decisions without reasons.

## 5. Snippets, the house note, and the end-of-day habit

**What the white paper holds.** Feedback runs both directions (WP p. 19); the pulse is tracked regularly (WP p. 22). 2.2.33 gives each lead a weekly note; 2.3.8 gives the lead one line at the close. Nothing like a snippet from every person or a note from leadership to the house.

**From the book.** Team snippets: a weekly document every member fills in so the team sees what everyone is doing without spending meeting time on status; for a larger group, a weekly or monthly update from the leader with wins, challenges, and ideas, personal notes welcome, used above all to keep the team connected to its leader; and a personal end-of-day habit of deciding what to tell people (Book p. 362).

**Considerations.**
- A weekly line from every person at a restaurant is either on the clock or it is unpaid work. On the clock, it competes with the close's thank-you notes (2.3.8). The person page (2.3.11) is a natural home if it exists.
- The house note is the one place leadership can say, in writing and in every language, what changed and why, what it is working on, and one open question with a channel for answers. It is also where 4.6.16's "what leadership is working on" line could live, so the house is never surprised that risks exist.
- The lead's one line at the close is already the book's end-of-day habit in miniature.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Weekly written updates from a founder to the whole company are common in small companies; restaurants more often use a printed sheet by the time clock, which is the same practice at lower cost.

**Options for 4.8.7.** (a) No snippet from every person; a monthly house note from leadership with fixed sections; the close's line is the habit. (b) A weekly line from everyone on the person page, on the clock, read by their lead; the house note monthly. (c) Neither; the brief and the team home are enough. Each commits Sŏn to the refusals: no figure, nothing about a person, no code name.

## 6. A chat tool and the off-shift rule

**What the white paper holds.** Systems are designed for the people who use them (WP p. 17); communication expectations are promised on the candidate sheet (WP p. 18); the OS surface is for the leads, the team home is the team's (2.2.13). No chat tool named or refused.

**From the digest.** The book's practice is yes, with the retention caution; the open question is whether Sŏn wants one at all given most staff are not at a computer during a shift. The off-shift flag: "available to read" versus "owed a response."

**Considerations.**
- Most restaurants already run on a group text nobody decided to create. The choice is not whether staff message each other, it is whether the house provides a place with rules or inherits one without them.
- The off-shift rule is legal as much as cultural: an employer that expects reading and replying off the clock is asking for unpaid work. The rule needs counsel's confirmation and needs to be on the candidate sheet.
- A person without a smartphone or a data plan exists. The written brief on paper at the building and the team home on a shared device cover them, if someone decides they will.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Scheduling tools for restaurants commonly include messaging; many operators state in writing that off-shift messages need no response until the next shift, both as a courtesy and to stay clear of wage claims.

**Options for 4.8.8.** (a) A chat tool for schedule notices, swaps, and links to the written brief only; the off-shift rule written and on the candidate sheet. (b) No chat tool; the team home and the schedule tool carry everything, and the house says nothing about staff's own group texts. (c) A chat tool with a broader scope and the record rule enforced by habit. Commits Sŏn to decisions leaking into chat.

## 7. Peer recognition

**What the white paper holds.** Stated outright: "peer recognition runs through a platform that lets colleagues acknowledge excellence in the moment, which lands differently than management praise because it comes from the people who understand the work" (WP p. 19). Nothing on what a recognition may say, how it is read, or how one is declined. 4.5.13 puts recognitions in the brief's slot; 4.5.8 rules on correcting a person in front of others; 4.5.19's card says a person's words are kept as said; 4.5.23 decides which moments the house marks.

**From the digest.** The old work reconciled recognition with a no-public-callout norm by treating it strictly as a peer's own words about a specific action, never a rating or comparison; declined recognitions are told to the author privately; repeated declines prompt a look at the platform's prompts. The risk named: recognition drifts into rankings, leaderboards, or manager-authored praise unless kept peer-driven and action-specific. A peer's own words carry only the customer word, flagged as needing a final answer.

**Considerations.**
- Recognition tools sell points and leaderboards. A leaderboard is a ranking of people, which is the thing the brief refuses (4.5.13). The specification (4.8.18) has to turn those off or fall back to paper.
- "In the moment" and "off shift" collide: a prompt to recognize someone sent at midnight is an off-shift message (4.8.8).
- A recognition entering a review (5.4) turns peers into raters. The white paper's "lands differently because it comes from peers" depends on it staying a gift.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Peer-recognition tools are widely used in offices and commonly come with points and public feeds; restaurants more often use a shout-out at the pre-shift, which is the same thing with no record.

**Options for 4.8.9.** (a) The old work's rule whole: a peer's words, a specific action, one service; no rating, count, or review input; declines private; the customer word is the only writing rule on a peer's words. (b) The same, read only at the brief and never posted where it accumulates into a count. (c) Recognition with light points but no ranking. Commits Sŏn to arguing where points end and ranking begins.

## 8. The feedback channel

**What the white paper holds.** Stated outright: "structured feedback runs through a dedicated channel that syncs to the execution system, so employees can surface issues, suggest improvements, and see their input lead to action. Feedback without follow-through breeds cynicism. The loop gets closed" (WP p. 22). "Team members weigh in on management effectiveness, operational improvements, and culture" (WP p. 19). 2.3.16 set the windows and three closing states; 4.5.11 named the destination for a concern about one's own lead and a founder; 4.5.17 is the page.

**From the digest.** The risk: without a rule against naming a coworker or a lead, the channel becomes a place where people are discussed without recourse. The open structural question about a concern with one's own manager is 4.5.11's now.

**Considerations.**
- Anonymity buys candor and costs the loop: an anonymous item cannot be answered to its author. The honest design offers both and says what each costs.
- "Management effectiveness" (WP p. 19) is a legitimate subject and a named lead is not. The line is between "the close's checklist is impossible in the time" and "the Maitre d is unfair."
- Whether other staff see open items is a transparency choice (2.2.6): visible items build trust and invite pile-ons.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies with a working suggestion channel almost always publish the item's state (received, in progress, done, declined with a reason); the ones that fail are the ones that go quiet.

**Options for 4.8.10.** (a) Process and tools only; named people refused and redirected to 4.5.11; anonymous allowed with no reply; states visible to the submitter; open items visible to all. (b) The same with open items visible only to the submitter and the leads. (c) No anonymity. Commits Sŏn to fewer items and more attributable ones.

## 9. The phone

**What the white paper holds.** "The way the phone is answered" is as important as the plating of the final course (WP p. 20). "Reservations, identity, and the phone in software collapse a reservations manager and a lead host into one" (WP p. 22). AI is "never [used for] anything load-bearing" (WP p. 21). Preferences live in the system, "because memory leaves when the server does" (WP p. 27). The white paper does not say a synthesized voice answers the phone, or what one would disclose; the old work's reasoning rested on a brand principle and is not a source here. Until decided, a person answers. 3.3.19 decided the same question for training media.

**From the digest.** The old work: whoever or whatever answers writes reservation changes and notes to the customer record, complaints to the incident log, and passes a staff-directed message on without recording it; a message that exists only in phone software is a gap. Which languages the phone system handles was never confirmed.

**Considerations.**
- Is the phone team communication at all? It is here because 2.2 had no task for it and 3.3.19's rule wanted a customer-facing counterpart. The decision is short; the build is Dominic's.
- The three positions are a person always; software with a disclosure in its first sentence; software without disclosure. The white paper's discipline of never asserting the unproven to a customer (WP p. 36) bears on the third without settling it.
- A caller in a listed language the software cannot handle must reach a person; otherwise the interview-language promise stops at the door for customers too.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Several US states have enacted or proposed laws requiring automated systems to disclose that they are not human in certain interactions; the scope varies and counsel confirms what applies in Texas. Restaurants that use automated phone answering commonly disclose it at the start of the call.

**Options for 4.8.11.** (a) A person always. (b) Software may answer and says it is automated first. (c) Software may answer with no disclosure. Each commits Sŏn to the language rule and to a path to a person.

## 10. The exit conversation and former employees

**What the white paper holds.** Stated outright: "exit interviews read for what could have been better rather than filed and forgotten" (WP p. 23); "how people grow beyond the building" is "the longest measure of whether the environment did its job" (WP p. 23). Post-mortems are private (WP p. 17). 4.3.8 is the departure read (the seat's contents); 4.3.9 is what the team hears; 4.3.14 closes the wording with counsel; 5.10 holds the exit itself.

**From the digest.** The old work proposed an optional exit conversation on the person's last scheduled shift, in their language, about the workplace, not shared as a performance record; internally a departure is described as coverage only; externally the company confirms dates and title unless the person opts into more; and an annual opt-in, aggregate-only outreach to former employees, with counsel on data handling.

**Considerations.**
- The conversation cannot be held by the lead the person is leaving, when that is the reason, and the person should not have to say so to get a different holder.
- "Read as process at the house review" is the white paper's use; anything else turns it into a record about the person after they have gone.
- Post-departure contact is a consent matter first and a culture measure second.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Alumni networks are common in consulting and technology and rare in restaurants, though the industry's informal version (a chef's former cooks) is one of its oldest.

**Options for 4.8.13.** (a) The old work's design whole. (b) The conversation only, no outreach. (c) Neither; the departure read (4.3.8) and the pulse carry it. Each commits Sŏn to the process-only use of what is said.

## 11. The reach review

**What the white paper holds.** "Sequence corruption: information is not flowing to where it is needed before it is needed ... The fix is system maintenance, not people management" (WP p. 14). 2.2.11 defined "reached" (every open period's brief since the version marks it; the team home shows it in every house language; a person's check-in dates after it) and handed the record to Dominic's domain.

**From the digest.** The old work proposed a weekly check at the leads' review and a quarterly one at the house review of whether changes reached every shift and language on time, read as a process gap and never as a person's fault; and that a change reaching the floor before the affected person is itself a diagnostic signal about where the process breaks.

**Considerations.** The record is only worth building if someone reads it. The leads' review is the natural weekly reader; the house review sees the quarter. What the review may change is the channel, the window, and the translation step, and it should say so, or a bad quarter produces a search for a culprit.

**Options for 4.8.14.** (a) Weekly at the leads' review from the system's record; quarterly pattern at the house review; misses logged as steps. (b) Quarterly only. (c) Read only when someone reports learning something late. Commits Sŏn to the failure being noticed by the person it failed.

## 12. Where Sŏn diverges from the book, in one place

- The book's team persists and meets weekly; Sŏn's crew dissolves at close, so the record, not the meeting, is the unit of communication.
- The book's pass-down comes from a manager's manager; Sŏn's comes from a weekly review the lead sat in, and the floor is one step from the founders.
- The book has one language; Sŏn has a list, and every page and every brief carries it.
- The book's snippet is desk work; at Sŏn every written line is paid time or unpaid work.
- The book's chat channel is where the team lives; Sŏn's staff live on the floor and on a phone off shift, where the law about what they owe is different.
