# 3.2 Hiring: what the book says

Full text: `extraction/build/s06/book.md` (pp. 193 to 215) and `extraction/build/s06/exercises.md` (pp. 228 to 259). This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn. ## Where this section sits

Chapter 3 runs recruiting (3.1), hiring (this section), onboarding (3.3), and hiring mistakes (3.4). Hiring begins once the recruiter and hiring manager screens have produced a pipeline, "which includes efforts to solicit internal referrals and to actively source more diverse candidates if your pipeline isn't sufficiently diverse" (p. 193). It ends when the offer is accepted, which she calls "just the precursor to the next phase of hiring": orienting the new person in their first weeks (p. 215).

## The frame: a set process with clear frameworks for assessment

Interviewing is a skill that improves with practice, and it works best "within a set process, with clear frameworks for assessment" (p. 193). Nothing elaborate: a simple framework for what all interviews focus on, a clear sense of the steps a candidate will go through, and a plan for how the offer decision is reached. Candidates should be prepped, not with the questions but by being set at ease: how long the process takes, the types of interview, what is expected, dress code, who they will meet (p. 193). Her own nine-hour interview day at a consulting firm is the counterexample; Stripe's candidate guide, sent from the early days, is the model, and she holds that "a more welcoming and fairer candidate experience leads to better hires" (p. 193).

Her general guidelines for setting up an interview process (pp. 193 to 194):

- Have a clear job description.
- Determine who will conduct the interviews.
- Cap the number of people a candidate meets at eight; four or five for entry-level, well-understood roles. Google's studies showed diminishing returns beyond that.
- Train the interviewers.
- Share the assessment rubric with every interviewer and assign each one or two elements of it, to avoid overlap.
- For custom or infrequently filled roles, hold a kickoff so everyone aligns on the role, the rubric, and their part.
- Consider a first round of three interviews to narrow the field before the full panel meets the finalists, ideally at least three candidates.
- Run a hiring committee or candidate review to finalize the selection.
- Have the recruiter and hiring manager check references.
- Make the offer, set a start date, send onboarding information.

The process flexes with stage and volume. High-volume roles can run faster with a deeper shared understanding of the role and may eventually drop hiring committee meetings; bespoke roles run a heavier version (p. 194). She adds that the recruiting leader is critical to a whole-company process that repeatedly produces high-quality hires, and that a process- and measurement-oriented person on recruiting operations is worth adding early (p. 194).

## Interviewing education

Even twenty-year interviewers should refresh their skills; STAR (situation, task, action, result) is one available framework. The most effective tactic is consistency: "ask the same questions of every candidate, while also leaving space in the interview to probe" (p. 194).

The four most common interviewer mistakes (pp. 194 to 195):

- Not using the rubric, so different candidates get different questions and there is no way to benchmark an excellent answer.
- Not interviewing for the capabilities the role most needs.
- Assessing whether they like the candidate rather than whether the candidate will succeed in the role.
- Over-prioritizing the right candidate experience rather than the right trajectory and aptitude to learn.

The best interviews find out how someone works with other people, gets quality work done themselves, motivates and develops themselves, has or can develop the expertise the role needs, and demonstrates leadership and resilience (p. 195).

Her own test in every interview is self-awareness and learning, which go together. Ask how their colleagues would describe them; if it is all positive, probe what constructive feedback they have received; then ask what they did to improve (p. 195). Watch "I" versus "we": too much "I" flags low humility or collaboration, too much "we" hides what role they played; she prefers "we" and always asks about the specific role. Eric Yuan's line beside it: whether they really want to learn is the number one thing he looks for (p. 195).

Résumé-heavy interviews are ineffective, especially early in a career. One résumé question breaks the ice; after that, get the candidate thinking about scenarios or about the specific role they played. Good interviews mix situational ("How would you handle this scenario?"), behavioral ("Tell me about a challenge you overcame"), and strict competency questions ("Describe the last model you built and how you approached the design") (p. 195). Behavioral questions are where company values enter without being named: if a principle is "put customers first," ask for examples of work and see whether they bring customers up at all (p. 196). A written exercise puts the candidate in a realistic work scenario and tests how they structure thinking (p. 196).

Hiring processes and interviewing skills need to be "well-defined and rigorously observed and measured" to prevent mistakes and bias. And the candidate experience matters because most candidates are rejected, and for each of them the process is the one experience they will have of the company (p. 196).

## The hiring process for non-leader roles

### Interviewing (pp. 196 to 198)

Scaled roles draw on a wide interviewer pool and panels vary with schedules; include a hiring manager or a proxy. For less frequently hired and leadership roles, have the same people interview every candidate, for calibration and pattern recognition, and include a cross-section of seniority and functions the candidate will work with (p. 196).

Once leadership can no longer meet every candidate, she suggests something like Amazon's Bar Raiser program: an exclusive group of interviewers who are stewards of the standard and culture, one on every panel, never from the hiring team, running the equivalent of the hiring committee meeting and able to veto single-handedly. Amazon invests heavily in training them and treats the role as an honor (p. 197). Stripe's version was the Elevate interviewer, selected on performance data, interview experience, and assessment ability, read from the applicant tracking system's history of "definitely," "yes," "no," and "not sure" responses against how hires later performed (p. 197). Elevate has been hard to scale and not every division used it, but she remains convinced such a program sends a message about strong hiring; the Elevate questions were whether the candidate will act like an owner, think rigorously, and be curious and a learner (p. 198).

Sidebar, Stripe's offer-approval process (p. 197): leaders could no longer meet every candidate but still signed off on every offer. Channels per functional area; recruiters posted the planned offer in a consistent format (proposed level, a link to the hiring committee summary, all interviewer and reference feedback); leaders committed to a 24-hour turnaround, reviewed, approved, or asked questions. Vetoes were rare, on lukewarm feedback or references. Five-plus years on, it is still in use.

### Decision-making (p. 198)

Before interviewing at any scale, be clear about who makes the hire or no-hire decision. She does not believe hiring managers should be the sole participants in selection; the better model is a consultative process, run consistently, that still puts the hiring manager as primary decision-maker because they are accountable for the team's results, with checks and balances such as final review by a committee or executive. Stripe's early check was an executive interviewing every candidate before a final decision, and often deciding not to hire, which taught people to consider candidates more deeply; the check was scaled back once the process was codified. Google's founders reviewed every hiring packet, then delegated to divisional leaders.

### Job level selection (pp. 198 to 202)

The next decision for an offer is job level, which determines compensation. Early companies rarely have levels and ladders cemented, but a basic structure is worthwhile; her sales leader's line: "How am I supposed to set expectations and compensation without that?" (p. 198). Table 3 sketches six levels by years of relevant experience and scope, from entry-level postgrad to senior director or executive (p. 199). Years can be a red herring; the ropes-and-knots analogy in Table 4 describes the six levels instead by knowledge, job complexity, and supervision (pp. 199 to 201). She cautions against overworking job ladders, which turn into checklists employees meet to get promoted; skills are easy to check off, capabilities are not, and capabilities matter more as someone advances (p. 199).

Everything after the level is compensation: salary bands and target offer points per level, a compa-ratio approach to avoid negotiation and bias, an annual pay parity review, her view that jobs should have set salaries with all motivators as bonus and equity, compensation as a company's largest expense, and planning by headcount versus by budget (pp. 199 to 202). This manual carries none of the figures; the compensation material belongs to 5.5.

### Candidate selection (pp. 202 to 204)

Stripe has two core approaches. Early on there was a hiring committee discussion for every candidate; now synchronous committee discussion is the default only for disagreement, a new role, or a unique position, and for all senior roles. In the consultative approach, the recruiter summarizes feedback and facilitates; the hiring manager consults the committee and concludes with a recommendation to decline or to pursue references and, pending those, offer (p. 203). Recruiters are measured on funnel efficiency and, above all, the quality of hires, and are expected to be the first to call out concerning feedback (p. 203). The hiring manager owns the decision to recommend a hire to final review but consults the other interviewers; and anyone on the hiring committee can veto a hire, which is extremely rare, once in her memory, when a candidate's answers made an interviewer personally uncomfortable (pp. 203 to 204).

Whatever the approach, the stewards of the process aim to (p. 204):

- Guard the candidate experience; for experienced candidates the process should feel like a conversation and a mutual learning experience.
- Really listen to interviewers' input.
- Bring the organization along, so everyone trusts the decision and supports new hires.
- Decide based on what is best for the company, not the team or the individual; there is never a good reason to compromise on a hire, however urgent the vacancy or however well-connected the referrer.

Sidebar, candidate review (pp. 204 to 207): Stripe's engineering process, designed for high volume and for hires matched to a team after the process. A panel of a lead, reviewers, and admins meets several times a week to review interview feedback, hire and no-hire recommendations, and level decisions for every candidate who reached the onsite stage. Its mission is a consistent, efficient, and transparent decision-making framework that seeks to control for bias. Four means: a standardized interview process tied to ladders and levels, verified by a consistent decision framework; consistency, which lets a shared understanding develop of how interview performance maps to impact; transparency, with feedback back to interviewers on feedback unrelated to the rubric, obvious bias, gendered pronouns, or narrow role fit; and bias control through consistent application and measured results, reviewed against self-identification data. The correct decision is defined as a person expected to at least meet expectations in their first performance review at the approved level. Six outcomes: hire at suggested, higher, or lower level; no hire with the committee overturned or confirmed; sent back for more information. Five sequential steps: submission, scorecard review, scorecard verification, discussion and decision and résumé review, submit the review scorecard. Roles: the lead ratifies outcomes and feedback; reviewers check whether recommendations are correct, whether scorecards reflect the rubric or are subjective, and whether the process was followed; the admin queues packets and records outcomes.

### Checking references (pp. 207 to 208)

"Not making reference calls is a mistake" (p. 207): interviews were only a set of 30- to 45-minute conversations, and references are more data. The recruiter and hiring manager share the calls; entry-level roles may need one call, made by the recruiter; the more senior the role, the more references, and the hiring manager should make at least one, ideally to the current manager. The candidate chose the references, so a lukewarm one is a strong signal: "Do not hire that person." Ask whether the person is in the top 50 percent of people they have worked with, then 20, 10, 5; people answer data questions honestly, and top 20 percent is not a ringing reference. Generic questions produce generic answers; her specific, behavioral ones: where they will be in three years; the last time you did not see eye to eye; ways they have been helpful to others; a time you coached them; a 1 to 10 rating with no 7 allowed; a skill you have seen them grow; and advice for me as a manager to help them succeed.

### Making the offer (pp. 208 to 209)

The offer letter is a critical document and any stock plan is a legal agreement; automate generating the correct documents early, because a wrong figure destroys trust (her flipped salary-and-equity anecdote). Never send a written offer before the recruiter or hiring manager has extended it verbally; "if anything about that offer is a surprise, your process has failed" (p. 208). The process is an extended conversation: understand motivation early, share high-level compensation information, explain the job ladder and where they might fall, and be clear if the company does not use formal titles. Share mutual feedback throughout to gauge and build interest. If questions remain after the offer, keep momentum: "Time kills all deals." Consider a sell chat with someone critical to the role or an executive; leaders should keep calendar flexibility for a 15-minute call. Candidates in those chats want to know how the leader sees the role, the company's prospects, what excites the leader, and the challenges; most of all they want to be inspired and to check that the answers match what they heard in the process (p. 209).

## The hiring process for leadership (pp. 209 to 215)

Leadership hires are the most bespoke part of hiring. Most of the core process translates; transparency and decision-making differ.

**Transparency.** Default to making a leadership search known internally, but be intentional about when. Hiring a first executive still layers whoever does that work today; tell them first. Replacing someone in a role is harder still: identify what you need and meet a few people, but do not bring anyone fully into a process until the current person's departure is agreed and announced. Leadership roles can take about six months to fill, so do not announce a new layer so early that people wait on an unnamed manager. On the candidate side, anyone in a top role elsewhere will rightly be paranoid about leaks: operate with discretion, keep calendars private, give only high-level updates internally (pp. 209 to 210).

**Decision-making.** A leadership hire is the CEO's or a top executive's decision, not a committee's, because people at other levels often do not know what leadership they need (the "faster horses" problem), may fear the change, and the hire's impact on business and culture needs a single accountable owner. Senior leaders should be judged as much on who and how well they recruit as on anything else. Even so, "woe betide you if you don't run a consultative process" (pp. 210 to 211).

**Managing the process as change management** (pp. 211 to 213). Treat the whole leadership hire as acceptance-building, "a form of starting onboarding before you even make the hire." Her steps:

- Screen so that at least two important people are excited about the candidate before involving others.
- Identify the main stakeholders: future direct reports, closely affected partner teams, teams gaining or losing scope. Do not exclude the people most likely to be negative; they will shape the leader's success, and the hiring manager still makes the call.
- Run an interview loop of key colleagues and stakeholder representatives. Send interviewers the reason the role is open, the job description, the key capabilities, the candidate's background, who has met them, brief feedback (not enough to bias), and each interviewer's focus and rubric.
- Set up the deliberation as a feedback-collection forum, not a decision forum: the hiring manager summarizes the role and needs, gives candidate overviews, has the recruiter summarize feedback, asks questions, summarizes what was heard, names concerns to probe, gives a decision timeline, and reports back with the reasons.
- Announce the hire internally: remind people of the process, celebrate the person, explain why they are right for the company and the role.

**Hiring internally** (pp. 213 to 214). Decide whether there is one internal candidate or several. If truly one, even after an internal posting, do not run a sham process; communicate the promotion thoughtfully, tell those affected first, celebrate, and suss out friction (share the news with a few partners or the new direct reports and watch the reaction). Meeting a few external candidates, and saying so, can validate an internal choice and signal an open process. If there might be more than one candidate, run a lightweight internal process: the hiring manager and one or two leaders interview each, then a committee discussion; give the unselected a feedback loop on what to develop; keep candidates confidential so the unselected can return to their roles quietly; and message the selection with what you looked for, the process run, and why the person emerged.

**Interviewing leaders** (p. 214). The experience trap: experienced leaders are good at interviewing. Allow enough time, test curiosity and desire to learn, and be wary of a candidate who answers a scenario with what they did before without asking about your context. Seek details: what they specifically did, whether it succeeded; watch for staying high-level or obfuscating outcomes.

**Hiring disagreements** (pp. 214 to 215). If you think a leadership hire is great and the organization disagrees: do the homework (references, an outside adviser or board member meeting the candidate), summarize the feedback for the concerned, really understand why if everyone is against it, and if homework and gut still say yes, make the hard call, make the intuition concrete, explain the thinking, and ask explicitly for commitment. The opposite case happens too; in one, three direct reports who had met every finalist and agreed the rubric in advance rightly swayed a CEO. When committees are wrong it is usually because they included inexperienced people. The stronger the process and the closer to the work the interviewers, the more weight their view can carry.

**References and offers for leaders** (p. 215). Higher stakes, more time. The candidate will not want current colleagues called, and it is not acceptable to press; go deep on prior companies and ask for someone they trust at the current one. Back-channel references are risky and, done wrong, undermine confidentiality; ask permission in her form: "I know some folks who've worked with you in the past. Is it okay if I reach out to them confidentially?" Title and scope should be clear from a good process; compensation negotiation should be handled by someone other than the CEO or hiring manager (a recruiter, search firm, CFO, or compensation lead), both to protect the new reporting relationship and because a candidate may assume the CEO can overrule the framework. Acceptance is the precursor to onboarding, which is every bit as important (p. 215).

## Exercises and templates that serve this section

**Interview framework and rubric (pp. 229 to 245; workbook pp. 43 to 66).** One role worked in full, recruiting for recruiters: the competencies assessed; the process (recruiter screen; team screen run in tandem with a written project; four onsite interviews of 45 minutes each, each assigned one or two competencies); per stage, who can run it, its length, a talk track or documented questions with situation, action, and result follow-ups, and a rubric of three or four columns from poor to strong with written anchors per criterion. The structured-thinking interview is a roleplay in three parts (define the scenario, build a solution, measure success) with an evaluation guide. The written project asks for two existing writing samples, graded on structure, grammar, and tone, with score bands for pass, second set of eyes, and fail.

**Sample interview questions (pp. 246 to 247; workbook pp. 67 to 69).** Less common questions grouped by working with others, getting work done, personal motivation, and leadership, each with what to seek and, sometimes, what to avoid. Examples: a time you worked on something you knew you would not enjoy; the lowest-morale team you have been part of; a project you led that failed and your role in the failure; how you balance competing demands; the best leader you have worked for and their weaknesses; the best hire you ever made.

**Written exercise example (pp. 248 to 250; workbook pp. 70 to 72).** A product manager exercise: analyze churn data and propose a retention roadmap in a few pages, about three hours of work. Graded out of 12 on written communication, data analysis, customer sense, and product sense, with score bands mapped to decisions.

**Candidate review: decision-making framework (pp. 250 to 252; workbook pp. 73 to 77).** The five steps in full: the packet (résumé, interview scorecards, a hiring committee scorecard with a five-point symbol per interview, a suggested outcome including the rare "yes for the company, no for my team," a suggested level); review of the committee scorecard and the hiring manager's rationale, including resolved fear, uncertainty, and doubt; verification that individual scorecards reflect the rubric and are neither too lenient nor too strict; discussion, decision, and a résumé check against level guidelines; and submission of the review scorecard with feedback to interviewers, who can feed back in turn.
