# 3.2 Hiring: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 3, pp. 193 to 215, with the chapter's exercises and templates at pp. 228 to 259. Full text: `extraction/build/s06/book.md`, exercises at `extraction/build/s06/exercises.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## A set process, well explained

Interviewing is a skill that improves with practice, best used within a set process: a simple framework for what interviews focus on, clear tactical steps for the candidate, and a plan for how the offer decision gets made (p. 193). Candidates should be prepped, not coached: explain the process, its length, the interview types, and what's expected, without telegraphing questions (p. 193). A candidate guide that does this well earns real goodwill; a rushed, unexplained process does the opposite (pp. 193 to 194).

## General guidelines for a strong interview process (pp. 193 to 194)

These apply from a young company's first hires through senior roles later on:

- Have a clear job description.
- Determine who will conduct the interviews.
- Cap candidates at a maximum of eight people met, fewer (four or five) for well-understood entry-level roles; Google research found diminishing returns past that.
- Train interviewers on effective interviewing.
- Share the assessment rubric with all interviewers and assign one or two elements to each, to avoid overlap.
- For custom or infrequently filled roles, hold a kickoff meeting to align everyone on the role, the rubric, and their part.
- Consider a first round of three interviews to narrow the field before the full panel engages, then a final round with the remaining three meeting at least three finalists.
- Run a hiring committee or candidate review process to finalize the selection.
- Have the recruiter and hiring manager check references.
- Make the offer, set a start date, send onboarding information.

Process weight should flex with volume: high-volume, well-understood roles can run a faster, higher-bandwidth process (sometimes without hiring committee meetings); bespoke, infrequent roles need the heavier version, since interviewers will need more grounding in what's being sought (p. 194).

The recruiting leader matters enormously to whether this process produces high-quality hires at scale, and being a strong recruiter personally doesn't guarantee being good at building the tooling, process, and measurement underneath it. Putting a process- and measurement-oriented person on recruiting operations early pays off (p. 194).

## Interviewing education

Consistency is the most effective interviewing tactic: ask every candidate the same questions, while leaving room to probe answers and get at root behaviors and abilities. STAR (situation, task, action, result) is named as a reasonably good framework in the market (p. 194).

Most common interviewer mistakes (pp. 194 to 195): not using the rubric; asking different questions of different candidates so there's no way to benchmark a good answer; not interviewing for the capabilities the role actually needs; assessing likability instead of role success; overweighting candidate experience relative to trajectory and aptitude to learn.

What the best interviews test for (p. 195): how someone works with others, gets quality work done themselves, motivates and develops themselves, has or can develop the needed expertise, and demonstrates leadership and resilience.

Her own tests for self-awareness and learning orientation: ask how colleagues would describe them; if only positive, probe for constructive feedback received, then ask what they did to improve. Watch "I" versus "we": too much "I" flags a possible lack of humility or collaboration; too much "we" can obscure their actual role, worth clarifying. She prefers more "we" (p. 195). Eric Yuan is quoted: wanting to learn matters more to him than background (p. 195).

Question types to mix: résumé-based (useful as an icebreaker and for early-career candidates, but weak alone), situational ("how would you handle this scenario"), behavioral ("tell me about a challenge you overcame"), and strict competency questions. Behavioral questions are also the best place to probe company values indirectly, for example asking for examples of keeping the customer top of mind without naming the value, to see if it surfaces unprompted (pp. 195 to 196). A written exercise is another good way to see how a candidate structures thinking and solves problems (p. 196).

Candidate experience matters independent of outcome: most candidates will be rejected, and for nearly all of them that's their only experience of the company. A rejected candidate sending a thank-you note is her marker of a strong, candidate-first process (p. 196).

## Hiring for non-leader roles

**Interviewing.** Scaled roles draw from a wide interviewer pool that varies by schedule; less-frequently-hired and leadership roles do better with the same people interviewing every candidate, for calibration and pattern recognition, plus a cross-section of seniority and function beyond the immediate team, for example including legal on an HR partner's panel (pp. 196 to 197).

**Bar raiser and Elevate-style programs.** Once leadership can no longer meet every candidate, a program modeled on Amazon's Bar Raiser (from *Working Backwards*) helps preserve standards: an exclusive group of trained interviewers, never from the hiring team, sit on every panel, run the hiring-committee-equivalent conversation, and can single-handedly veto a candidate. It doubles as recognition for people who embody the culture (pp. 197 to 198). Stripe's version, "Elevate," selected high performers using historical interview-feedback data from the applicant tracking system: for candidates not hired, whether the interviewer was opinionated enough to say no; for candidates hired, whether "definitely" or "yes" assessments correlated with how they performed (p. 197). Its core questions: will the candidate act like an owner, be a rigorous thinker, and be curious and a learner (p. 198).

**Sidebar: Stripe's offer-approval process (pp. 197 to 198).** As Stripe grew past leadership meeting every candidate, they built Slack channels per functional area with recruiters and approving leaders, a 24-hour turnaround commitment, and a consistent offer template (level, hiring committee summary, interviewer and reference feedback). Vetoes were rare but happened when feedback and references were lukewarm.

**Decision-making.** Before interviewing at scale, be clear who makes the hire or no-hire call. Her preferred model: a consultative process, run consistently, with the hiring manager (or equivalent) as the primary decision-maker, since they're accountable for the team's results, plus checks and balances such as a committee or executive review (p. 198). Stripe's early practice of having an executive interview every candidate before a final decision was later scaled back as the process was codified; she compares this to Google founders reviewing every hiring packet before delegating that to divisional leaders (pp. 198 to 199).

**Job level selection.** Compensation follows from job level, so putting a basic level structure in place early is worth it even before it's fully cemented (p. 198). Her summary table of levels 1 through 6+ runs from entry-level postgrad to senior director or executive, defined loosely by years of experience plus scope and impact (p. 199, Table 3). Years of experience is flagged as a red herring, especially in engineering, where impact varies widely at the same tenure (p. 199). An alternative "ropes and knots" analogy (from a compensation consultant) describes each level's knowledge, job complexity, and supervision expectations without leaning on years (pp. 199 to 201, Table 4). Her caution: job ladders that get too detailed become checklists employees game for promotion rather than genuine capability development (p. 199).

**Compensation mechanics.** Each level typically has a salary band and target offer points; Stripe uses a compa-ratio (an individual's pay divided by the band midpoint) so the recruiter and hiring manager recommend one of two points rather than negotiating, reducing bias and increasing efficiency (pp. 199 to 200, Figure 13). A more experienced candidate at a given level might land higher in the band. Stripe also runs an annual pay parity review to keep similar performers at the same level paid similarly (p. 200). Her broader view: salaries should be set by role, with compensation motivation living in bonus and equity, which also simplifies internal transfers since a salary change reflects the market rather than a judgment of worth (p. 201). Compensation is also central to annual planning, since it's usually a company's largest expense; younger companies may plan in ranges (e.g., a Level 2 or 3) rather than fixed levels, and she favors giving planners a budget with flexibility over fixed headcount by level (pp. 201 to 202).

**Candidate selection process.** Stripe runs two core approaches (Figure 14, p. 203): synchronous hiring committee discussion (now reserved for disagreement, role newness, or uniqueness, and standard for all senior roles) and candidate review, described below. In the hiring-committee approach, the recruiter summarizes feedback and facilitates; a hiring manager, where present, consults the committee and concludes with a recommendation. Recruiters are measured primarily on funnel efficiency and candidate quality, not volume. Anyone on a hiring committee can veto a hire, an extremely rare but important safeguard (pp. 203 to 204).

Goals for whoever stewards the process (pp. 204 to 205): guard the candidate experience; really listen to interviewer input; bring the organization along so it trusts the decision; and decide based on what's best for the company, not the team or the referrer.

**Sidebar: Candidate review, CR (pp. 204 to 207, and the fuller framework at pp. 250 to 252).** Built for Stripe's high-volume engineering hiring, where a candidate is often matched to a team only after the process concludes. CR reviews interview feedback, hire/no-hire recommendations, and leveling for most engineering roles. Its mission: fill engineering roles with high-caliber candidates through a consistent, efficient, transparent, bias-controlled decision framework. Roles: CR lead (ensures consistent application, leads discussion, ratifies the outcome, attends calibration sessions across panels), CR reviewer (reviews feedback against the rubric, includes a recruiting team member), CR admin (runs logistics, queues packets, records outcomes). Six possible outcomes: hire at suggested/higher/lower level; no hire with the hiring committee outcome overturned or confirmed; or TBD pending more information. The five-step framework: candidate submission; hiring committee scorecard and rationale review (scored strong no to strong yes per interview, mapped to the rubric); scorecard verification (checking scorecards reflect the rubric, aren't too lenient or strict, aren't subjective); discussion, decision, and résumé review (checking the outcome against years-of-experience guidelines); and submitting the CR scorecard with feedback looped back to interviewers, recruiters, and hiring managers.

**Checking references.** Skipping reference calls is a mistake; interviews are only 30 to 45 minutes each, and references add real data. Entry-level roles may need only one call; more experienced candidates and higher-impact roles warrant more, and the hiring manager should personally make at least one, ideally to the current manager (p. 207). A lukewarm reference from someone chosen by the candidate is a real signal to walk away (p. 207). Her preferred question, asked once the reference is comfortable: "top 50 percent? Top 20, 10, 5?" People tend to answer honestly when asked for a number, more so than in response to open-ended prompts like "what are their strengths and weaknesses" (pp. 207 to 208). Other specific, behavioral reference questions she uses (p. 208): where do you see this person in three years; when did you last not see eye to eye; how have they been helpful to others; tell me about a time you coached them; rate them 1 to 10 (you can't say 7); what skill have you seen them grow; what advice do you have for me as their manager.

**Making the offer.** Offer letters and equity terms are legal documents; automate generation early, since an error (her example: salary and equity figures accidentally swapped) is trust-destroying. Never send a written offer before it's been extended verbally, so nothing in it is a surprise (p. 208). The recruiting process is an extended conversation: understand motivation and career fit early, discuss compensation expectations, and explain the job ladder and leveling, especially where external titles don't map to internal levels (pp. 208 to 209). Momentum matters at close ("time kills all deals"); a short "sell chat" with someone central to the role, or an executive, signals the candidate matters. Candidates in these calls typically want to know how the leader sees the role's importance, the company's medium- and long-term prospects, what excites the leader, and what the challenges are (p. 209).

## The hiring process for leadership

Leadership hires are the most bespoke part of hiring at any stage, driven by role clarity, confidentiality needs, and stakes. Two things differ most from the core process: transparency and decision-making (pp. 209 to 210).

**Transparency.** Default toward internal transparency about an open leadership search, but be intentional about timing, especially when "layering" (a new hire coming in above an existing person) or replacing someone in role. Lay groundwork with affected people first, using honest feedback about their impact and experience, and avoid announcing a new layer too early since a search can take about six months. Early groundwork (defining the role, building a pipeline) before going public shortens the wait once it's announced (pp. 209 to 210). When replacing someone in an existing role (her general counsel example), don't begin candidate conversations, even confidential ones, until the incumbent's departure has been discussed and ideally a mutual transition agreed. On the candidate side, confidentiality must be strictly honored: private calendars, only high-level updates to internal audiences (p. 210).

**Decision-making.** She believes a leadership hire should be decided by the CEO or top executive, not a group committee, because a group often doesn't know what leadership it needs (the "faster horses" problem), may fear the change a new leader brings, and because the impact is large enough that the person hiring must be accountable for it. Even so, run a consultative process; being the final decision-maker doesn't mean deciding alone (pp. 210 to 211).

**Managing the process as change management.** She frames leadership hiring as change management and acceptance-building, effectively onboarding before the hire is even made, since a new leader is like introducing a foreign substance into an organization's body, at risk of "organ rejection" (p. 211). Her steps (pp. 211 to 213):

1. Screen the candidate so at least two important people are excited about them before investing wider organizational time.
2. Identify the main stakeholders affected: direct reports, teams gaining or losing scope, key partner teams. Don't exclude people likely to react negatively; their buy-in matters even more, though their feedback isn't the only feedback.
3. Run an interview loop of key colleagues and stakeholder representatives, briefed in advance on why the role is open, the job description, key capabilities sought, and each interviewer's specific focus and rubric.
4. Set the hiring deliberation up explicitly as a feedback-collection forum, not a decision-making forum: the hiring manager states this up front, summarizes the role and candidates, has the recruiter summarize feedback, probes for understanding, and closes by naming areas of concern still to probe with the candidate or references, plus a timeline, followed later by an explanation of the decision made.
5. Announce the hire internally in a way that reminds people of the process, celebrates the person, and explains the fit.

## Hiring internally

When an earlier decision tree (p. 171, referenced but outside this session's page range) points to hiring from within, the same principles of bringing the team along apply, but the process can be lighter. First determine whether there's truly one internal candidate or several possible ones; don't run a sham process if only one person is realistic, but do communicate the change thoughtfully to those affected before a wider announcement (pp. 213 to 214). Rooting out friction ahead of an internal promotion: quietly gauge reactions from likely cross-functional partners or new direct reports before announcing; strong pushback may signal the wrong call, or that more change management is needed to support the choice made (p. 214). Meeting a few external candidates, even without investing as heavily as an external search, can validate the internal decision and demonstrate an open, inclusive process (p. 214). Even a lightweight internal process (hiring manager plus one or two leaders interviewing, followed by a committee discussion) builds trust and signals rigor; keep internal candidates confidential throughout, and give feedback to those not selected so they can develop toward future opportunities (pp. 214 to 215).

Interviewing internal or experienced leadership candidates carries its own trap: experienced people tend to be practiced interviewees. Don't be swayed by polish; probe with follow-ups for depth, test curiosity and desire to learn, and watch for candidates who describe past work without asking questions about the new context (p. 215, continuing to p. 215's top).

## Hiring disagreements

When a hiring manager or senior leader believes strongly in a candidate the rest of the organization doesn't support (or the reverse), the fix is more homework, not less: additional reference calls, an outside adviser or board member meeting the candidate, and a clear summary of feedback for the people with concerns. If the homework and instinct still point toward the hire, make the call, explain the reasoning, and explicitly ask for the team's commitment to help the new person succeed (pp. 215 to 216, continuing past this session's range). She also describes the reverse case, where a hiring group convinced a skeptical senior leader, crediting it to those advocates having met every finalist and pre-agreed on the rubric, evidence of a healthy process. Where a committee gets a call wrong, it's usually traceable to inexperienced participants on the panel.

## References and offers for leadership hires

Higher stakes, more time, and trickier references, since a leadership candidate often can't have their current employer contacted. Go deep on references from previous companies, and ask who they trust enough at their current company to be a discreet reference. Back-channel references are tempting but risky to confidentiality; her preferred approach is to ask the candidate directly for permission to reach out confidentially to specific known contacts. On the offer itself, have someone other than the CEO or hiring manager handle compensation negotiation, so the new leader's relationship with their manager doesn't start adversarially, and so a CEO isn't assumed to be able to override the compensation framework.

## Exercises and templates (pp. 228 to 259)

Full text at `extraction/build/s06/exercises.md`. All are Stripe-specific worked examples meant to be adapted, not copied.

- **Interview framework and rubric, recruiting-for-recruiters example (pp. 229 to 245).** A full worked interview loop for an L2+ recruiter role: competencies assessed (collaboration, conscientiousness, willingness to be wrong, intrinsic motivation, structured thinking, resilience, accountability), a recruiter screen, a team screen paired with a written project, and four onsite interviews, each with sample questions, follow-up probes, and a poor/good/strong (or poor/weak/good/strong) rubric.
- **Sample interview questions (pp. 246 to 248).** Organized by working with others, getting work done, personal motivation, and leadership, each with what the answer is meant to reveal.
- **Written exercise example (pp. 248 to 250).** A product manager churn-analysis prompt with a 12-point rubric (written communication, data analysis, customer sense, product sense) and a points-to-decision table.
- **Candidate review decision-making framework (pp. 250 to 252).** The detailed five-step version summarized above.
- **Manager transitions guide (pp. 252 to 253).** A three-step guide and email template for moving a report to a new manager: preview the transition, hold a joint transition meeting covering scope, strengths, development, and working preferences, then have the new manager follow up on action items.
- **Working with Claire, an example, and the Working with Me template (pp. 253 to 257).** Her own worked example covering operating approach, management style, communication norms, and a blank template with the same headings: my role, about me, operating approach, management style, supporting you and your team.
- **New Leader Experience, NLE (pp. 257 to 259).** Stripe's structured leadership onboarding program: welcome emails, prescheduled meetings, a leadership assessment (Hogan Personality Inventory), six months of coaching, first-month actions, and a 90-day 360 review. A week-by-week table names who (recruiter, onboarding point person, hiring manager, people partner, coach) does what, from offer acceptance through the three-month mark.
