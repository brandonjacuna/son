# 3.2 Hiring: considerations

Context for the decisions in `tasks.md`, in the book's order. Each topic gives what the white paper holds (cited as "WP p. N", including where it is silent), what the earlier extraction argued that is worth keeping, options with what each commits Sŏn to, and where Sŏn may diverge from the book. Nothing here is a verdict. "Read" means one thing an interview is trying to learn; "packet" means the set of scorecards and notes a hire decision rests on.

## 1. A set process, explained to candidates

**What the white paper holds.** Talent acquisition is built as a system that respects the candidate's time and reads competency rather than instinct (WP p. 17). The applicant system lets candidates self-schedule, choose language and format, and tells them what to expect at each step; high-volume roles such as line cook and porter get a short phone screen; format choice doubles as a filter (p. 17). Interviews are semi-structured with documented questions (p. 18). Roles with a physical skill component get a paid practical as the second interview, replacing the unpaid tryout shift (p. 18). Leadership and salaried roles end with a dinner in the room (p. 18). The transparency sheet is handed at the first interview (p. 18). Speed is respect; no one is ghosted (p. 18). The white paper is silent on how many people a candidate meets, who decides, and what a scorecard looks like.

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** A stage table by seat class: every seat gets an application, a first interview, a decision, and an answer; high-volume seats add the phone screen; physical-skill seats add the practical; leadership and salaried seats add the dinner; seats that own a domain add a second interviewer from the adjacent domain; before opening, every seat adds a founder stage. The book's ceiling of eight interviewers is not the binding constraint at Sŏn's size; the binding constraint is a floor: whether a packet may ever be one person's read alone. The book's kickoff meeting for a bespoke role has a natural substitute in a documented seat description that every interviewer reads. A candidate-facing note per stage (what it is for, how long, who is present by seat, what it reads and does not read, what to wear, when they will hear) does the work of the book's candidate guide (pp. 193 to 194). A realist's note: before opening, only two founders run forty-five to sixty minute first interviews for an entire cohort, on top of the build; the hiring calendar has to block that as real hours.

**Options for the stage table (3.2.4).**
- (a) The seat-class table above. Commits Sŏn to a different loop per class, a second trained interviewer for domain-owning seats, and a written rule about the smallest packet allowed.
- (b) One loop for every seat (screen, interview, practical or dinner, decision). Commits Sŏn to fewer rules and to running a dinner for seats where it may not fit, or a practical for seats with no physical skill.
- (c) The white paper's four named stages and nothing more until the first cohort shows what is missing. Commits Sŏn to revising the table after opening, with candidates already through it.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book's narrowing first round of three and final round of three finalists (p. 194) assumes a pipeline deep enough to hold three finalists per seat; Sŏn's first loops may have one. The book's kickoff meeting becomes a document.

## 2. What interviews read, and how

**What the white paper holds.** Interviews assess specific competencies, cultural alignment, and growth potential rather than gut feeling, and also whether the building is equipped to create the environment this person needs (WP p. 18). Every position has documented competency requirements visible to anyone (p. 11); new people enter at the outside edge (p. 9). Nothing in the white paper sets an experience requirement for any seat, and nothing says experience counts for nothing.

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** Four reads: competency for the strand at entry level; growth potential read as self-directed mastery; cultural alignment read as the absence of forbidden conduct plus comfort with distributed authority and with the customer as customer; and what the building must supply for this person to succeed, recorded in the candidate's words and never scored on them. A list of what an interview may not conclude: a felt fit not tied to a read; presence, charm, or energy; a competency the house is supposed to develop rather than require (scoring that as absent scores the house's obligation as the candidate's failure); load or tempo (only the practical can read that); month-six success. Question discipline: every read gets a situational, a behavioral, and where possible a strict competency question; probes follow situation, task, action, result; "what did you actually do" is the mandatory probe against vague "we" answers. The book's self-awareness test (how would colleagues describe you, what constructive feedback have you had, what did you do about it; p. 195) as an anchor question at every first interview. Anchors at three evidence levels, written for capacity that could show up in any kind of work, with no presence word at any level and the candidate's own word choices left unscored. The anchors are provisional until a cohort's real answers have tested them.

A risk named in the earlier work: later, higher-energy service periods select for a more extroverted profile, and the people doing later hiring were selected that way, producing a slow drift toward one kind of personality. The anchors and the practical are built to resist it but cannot see a pattern across many packets; only a cross-packet read can (topic 11).

**Options for how experience counts (3.2.3).**
- (a) No experience or credential requirement on any seat description; experience is neither for nor against a candidate, and prior fine-dining experience is probed with the book's experience-trap questions (p. 215). Commits Sŏn to anchors written for capacity, to training interviewers to notice when they are reaching for a résumé, and to a first cohort that may need more training time.
- (b) No requirement, but experience may count as evidence of capacity at the competency read. Commits Sŏn to defining what counts as evidence and to interviewers weighing it consistently.
- (c) Experience requirements on the lead and domain-owning seats only. Commits Sŏn to two rules, one for the edge and one for the center, and to explaining the difference on the sheet.

**Options for question publication (3.2.12).**
- (a) Questions public on the seat description, anchors private. Commits Sŏn to candidates who have prepared and to anchors that reward real evidence over rehearsed answers.
- (b) Question areas public, questions private. Commits Sŏn to the book's "prep, don't coach" line (p. 193).
- (c) Nothing public beyond the stage notes. Commits Sŏn to the industry default.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Structured interviews, the same questions asked of every candidate and scored against written anchors, are the best-evidenced interview form in the selection literature, and Google's people-operations work popularized them along with a small fixed number of interviewers. Work samples (a candidate doing a piece of the job) are among the strongest single predictors of later performance in that literature, which is the case for the practical carrying real weight. Danny Meyer's Union Square Hospitality Group has long said it hires for emotional skills first and technical skills second, an example of a house naming what it reads and holding to it.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book allows weighing trajectory against experience (p. 195); if a seat carries no experience requirement there is nothing to weigh it against, and the interviewer's job becomes noticing when they reach for it anyway. Values worked into behavioral questions without naming them (p. 196) waits on principles version one (2.1.5); until then the alignment read runs on the baseline conduct standard and distributed-authority questions only.

## 3. Who interviews, and how they are trained

**What the white paper holds.** Interviewers are trained, because asking a good question and reading the answer is a learned skill (WP p. 18). Training lives on a learning platform any qualified team member can build modules on and be paid for (p. 19). A candidate whose first language is not English can interview with someone who speaks it (p. 17). The white paper is silent on who trains the first interviewers, on a selected interviewer group, and on what happens when no one on the team speaks a candidate's language.

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** The founders are untrained by the record's own definition, and the platform that would train them does not exist before there is a team. Options argued: write the modules as documents first; the founders train each other by running the same sequence (modules, a live practice session, an observed stage with no scorecard, a paired stage with independently scored practice runs on consenting people who are not candidates, then solo), reading the practice scorecards together as their first calibration; keep an interim, dated training record by hand until the platform can issue one; treat the first real lead candidate's scorecards, read jointly, as the first real two-person calibration. An outside interviewing trainer was argued against on the ground that a bought product imports the industry's norms; that is an argument, not a ruling. The module outline: the reads and what each may and may not conclude; the seat description; question discipline; the anchors with worked examples written for training, never drawn from a real candidate; writing an evidence-based scorecard, including the presence-language scan and why a hit triggers re-training; what may never be said about pay or outcome; disqualifiers; stage-specific modules for screeners, assessors, and the dinner and founder stages; records rules (everything stays in the applicant system; a referrer never runs a stage for the person they referred). Re-training sends someone back to a module and one more paired stage, is never a sanction, and is recorded only on the training record.

**Options for training the founders (3.2.13).**
- (a) Documents first, founders train each other, interim record by hand. Commits the founders to writing the modules before the leads' loop, and to practice runs on people who are not candidates.
- (b) An outside trainer for the founders, with Sŏn's modules written afterward from what was learned. Commits Sŏn to a bought frame at the very first loop and to unlearning parts of it.
- (c) The first lead hire brings interviewing skill and trains the founders in turn. Commits Sŏn to running the lead loop untrained.

**Options for the interviewer pool (3.2.14).**
- (a) Every trained person in a strand interviews for that strand's seats; no selected class. Commits Sŏn to a wide pool and to calibration doing the work a bar raiser would.
- (b) A small group of trained people who embody the standard sit on every loop, in the book's Bar Raiser shape (pp. 197 to 198). Commits Sŏn to naming a class of culture stewards in a house of roughly eight leadership lines (WP p. 22), and to their hours on every packet.
- (c) The adjacent-domain interviewer (topic 1) is the outside voice, and there is no separate class. Commits Sŏn to that seat existing on every loop where it matters.

**Options for the language gap (3.2.15).** An interpreter in the room, with the interviewer still the trained one; the stage waits until a trained speaker exists, with the candidate told so; the promise is stated as "in your language where we can" until the team can honor it in full. Each commits Sŏn to saying, on the seat description, exactly what a candidate can expect.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Amazon's Bar Raiser program, as described in *Working Backwards*, is the best-known selected-interviewer model, and its own authors note it depends on a large pool of trained people. Most restaurants train no one to interview at all, which is the industry default the white paper names (WP p. 17).

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book's Elevate method selected interviewers by reading their history against outcomes (p. 197); Sŏn will not have the volume or the years for that, so anything that stands in must be a leading read rather than a lagging one (topic 11).

## 4. Deciding: who, on what, and how fast

**What the white paper holds.** Authority lives in the domain; the person who owns the domain is accountable to its outcomes (WP p. 7 to 8). When the skills and alignment are there the decision is made quickly and development starts immediately (p. 18). A company built to run without the founders (p. 6). The white paper is silent on a committee, an approval step, a veto, a scorecard, or a decision deadline.

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** The book's candidate-review mission, "a consistent, efficient, and transparent decision-making framework that seeks to control for bias" (p. 205), names four functions, not a form. A house that declines the committee still owes each function a home: consistent application (the seat description and kit); scorecard verification (partly the system's refusal of an incomplete scorecard, mainly a cross-packet read); verification by someone other than the decider (the founder stage before opening, then a real gap, topic 11); feedback to interviewers (whoever runs the calibration read); bias control through measurement (an honest pattern read on stage and source, with volume too small to claim significance); the veto (narrowed to a conduct flag anyone may raise with evidence); the "TBD, more information" outcome (a same-stage re-run rather than a send-back). The earlier work argued for the domain lead deciding alone on a complete packet of independently submitted scorecards, the day the packet completes, the answer sent the same day, with the applicant system refusing a decision on an incomplete packet or an active flag. It also argued for a scorecard with an evidence line per question, a mark per read (does not meet, meets, meets with evidence beyond, not assessed with reason), a disqualifier flag, and an advance-or-decline recommendation that names a read on any decline; and against a free-text verdict (an unauthorized second decision, the place a first impression gets written down, uncalibratable, and unusable in an answerable decline letter), a numeric scale or composite, a "not sure" value, and any field that writes to the employee record. The decider's reading order: flags first, then the building-side answers, then evidence before marks, practical rows before interview notes, recommendations last so the lead forms their own read first. A dissent line where the lead goes against a scorecard: the read, the interviewer's evidence, the evidence read as outweighing it; the interviewer told the same day; no appeal, since the preserved scorecard is the appeal; repeated overrides read as a pattern, first about the anchors, second about the interviewer, third about the lead. Five instruments were declined outright: a work-style or personality grid in interviews; résumé review and take-home assignments; a hiring committee or meeting; an outside recruiter's loop and scorecard; a bias-neutralizing copy tool. Each of those is an option again here.

**Options for who decides (3.2.7).**
- (a) The domain lead alone, on a complete packet, same day. Commits Sŏn to the packet doing the committee's work, to a hard rule that no one decides alone in the sense that no packet is one read, and to accepting that the outside check on entry-level packets is periodic, not per hire.
- (b) The domain lead decides on the packet, with a named second reader (the other lead, or a founder) who must have read it and may write a dissent but not overturn. Commits Sŏn to a second trained reader's hours on every packet.
- (c) A small standing group discusses each packet before the lead decides, in the book's committee shape (pp. 203 to 204). Commits Sŏn to a meeting per hire and to the calendar cost the white paper's lean leadership count cannot easily carry (WP p. 22).

**Options for the veto (3.2.8).** (a) Only a conduct disqualifier ends a candidacy, raisable by any interviewer with evidence, overridable by no one including a founder; competence disagreement is dissent, never veto. (b) Any interviewer may veto on any read, in the book's shape. (c) Only the decider or a founder may end a loop. Each commits Sŏn to a different answer to "who can stop a hire I want to make."

**Options for the founders' place before opening (3.2.6).** (a) A founder runs a short stage on every candidate, reading only the conduct standard and the building-side question, with a "no" that must name a gap; it ends for entry-level seats on a stated condition. (b) A founder meets every finalist in full, in the book's shape (p. 171), until a headcount. (c) No founder stage; the lead loop stands on its own from the first cohort. Each commits the founders to a different number of hours and a different answer to when the house is trusted to hire without them.

**Options for the decline (3.2.22).** (a) The decline names the read, in a sentence a candidate can act on, sent through the system within the stated window. (b) A clear no with no reason, on counsel's advice. (c) A no with an offer to talk. Each commits Sŏn to a different exposure and a different candidate experience; counsel's list (0.1) bears on it.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most companies that formalize hiring at all give the hiring manager the decision with some check above or beside it; the book's own preference is that shape (p. 198). Whole Foods was long known for letting a team vote on whether a new hire stayed after a trial period, a check that sits after the hire rather than before it. Amazon's Bar Raiser holds a per-hire veto by design.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book assumes a recruiter facilitates and summarizes (pp. 203 to 204); Sŏn has no recruiting seat, so the packet has to carry the summary itself. The book's standing veto (p. 203) may narrow to one kind of finding. The book's offer-approval channel with a leader's turnaround (pp. 197 to 198) has no one structurally above a lead to sit in it; the same protective work would have to be done by the system's refusals and by the calibration read.

## 5. The paid practical

**What the white paper holds.** For roles with a physical skill component, the second interview is a paid practical in a real environment, replacing the unpaid tryout shift, which is degrading, legally exposed, and a poor predictor (WP p. 18). Competency validation before anyone touches a table (p. 19). Creators may be offered a seat at a training day (p. 31). Silent on length, scoring, who assesses, and what a candidate is owed.

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** A practical is a scored block, not a shift: setup (procedure read), the first wave (load read, only when real load exists), and treatment of the people around the candidate throughout. Rows: one per entry-level procedure (reproduced, reproduced with correction, not reproduced), two universal rows (asked before guessing, cleaned as they went), one load row marked "not read" in a load-free training service and never inferred, conduct rows whose flag ends candidacy, a closing debrief question; no composite; a recommendation names its rows. The assessor is a trained person from the seat's strand who does not decide the hire and is not the person teaching, scheduled as an extra body; a first-time assessor is shadow-scored. What the practical may never conclude: month-six performance, load from a training service, presence, a competency the house is meant to teach, speed relative to the current seat holder, that being corrected twice is worse than once. What the candidate is owed: the rows in advance (they are public), a person beside them, a set-up station, never sole coverage, never a recovery or generosity decision, a paid stop if the service turns short, pay for the greater of hours worked or hours promised, and the rows named if declined. Cost accounting: a practical slows a first wave and costs a teaching body and an assessing body, so one per section per service, never on a period's first service, never on a service the leads' review flagged as short. Before opening, cohort practicals run in training services without load; the first four weeks of real dinner service become the real load read, and a hire who struggles then is first a training and staffing question. The same rows should serve a new hire's first-solo readiness read (3.3) so a candidate and a new hire are read against one list. A training day with an outside creator present turns a candidate's assessment into filmed content.

**Options for the shape (3.2.36).** (a) The scored block above. Commits Sŏn to staffing two extra bodies per practical and to a candidate note that states hours and pay. (b) A full paid shift. Commits Sŏn to a longer, costlier read that includes load but also fatigue, and to more of a candidate's time. (c) A short skills demonstration off the floor (a station set-up, a tray, a knife task) before any real service. Commits Sŏn to a read with no customer present and no people rows.

**Options for the assessor (3.2.37).** (a) A trained strand member who neither teaches nor decides. (b) The domain lead assesses and decides. (c) The person teaching also scores. Each commits Sŏn to a different separation between teaching, scoring, and deciding, and a different staffing cost.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. The unpaid tryout shift ("the stage" or "trail") is the restaurant industry's default and has drawn wage-and-hour scrutiny in several jurisdictions; the white paper's paid practical is a direct correction. Work samples are among the strongest predictors of job performance in the selection literature.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book's work sample is a written exercise (p. 196, pp. 248 to 250); Sŏn's is physical and in a room, which is why who assesses and what is owed matter more than in the book's version. Counsel's view on the paid practical's employment status belongs in 0.1.

## 6. Levels, and what hiring needs from them

**What the white paper holds.** Progression develops connections, not altitude; new people enter at the outside edge; every position has documented competency requirements; after a set period the next skill set unlocks and the practical assessment auto-schedules (WP p. 9, p. 11, p. 14). Role naming and compensation architecture are open items (p. 15). "Salaried" appears once, naming who gets a dinner in the room (p. 18), and is never defined. The compensation percentage is still being finalized (p. 20, p. 22).

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** Hiring needs three things from the structure regardless of whether levels exist: a documented entry bar per seat; a fixed, published unlock period per seat (before any advancement-path promise is made to a candidate); and an entry pay rule from 5.5, including what "salaried" means for the lead seats. Hiring does not need a placement decision if everyone enters a seat at the same point regardless of experience. The lead seats and the chef seat are hired into the center, not the edge, and the record has no word for what a lead enters at; that is 4.1.8. The book's caution that detailed ladders become checklists people game (p. 199) is met if the entry bar is validated by a scored practical at each unlock rather than self-reported. The book's idea of pay set by the role, moving only with the market or a role change (p. 201), is a shape 5.5 may want.

**Options.** None to decide here; 4.1.7, 4.1.8, 4.1.9, and 5.5 hold them. The hiring-side consequence to carry into those sessions: without an entry pay rule no offer can go out; without a published unlock period the advancement path on the transparency sheet is a promise the house cannot keep; and if 5.4's review later scores against something other than the reads used at hiring, a person is hired against one standard and reviewed against another.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book's sequence is select the candidate, set the level, set the pay (pp. 198 to 202). Sŏn's may be: the seat carries its pay, the candidate enters the seat. Compensation figures, bands, and ratios are not a source for this work.

## 7. References

**What the white paper holds.** Nothing. References are not mentioned.

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** The argument for splitting by seat class: for edge seats in this labor market, prior employers are often unreachable inside a short response window, frequently return only dates of employment, and the manager a candidate can name is not infrequently the reason they left; a candidate who left a bad room and cannot offer that manager as a reference would be penalized for exactly what the conduct standard protects against; and the practical is already a work sample, generally a stronger signal. For the lead seats, domain-owning seats, and the chef seat under the hire branch, calls were argued for: those seats have no practical equivalent, and a person about to hold a domain alone deserves outside input. References were argued as due diligence gathered by the decider between the first interview and the final round, not a stage; a note with no mark or verdict; never the sole basis of a decline; named consent for each person contacted; back-channel contact only with that consent. The book's seven questions (p. 208) adapted: keep the three-year outlook, the percentile question, the disagreement anecdote, the coaching anecdote, the one-to-ten-with-no-seven; reword "helpful to others" toward how the candidate treated people they had no authority over; add for lead candidates what they took on that was not theirs and what they refused to do. The book's rule that a lukewarm reference is disqualifying (p. 207) was tested: right when the candidate chose the reference and the reference is free to speak; wrong when a current employer must be vague to protect the candidate or themselves, and possibly inverted when the reference is someone the candidate correctly stood up to.

**Options for which seats (3.2.16).** (a) Lead, domain-owning, and chef seats only; the decider calls, both founders for the lead seats. (b) Every seat, one call for edge seats in the book's shape (p. 207). (c) No references at all, the practical and the loop carrying the weight. Each commits Sŏn to a different number of calls, a different response window, and a different answer to what an edge candidate with no callable manager is owed.

**Options for weighing (3.2.17).** (a) Evidence to weigh, never automatic; a decline must name a read from a stage the candidate experienced. (b) The book's rule: a lukewarm chosen reference ends it. (c) A lukewarm reference triggers one more call, then the decider decides. 

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Many employers now confirm dates and title only when called as a reference, which is why the book prefers questions that ask for a number. The book's percentile question is its own, and it works because people answer a data question more honestly than an open one (pp. 207 to 208).

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** Skipping references for edge seats is a divergence the book calls a mistake (p. 207); the case rests on the practical being a stronger and fairer signal in this market. It should be made out loud, not assumed.

## 8. The offer and the close

**What the white paper holds.** The candidate receives at the first interview a single sheet with the org chart, their direct lead, compensation and benefits, communication expectations, the training plan with pay dates, the compensation mechanics, and the advancement path; no surprises and nothing withheld (WP p. 18). The team is paid on a point system weighted by days worked, calculated daily (p. 20). The company runs on Rippling (p. 20). A candidate treated well at the no returns to the pool next year (p. 18). Silent on who extends the offer, negotiation, and how long an offer stands.

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** By the offer, the transparency sheet has already done most of the "nothing a surprise" work; the spoken offer is the sheet read again with a start date and a mentor added. A fixed order for the spoken offer. Who extends: the deciding domain lead, not a founder, on the reasoning that a founder extending an offer the lead decided places someone above the lead at the moment the candidate is deciding who they will work for; a partner extends for the lead and chef seats. The written offer generated from the packet and sheet, matching the spoken one, with terms counsel has set; acceptance creates the employee record and starts onboarding. The book's "sell chat" (p. 209) as a performed enthusiasm call was declined, with the sheet and the public build doing that work in writing and an unscored, candidate-requested conversation available during the window. A fixed response window, extendable once at the candidate's request, after which the seat reopens with a "we've moved on but you're in the pool" answer. No negotiation of pay within a seat, argued structurally: pay mechanics are public before a candidate meets a decider and identical for everyone in a seat; start date, shift shape, and accommodations move within what the sheet allows; no lead or partner may make a side deal for one person.

**Options for who extends (3.2.19).** (a) The deciding lead; a partner for the lead and chef seats. (b) A founder extends every offer before opening, then hands it to the leads. (c) Whoever the candidate met last. Each commits Sŏn to a different signal about who the candidate works for.

**Options for negotiation (3.2.20).** (a) Nothing moves on pay within a seat; date, shape, and accommodation may. Commits Sŏn to a public rule it must hold for the lead seats too, and to losing a candidate rather than bending it. (b) The lead seats negotiate, with someone other than the deciding partner holding the conversation, in the book's shape (pp. 216 onward). Commits Sŏn to two rules and to explaining why. (c) Negotiation within a published range for every seat. Commits Sŏn to the range being real and to the bias the book warns of (p. 200).

**Options for the window (3.2.21).** A short fixed window with one extension; an open window until the seat fills; a window that differs by seat class. The candidate who cleared the bar after the seat filled needs a written answer either way.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Publishing pay on the posting is now required by law in several U.S. states and cities, and houses that do it report fewer failed offers late in the process. The book's rule that the written offer never precedes the spoken one (p. 208) is close to universal among companies with a recruiting function.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book's compa-ratio and two offer points (pp. 199 to 200) presuppose bands; a seat-carries-its-pay rule has no offer points at all. Whether that holds for the lead seats is the real question, and it is Dominic's as much as Brandon's.

## 9. Leadership hiring: the two leads and the chef seat

**What the white paper holds.** Two founders own everything outside the kitchen; the chef partner owns the culinary vision as an equal founding partner, which makes the culinary seat founding rather than hired (WP p. 3, p. 5). The Operations Lead and the Maitre d are parallel, neither reporting to the other, co-leads of different systems serving the same center (p. 10). Leadership and salaried roles end with a dinner in the room (p. 18). The build is documented in public, including a chef search, as the most compelling material in the archive (p. 29). The parent company is not a brand and stays in the background (p. 36). Silent on the lead loop itself and on who decides when the founders split.

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** The book's leadership loop assumes five things Sŏn lacks before opening: an organization to bring along, a person being layered or displaced, a board member to help sell, a committee run as a feedback forum, and roughly six months of runway; only the last applies, and the calendar should honor it rather than force a date. Element by element: candidate confidentiality adopted in full, which sits in tension with a documented public build; "get two important people excited" becomes both founders independently scoring a stage, with "excited" itself declined in favor of evidence; "identify stakeholders" becomes the first-hired lead sitting on the second's loop; the feedback-collection forum becomes independent scorecards plus a dissent line; the celebratory announcement becomes a plain statement to the build audience that the seat is filled and by whom, only with the candidate's consent; the experience trap applied in full (p. 215: watch whether an experienced candidate asks about context before answering, always ask what they specifically did, always ask whether it worked). The lead-seat first interview swaps the strand-competency block for one that tests holding a domain with no one above or below, reading a problem as a systems question first, and saying what they would refuse to do. The chef seat's process was designed for both branches of 2.1.10 without choosing: under partnership, a negotiation with confidentiality, the experience-trap technique applied to how the candidate talks about cooking for this house, and consent-only due-diligence references; under hire, the lead loop nearly whole, with a cooking practical scored on founder-written rows and a reference note both founders call. Either way the module on judging depth of culinary knowledge has to exist first, since the same two people read under either branch. Later lead hires: once the pool could hold someone, an outside lead hire needs a written reason; whether a domain's own team members ever sit on a later lead loop was filed open.

**Options for the founders' disagreement (3.2.11).** (a) The founder whose domain the seat serves decides and writes why, the other's scorecard preserved as dissent. (b) Both must agree; a split means more homework (another reference, an outside reader), then the 2.1.13 deadlock rule. (c) The 2.1.13 rule applies directly. Each commits the founders to a different answer to the book's claim that one accountable person should decide a leadership hire (pp. 210 to 211).

**Options for the lead loop (3.2.23).** (a) Both founders interview every lead candidate; the first lead hired sits on the second's loop; the dinner is the final stage; the build audience hears a plain statement after acceptance, with consent. (b) The book's loop with stand-ins: an outside adviser as the stakeholder voice and a feedback meeting between the founders. (c) The same loop as any domain-owning seat plus the dinner, nothing bespoke. Each commits Sŏn to a different amount of confidentiality against the public build, and a different number of hours.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Executive searches commonly run under strict confidentiality with the candidate's current employer never contacted; the book's advice to ask the candidate for a discreet reference at their current company (pp. 216 onward) is the common workaround. Restaurant groups that document their build publicly, the white paper's own grocer example among them (WP p. 29), tend to announce a hire only after it is signed.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book's leadership decider is one person (p. 210); Sŏn has two equal founders and a third seat whose standing is undecided. The book's change-management steps assume an organization; before opening there is none, so most of them fall away and return for later lead hires after opening.

## 10. Hiring internally

**What the white paper holds.** After a set period the next skill set unlocks; the practical assessment auto-schedules with the domain lead; a ready person with no open seat enters a ready-now pool; the pool surfaces who is furthest along when a person leaves (WP p. 11, p. 14). When ready, a person schedules a competency conversation with the appropriate lead and is signed off (p. 19). The goal is internal succession, to the point where the building rarely hires a manager from outside (p. 19). Internal promotion rate is a tracked measure (p. 23). The white paper's two accounts of what triggers advancement (the system alone, or a lead's sign-off) do not fully agree; that is 4.1.11.

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** For exactly one qualified pool candidate the book's sham-process concern (pp. 213 to 214) does not arise, because pool membership was earned by a real, failable assessment and the seat's own practical is the test; the other reads are already documented from months in role, and only the building-side question is asked again. For several candidates, everyone on the track takes the same practical on identical rows, scored by an assessor who does not decide. For a lead seat filled from inside, the lead block and the dinner still run (holding a domain with no one above has not been shown by holding a lower seat, and the dinner reads the person as a customer), while references are skipped because the employee record serves. Meeting outside candidates to show rigor (p. 214) was declined as itself a kind of sham: running people through stages the house does not intend to decide on breaks the no-ghosting rule worse than skipping the gesture. Those not chosen get the rows in writing the same day, pool standing kept with a next assessment date, and nothing on their record beyond that. Visibility: each person sees their own pool status; leads and the training owner see their own tracks; no one else sees who is in a pool; an internal candidate's practical is scheduled without naming the seat on any public schedule; an announcement names only the new seat holder.

**Options (3.2.44).** (a) Pool first, practical as the test, no outside candidates, feedback by rows. (b) The book's lightweight loop: a couple of leads interview each internal candidate, then a discussion, with a few outside candidates met for calibration. (c) The pool is read but the seat is always posted, inside and out, and everyone runs the same loop. Each commits Sŏn to a different answer to whether an internal candidate's months in role count as their interview.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies that publish a promotion-from-within rate as a measure, as the white paper does (WP p. 23), generally pair it with a rule that open roles are visible internally before they are posted outside.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book's internal process is a lighter interview loop (pp. 214 to 215); Sŏn's may be an assessment with no interview at all for non-lead seats, on the ground that the record already holds what an interview would ask.

## 11. Calibration, bias control, and the outside check

**What the white paper holds.** Leading indicators are read before the damage compounds (WP p. 12, p. 22). Blame the process, fix the process; post-mortems are private and process-focused (p. 17). The house reads its customers' demographic composition at the door (p. 21, p. 27) and states no read of its team's composition; whether the house says anything about who it hopes its team will be is 4.7's question. Silent on hiring calibration.

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** A monthly calibration read of every completed packet by one named person (a substitute before opening), scanning for presence language, unasked questions, misfiled evidence, a to-be-developed competency scored as absent, an off-description decline reason, per-interviewer leniency or strictness as a pattern (never a number), the personality-drift check (whether behind-the-scenes seats decline at a different rate than customer-facing ones), hires against a "does not meet", declines contradicting every scorecard, and any unevidenced flag; producing an anonymized record and per-interviewer notes; refusing a candidate's name, a reopened decision, a numeric target, a meeting, or a verdict on an interviewer. It was argued to run at least three consecutive months on real packets before dinner's gate. The book's method of reading interviewers against outcomes (p. 197) needs volume and years; a ninety-day read of each hire's packet against their on-the-job record, one anonymized line per interviewer per hire, is the leading-indicator substitute, treating a hire who struggles under load first as a question about the practical and training design. Any first-year departure or seat change re-triggers that read. Both of those outcome-side reads are 3.4's ("marrying hiring data to performance data"); 3.2 decides whether the record exists for 3.4 to read. The gap: once the founder stage ends for entry-level seats, no outside-domain read remains on those packets except the monthly, after-the-fact pattern. Three options: keep the sunset and rely on the assessor's separation, the scored practical, the calibration read, and the hire-against-a-flag trigger; keep the founder stage indefinitely, which keeps a founder in every hire in a house built to run without them; make the adjacent-domain interviewer standard for every seat, at a trained person's hours per packet. The earlier work's "no record sorts a person into a performance situation" rule and the recurrence thresholds for a repeating domain belong to 5.7, 5.8, and 4.2, not here.

**Options for the read (3.2.41).** (a) The monthly read above, one owner, anonymized. (b) Findings read at the leads' review (2.3.13) with no separate owner. (c) No standing read; the applicant system's refusals and the dissent line are the only checks. Each commits Sŏn to a different number of hours a month and a different chance of seeing a slow drift.

**Options for the outside check (3.2.42).** As listed: periodic only; founder stage kept; adjacent-domain interviewer on every packet.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies that run structured hiring at scale commonly hold a periodic calibration where interviewers read the same feedback and compare marks; the book describes Stripe's version (pp. 250 to 252). At Sŏn's volume the same function has to be done on the packets that exist, not on a statistically meaningful sample.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book's bias control is measured against self-identification data and significance (p. 205). Sŏn's volume cannot claim significance, and whether the house collects self-identification data at all is 4.7's decision; until then any bias read is an honest, untargeted pattern read on stage and source.

## 12. The hiring calendar and readiness

**What the white paper holds.** Dinner opens first and runs alone for at least a quarter (WP p. 36). Development starts immediately after the decision (p. 18). The chef search is part of the documented build (p. 29). The compensation percentage is still being finalized (p. 22).

**From the earlier work, worth keeping.** The calendar runs on gates, not dates. The chef seat's settlement gates the whole kitchen chain: menu, kitchen seat descriptions, kitchen practicals, kitchen hires, training, dinner's gate. Both leads must be in the building long enough before dinner's gate for the leads' review to have the consecutive weeks of records 2.3.13 requires. The compensation architecture is on the critical path because the transparency sheet with pay mechanics is handed at the first interview; the leads' first interviews happen months before 5.5 would naturally run, so either 5.5 is pulled ahead or the leads' offers wait. That is 3.2.18, and it is a founder decision, not a scheduling detail. Refusals: no practical scheduled to hit a start date; no first stage before the runner's training is recorded; no offer while pay mechanics read "being finalized"; no kitchen practical before the menu exists; no gate opened around a failed readiness row. Readiness rows this chunk adds to 2.2.39 are listed in 3.2.47; each row that depends on an open decision reads as failed rather than waived.

**Options for the compensation order (3.2.18).** (a) 5.5 finishes before any lead candidate's first interview. Commits the founders to doing compensation design first, before the seats it prices are described. (b) The leads interview against a sheet marked "being finalized" and no offer goes out until it is filled. Commits Sŏn to a lead candidate who has invested weeks without a pay answer, and to the risk of losing them at the offer. (c) An interim answer for the two lead seats only, which 5.5 later generalizes or revises. Commits Sŏn to a promise to two people that the later architecture must honor.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book's six-month leadership search (p. 210) is a duration; Sŏn's is a chain of gates whose first link (the chef seat) is not a hire at all under one branch of 2.1.10.
