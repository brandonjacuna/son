# 5.7 The steady middle: considerations

Context for the working session. The book gives this topic one page (p. 431). White paper cited as WP p. N. Prior work argued that a restaurant should give it the most attention of the three performance chunks (86ajgmmpg); that is presented here as an option and a reason, not a rule.

## 1. What the white paper holds, and where it is silent

The white paper's argument for its own kitchen technique is a consistency argument: manual preparation decays as volume rises, "the tenth cup is not the first," and the method's value is to "raise the floor and hold it" (WP p. 4). Its organizational design says the same: "no hierarchy of importance" (WP p. 20); every position matters "because the sequence is the mechanism" (WP p. 8); distributed anchors beat concentrated ones (WP p. 9). Progression "develops connections, not altitude," and "a person who has been in the system longer does not move toward the center" (WP p. 11). Pay is a point system weighted by days worked (WP p. 20). Peer recognition lands differently because it comes from the people who understand the work (WP p. 19). The mentor is chosen as a cultural steward, not the strongest performer (WP p. 18). Retention and internal promotion rate are evidence the development commitments are real (WP p. 23).

Silent on: any word for the person who stays at a level by choice; whether an unlock expires; whether anyone asks why it was not taken; how hours are allocated when there are not enough; what tenure earns.

One tension the white paper does not resolve, worth naming to Brandon: the advancement design gives tenure zero weight (WP p. 11), and the belonging design depends entirely on the same server being there visit after visit (WP p. 25: "their table, their server, their order"). The person who holds a seat for years is the one who produces the customer recognition the white paper calls a leading indicator (WP p. 22). The design values that person's steadiness and gives it no name.

## 2. The word, and whether it is an outcome (5.7.2)

The book calls them medium performers and says they are a real category, often the culture carriers (p. 431). Prior work refused any tier on a person and proposed reading three situations off existing records instead: a person moving past a level, a person holding a level, a seat whose level is in question, with no field or list sorting people into them (17tn048qfmq). 5.8.10 decides whether any record sorts a person. This decision is about the word and whether the house says out loud that holding a seat well is an outcome it wants.

Options:
- (a) No word; the record shows the level held and since when, and that is all. Commits Sŏn to a person reading their own steadiness off a date, and to leads with no vocabulary for it.
- (b) The house names it in the positive (holding a seat) and says in the principles or the team home that it is a valued outcome. Commits Sŏn to meaning it when the promotion rate is read, and to a word that can still become a euphemism.
- (c) The book's "medium performer," used by leads in a portfolio view. Commits Sŏn to a rating in all but name, which 5.8.10 may refuse.

Why weight it: prior work's reason was that a full night is carried by people simply holding their stations, the white paper's design has no load-bearing role (WP p. 8), and the tight-labor-market argument (WP p. 16) is about the ordinary worker, not the top of the market. Whether Brandon agrees the book's proportions are wrong for a restaurant is his to say.

## 3. The unlock that is never taken (5.7.3)

The book: it should be acceptable to reach a terminal level; allow the stasis, seek the person's feedback, celebrate consistency (p. 431). The white paper: the next skill set unlocks automatically after a set period and is self-paced (WP p. 11). Nothing says the unlock closes or that anyone asks.

Considerations:
- If nobody ever asks, the puller who is too busy to advance and the person who is content look the same on the record. The career conversation (4.4.3) is a natural place for the question, once, in the person's words.
- If the house asks often, the question becomes pressure, and the internal promotion rate (5.7.8) becomes the reason for asking.
- A "terminal" marker the person chooses (an opt-out) protects them from being asked and tells the pool ordering to skip them. It is also a label on a person, which prior work refused (17tn048qfmw).
- 2.2.14's development goals: a goal may be depth in the current level, not the next one. Whether that is stated matters, because a goal form that only points upward tells the steady person they have none.

Options:
- (a) The unlock stays open indefinitely; nobody asks; the record is the only record. (b) The unlock stays open; the career conversation asks once and writes the person's answer in their words. (c) A stated opt-out marker the person may set and clear.

## 4. Tenure (5.7.4)

The book's recognition for consistency is verbal and assignments, not compensation (p. 431). The white paper's pay already weights days worked (WP p. 20), so tenure earns pay by arithmetic without a tenure rule. The open question is whether tenure appears anywhere else. Prior work held that tenure in a seat has zero weight in advancement and that the pool is never ordered by it (digest).

Options:
- (a) Nowhere but pay; the customer recognition rate is the house's measure of what tenure produces, never a person's. (b) On the seat row as a fact (level held since), visible to the person and lead. (c) The server's book of regulars is named as what tenure earns: it is theirs, and follows them (WP p. 25).

What tenure never does, whichever option: order the pool, the schedule, or a designation. 5.5 decides anything pay-side, including whether the days-worked weighting is the whole of it.

## 5. Recognition without a rating (5.7.5)

The book: verbal recognition and special assignments (p. 431). Prior work argued recognition for this group should run through peer recognition, the person's own record read back at reviews, the pay arithmetic, and customer recognition metrics, never a lead's individually chosen assignment, because a lead's favor is what a rating is made of (17tn048qfmw).

Considerations:
- The mentor pool is the white paper's own answer: the mentor is "a proven cultural steward rather than simply the strongest performer" (WP p. 18). That is the steady middle described as a qualification. Whether 3.3.13's mentor rule says so is a question for this decision.
- The assessor pool (4.1.14) is another: the person who has held a seat well is a credible reader of whether someone else is ready.
- "Special assignment" at Sŏn is the per-service designation, which 4.1.19 gives a rotation floor. Whether anything exists outside the rotation, and who allocates it, is the book's practice against the white paper's design.
- A lead saying "well done" at a check-in is not a rating. A lead saying it in the brief is public praise, which 5.3.7 governs.

## 6. Seat rhythm (5.7.6)

The book: a team that delivers similar work consistently well on a predictable cycle is often better suited to medium performers (p. 431). At Sŏn the service periods differ in rhythm (the early morning is grab-and-go, dinner is the full room, late night is a quieter register, WP pp. 5, 33) and so do the strands and stations. The risk is the book's frame becoming a ranking: "the morning is where the medium people go." The white paper's rule that neither strand is primary (WP p. 10) and that no hierarchy of importance exists (WP p. 20) is the guard.

Options:
- (a) The seat row carries a rhythm line describing the work (predictable, variable, mixed), so a person choosing a seat or period can choose steadiness; the line never describes people. (b) No line; rhythm is learned by working it. 

3.3.29's shorter plan is how a person moves toward a steadier seat or period. 4.1.16's seat row kit is where the line would live.

## 7. Protections and hours (5.7.7)

The book says only that medium performers are easy to neglect (p. 431). Prior work turned that into a list of what the house owes them: full pay weight for the seat held, workload protection ahead of anyone chasing something, a predictable schedule, the same check-in cadence, mentoring access, rotation-based designations rather than favoritism (17tn048qfmw). It also held that whose hours are reduced when the schedule shrinks follows a published rule (stated preference, then rotation by eligibility, skill level as a scheduling fact only), never a lead's choice and never a performance signal, and that a reduction is a scheduling change, not a departure until a defined final step (17tn048qg1q). The departure boundary is 5.10's; the published rule is this chunk's because the steady person is the one it protects.

Considerations:
- Hours are pay at Sŏn (WP p. 20), so an unpublished hours rule is an unpublished pay lever in a lead's hands.
- The person moving (in the pool, authoring modules, holding designations) is the one a lead is most tempted to schedule on the best shifts. The rotation floor (4.1.19) covers designations; the schedule itself needs its own rule.
- Seasonal reductions announced in advance are what the industry rarely does and what the white paper's "respect for time" (WP p. 17) implies.

Options for the reduction order: (a) stated preference first, then rotation by eligibility. (b) seniority in the seat (the union pattern). (c) the lead decides, with a written reason. Each commits to something: (a) to a published mechanism a person can read; (b) to tenure meaning something 5.7.4 may have refused; (c) to the discretion the white paper designs out.

## 8. The promotion rate as pressure (5.7.8)

The white paper reads internal promotion rate as evidence (WP p. 23). Prior work flagged that if it becomes a target, a lead will push unlocks onto people who never asked, undermining the idea that holding a seat by choice is a legitimate outcome (digest). 2.2.18 decides whether a target is set before a baseline; 2.2.21 and 2.2.25 hold the metric definitions.

Options:
- (a) Define the rate as seats filled from within over seats filled; a person who never enters the pool does not lower it. (b) Define it as people advanced over people who entered the pool; same effect, different denominator. (c) The book's implicit reading (advanced over everyone), with a chose-to-hold count beside it so the two are read together.

Whichever definition, the question is whether the rate is ever a target a lead is read on. General practice, not Sŏn-specific: any promotion-rate target tends to produce promotions.

## 9. Counsel

Hours reduction and seasonal layoffs touch employment law (notice, whether a reduction to zero is a termination, unemployment eligibility). 5.7.13 is reviewed with 5.10's counsel work. Nothing else here needs counsel.

## 10. Where Sŏn diverges from the book

- Weight: the book's one page becomes, if Brandon agrees, the chunk with the most protections, because consistency is the product.
- No terminal level as a designation, if 5.7.3 keeps the unlock open; the book's "allow the stasis" becomes "never close the door and never push through it."
- Recognition runs through peers, the record, the pools, and pay, not a lead's chosen assignment, if 5.7.5 follows the white paper's own mentor logic.
- Tenure earns pay by arithmetic and a book of regulars by relationship, and nothing by rank.
