# 5.7 The steady middle: working session

## 1. Orientation

This chunk covers the people who hold a seat well and do not move: what the house calls them, what it owes them, how it recognizes them without rating them, and how it keeps its own advancement machinery from pushing them. The book gives it one page (p. 431), between high performers (5.6) and low performers (5.8). Prior work argued a restaurant should give it the most attention of the three, because a full service is carried by people holding their stations. Whether Brandon agrees is the first thing to find out.

Upstream, already decided or in progress: whether Sŏn has ladders (4.1.7), the rotation floor on designations (4.1.19), how development goals are set (2.2.14), the check-in cadence (2.3.10), peer recognition (4.8.10), the mentor rule (3.3.13), the pools on the seat inventory (4.1.14), the metric register (2.2.25), compensation (5.5, which 5.7.4 waits on), and the departure boundary for an hours reduction (5.10, which 5.7.7 and 5.7.13 wait on). 5.8.10 decides whether any record sorts a person into a performance situation; 5.7.2 should be read with it.

Who else must agree: both seated founders on every decision; the chef partner on the kitchen's seat rows (5.7.6) and pools (5.7.5).

## 3. Briefs

### 5.7.2 Decide what the house calls holding a seat, and whether it is named as an outcome

**The question:** is there a word at Sŏn for the person who holds a seat well and stays, and does the house say out loud that it wants them?

**Why it matters now:** the principles (2.1.5) and the team home are being written. If holding a seat is an outcome the house values, it needs to be in them before the first hire reads them. If it is not named, the advancement design is the only story a new person hears.

**Openers:**
- Name the person on a floor you ran who had been on the same station for six years and was the reason the night worked. What did the org chart call them? What did the regulars call them?
- Sideways: the white paper's matcha argument is "the tenth cup is not the first," and the method's whole value is holding the floor (WP p. 4). Say that about a person instead of a cup.
- The book gives this one page in thirty. Is that the book's tech-company context, or is it right?
- If a candidate asks "what if I just want to be a great server here for ten years?", what is the answer on the sheet?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Is there a word? "Steady," "holding," "medium," none?
- Is holding a seat named as a valued outcome in the principles or the team home, or left implicit?
- Whatever it is called, is it a dated fact of a seat and a person (level held, since when) rather than a category on the person? 5.8.10 decides the record rule; this decides the language.

**What the book says:** given enough time every company shows the full curve; medium performers are a real category, easy to neglect, often the culture carriers; treat the team as a portfolio where each person is supported, coached, or moved (p. 431).

**White-paper default:** no hierarchy of importance (WP p. 20); tenure adds threads, not altitude (WP p. 11); every position matters because the sequence is the mechanism (WP p. 8). No word for the person who stays by choice.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Long-tenured servers are the visible asset in the rooms that have them (classic dining rooms in New York and Paris are the usual examples), and those rooms rarely have a word for it beyond the person's name. The absence of a word has not stopped the practice; it has stopped the practice from being designed.

**Options:** (a) no word; the record shows the level and the date. (b) named in the positive as an outcome the house wants. (c) the book's "medium performer" in a lead's portfolio view. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** a positive word that becomes a euphemism within a year; a "no word" answer that leaves the advancement story as the only one.

**A finished answer:** the word or the decision to have none; where it appears; the sentence that says whether the house wants this outcome; and a note that the record rule is 5.8.10's.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.7.3 Decide whether the next unlock ever closes, and whether anyone asks why a person has not taken it

**The question:** the next skill set unlocks after a set period. If a person never takes it, does the door close, and does anyone ask why?

**Why it matters now:** the learning platform's configuration and the career conversation guide (4.4.13) are being built. Both need to know whether an untaken unlock is a state that expires, a state someone asks about, or just a state.

**Openers:**
- Someone has had the next unlock available for eighteen months. They are excellent at their seat. Nobody has said a word. Is that the design working or failing?
- Sideways: the puller who is too busy to advance and the person who is content look the same on the record. How would you tell them apart without asking?
- What does it feel like to be asked "why haven't you moved up?" for the third time?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Does the unlock stay open indefinitely or close after a period?
- Is there a terminal designation: none, the person's stated choice in their words at the career conversation, or an opt-out marker on the seat row?
- Who asks, how often, and where does the answer go?
- May a development goal (2.2.14) be depth in the current level rather than the next one, and does the goal form say so?

**What the book says:** it should be acceptable to reach a terminal level; allow the stasis, provided the person contributes well; seek their feedback; celebrate consistency (p. 431).

**White-paper default:** the next skill set unlocks automatically after a set period; it is self-paced (WP p. 11). Silent on expiry and on asking.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Professional firms with an "up or out" rule are the counterexample: they close the door on purpose. Firms that dropped the rule generally added a stated senior non-partner track so the choice to hold had a name.

**Options:** (a) open indefinitely, nobody asks. (b) open indefinitely; the career conversation asks once and writes the answer in the person's words. (c) an opt-out marker the person may set and clear. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** an asking cadence that turns into pressure once 5.7.8's rate is read; an opt-out marker that becomes a label.

**A finished answer:** open or closes, the designation or none, who asks and when, and whether the goal form points down as well as up.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.7.4 Decide whether tenure in a seat counts for anything beyond pay's days worked

**The question:** the white paper says tenure does not move a person toward the center, and also that the regular has "their server." Does time in a seat earn anything Sŏn names?

**Why it matters now:** 5.5 is deciding pay; the customer recognition rate is being defined (2.2.19); and the pool ordering (4.1.11) needs to know whether tenure is ever a tiebreaker. Left unsaid, tenure becomes a quiet criterion.

**Openers:**
- A server in year four has forty regulars who ask for them by name. A server in year one just passed the same assessment. Same seat, same level. What is different, and does Sŏn say so?
- Sideways: the white paper gives the regulars to the server, not the house: "if a server moves to another property in the group, their regulars may follow" (WP p. 25). That is tenure earning something. What is it called?
- Would you put "since 2027" on a seat row?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Does tenure appear anywhere but the pay arithmetic: nowhere, on the seat row as a fact, in the recognition rate as the house's measure?
- Is the book of regulars named as what tenure earns?
- Confirm: tenure never orders the pool, the schedule, or a designation.

**What the book says:** celebrate consistent performance; recognition here looks less like compensation and more like verbal recognition and special assignments (p. 431).

**White-paper default:** tenure adds threads, not altitude (WP p. 11); pay weighted by days worked (WP p. 20); the fifth visit depends on the same server (WP p. 25). The two are not reconciled.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Union houses order shifts by seniority and say so; most independent rooms let seniority order sections informally and never say so. Both are tenure rules; only one is published.

**Options:** (a) nowhere but pay. (b) on the seat row as a fact. (c) the book of regulars named as tenure's earning. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** a "nowhere" answer while the schedule quietly favors seniority; anything pay-side, which is 5.5's.

**A finished answer:** where tenure appears and where it never does, and the sentence about regulars.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders, with 5.5 complete.

### 5.7.5 Decide how a person holding steady is recognized, and whether the mentor and assessor pools draw from them

**The question:** without a rating, a raise outside the arithmetic, or a lead's favorite assignment, how does the house tell a steady person they matter?

**Why it matters now:** the mentor rule (3.3.13), the pools on the seat inventory (4.1.14), and the recognition platform's page (5.3.14) are being written. The white paper's own mentor sentence describes this group as a qualification; whether the rules say so is decided now.

**Openers:**
- The white paper picks the mentor as "a proven cultural steward rather than simply the strongest performer" (WP p. 18). Who is that, on a floor you have run?
- Sideways: the book's recognition is "special assignments." At Sŏn the special assignment is the designation, and it rotates. Is there anything left for a lead to give?
- When a peer recognizes a steady person on the platform, and when a lead does at a check-in, which one lands?

**Narrowing questions:**
- What may a lead do: say it at the check-in, say it in the brief under 4.5.13's slot, nothing a peer could not do?
- Do peer recognition, the record read back at the review, and the pay arithmetic carry the rest?
- Does a special assignment exist outside the rotation floor? Allocated how?
- Do the mentor and assessor pools name steadiness in a seat as a qualification?

**What the book says:** recognize contributions; verbal recognition and special assignments; seek their feedback on how to improve the team (p. 431).

**White-paper default:** mentor as cultural steward (WP p. 18); peer recognition lands differently (WP p. 19); paid module authorship is open to any qualified team member (WP p. 19).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Kitchens that assign training of new cooks to the most consistent line cook rather than the most ambitious one report steadier onboarding; it is the white paper's mentor logic in practice.

**Options:** (a) recognition runs through peers, the record, the pools, and pay; a lead says it privately and nothing more. (b) the book's practice: the lead may give an assignment outside the rotation. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** a "special assignment" that is a designation by another name; pools that name steadiness and then fill from the ambitious anyway.

**A finished answer:** the lead's list, the rest named, yes or no on assignments outside the rotation, and the pool rule.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen's pools.

### 5.7.6 Decide whether a seat's rhythm is stated so people can choose steadiness

**The question:** does each seat row say what kind of work it is (predictable cycle, variable scope), so a person can choose for steadiness without anyone choosing for them?

**Why it matters now:** the seat inventory (4.1.15) and its kit (4.1.16) are being built. A rhythm line is trivial to add now and awkward later. The risk is the book's frame becoming "the morning is where the medium people go."

**Openers:**
- Describe the early morning grab-and-go seat and the Saturday dinner floor in one line each, as work, without a person in the sentence.
- Sideways: the book says predictable-cycle teams suit medium performers (p. 431). Read that sentence to the morning team. How does it land?
- Has anyone you have worked with chosen a quieter station on purpose and been read as giving up?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Does the seat row carry a rhythm line?
- Does the line describe the work only, never the people suited to it?
- How does a person move toward a steadier seat or period: 3.3.29's shorter plan?

**What the book says:** match the person to the kind of team; the predictable-cycle team is often better suited to medium performers, who still get development plans and recognition (p. 431).

**White-paper default:** the periods differ in rhythm (WP pp. 5, 33); neither strand is primary (WP p. 10); no hierarchy of importance (WP p. 20).

**How others have handled it:** Nothing useful beyond the book.

**Options:** (a) a rhythm line on the row, work only. (b) no line. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** a line that ranks; a line that becomes a reason to schedule someone somewhere.

**A finished answer:** yes or no, the wording rule, and the move path.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen's rows.

### 5.7.7 Decide the protections a person holding steady has: schedule, load, and whose hours are reduced

**The question:** when the house's attention and the best shifts go to the people moving, what does the person holding a seat get by rule, and when hours shrink, whose go first?

**Why it matters now:** hours are pay at Sŏn (WP p. 20), so an unpublished hours rule is an unpublished pay lever. The scheduling surface is being configured and the first seasonal dip will come in the first year. 5.10 sets where a reduction becomes a departure; this decides everything before that line.

**Openers:**
- January. Covers are down a third. You have to cut hours across the floor. Who loses them, and how did they find out?
- Sideways: the person in the pool, authoring modules and holding the room designation, is the one every lead wants on Saturday. The person who has held the same station for three years is the one who made Saturday work. Who gets Saturday?
- What did you watch happen to steady people in rooms where the manager built the schedule by feel?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Same check-in cadence, same rotation access, no worse a schedule than a person moving: confirm each.
- Whose hours are reduced: stated preference first, then rotation by eligibility, skill level as a scheduling fact only? Seniority? The lead with a written reason?
- Published in advance, with how much notice? Seasonal patterns announced when?
- A reduction is a scheduling change, not a departure, up to 5.10's boundary: confirm.
- Never a lead's read of a person; never a performance signal: confirm.

**What the book says:** medium performers are easy to neglect (p. 431).

**White-paper default:** cooling failure (WP p. 13); burnout is a system failure (WP p. 17); hours are pay (WP p. 20). Silent on allocation.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Union houses reduce by seniority, published. Most independents reduce by the manager's read, unpublished, and it is the most common grievance in the trade. Some houses ask for volunteers first, then rotate.

**Options:** (a) preference, then rotation by eligibility. (b) seniority in the seat. (c) the lead, with a written reason. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** a rule that reads "skill level" as a merit order rather than an eligibility fact; a reduction used as a quiet pushout, which 5.10 guards.

**A finished answer:** the three confirmations, the reduction order, the notice, and the boundary sentence pointing to 5.10.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.7.8 Decide how the internal promotion rate is read so it never becomes pressure to advance

**The question:** the white paper reads internal promotion rate as evidence the commitments are real. How is it defined so that a person who chooses to hold does not lower it, and so no lead is ever read on it?

**Why it matters now:** 2.2.25's register is being written. A rate defined as "advanced over everyone" makes every steady person a drag on a number someone will want to move.

**Openers:**
- Dominic puts internal promotion rate on the house review. It is flat for two quarters. What is the first thing a lead does?
- Sideways: any promotion-rate target tends to produce promotions. Is that the outcome the white paper meant by "evidence the commitments are real" (WP p. 23)?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Definition: seats filled from within over seats filled? People advanced over people who entered the pool? Advanced over everyone with a chose-to-hold count beside it?
- Is the rate ever a target a lead is read on?

**What the book says:** allow the stasis (p. 431).

**White-paper default:** retention and internal promotion rate are direct evidence (WP p. 23); 2.2.18 governs targets before baselines.

**How others have handled it:** Covered above; nothing further.

**Options:** (a) seats from within over seats filled. (b) advanced over pool entrants. (c) the naive rate with a companion count. Depth: cons. 8.

**Watch for:** a definition nobody outside the room can recompute.

**A finished answer:** the definition in one line, the companion count or none, and the rule on targets.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
