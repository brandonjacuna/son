# 5.8 Managing low performers: considerations

Context for the working session. Topics follow the book's order (pp. 432 to 448; templates pp. 474 to 482). White paper cited as WP p. N. The prior extraction built a full mechanism here (a "review window") and a long list of refusals; both appear as options with their reasoning. Counsel's answers (5.8.2) are a prerequisite for several decisions and are not guessed at here.

## 1. What the white paper holds, and where it is silent

The white paper's rule for any operational problem is to run the failure-mode reference "before intervening at the people level" (WP p. 14). Its name for an underperforming seat is tension slack: "a role is occupied but the person is disengaged, the thread present but not carrying load, and other nodes are compensating without visibility. The fix is not replacing the person. It is diagnosing which adjacent nodes are overloaded and re-establishing tension in that domain" (WP p. 14). Its first question is "not who failed. It is what allowed this to happen"; management absorbs blame; post-mortems are private and process-focused (WP p. 17). Burnout is a system failure (WP p. 17). No surprises governs reviews: "if something is wrong, the person knows before the review" (WP p. 19). The ninety-day plan has milestones, assessments, and check-ins (WP p. 18). Competency is validated by a practical assessment and "no one touches a table until they are actually ready" (WP p. 19). The interview asks "whether the building is equipped to create the environment this person needs to succeed" (WP p. 18). The back-office administrator is removed: "cash, payroll, HR, compliance" run in software (WP p. 22). The only "at fault" language in the document is about customers, who "should never feel at fault, even when they are" (WP p. 26).

Silent on: what happens when the process has been fixed and the person still is not holding; any improvement plan; timelines for a person; attendance; who holds employment records; hardship; a demotion or a step back; who decides a separation.

The white paper's frame is an argument that most low performance is design failure. It is not an argument that none is. Where individual accountability begins is a question it leaves open, and this chunk is where Brandon answers it.

## 2. Who holds the HR function (5.8.3)

The book assumes an HR partner who is told, who flags legal risk, and who does not run the process (pp. 433 to 434). The white paper removes the administrator and names no human custodian of employment records. Prior work flagged this as a gap and argued against creating any HR-titled, floor-manager, or assistant-manager seat to fill it, splitting the load across the floor lead's check-ins, general operations recordkeeping, and outside counsel (17tn048qfp1, digest).

Options:
- (a) No HR seat. The Operations Lead is the record custodian (compliance is already theirs, WP p. 10); the other lead or the partner who holds the lead's monthly conversation is the second reader; counsel is called by rule at defined points. Commits Sŏn to two leads who each carry a piece of a function neither was hired for, and to counsel's bill as the safety net.
- (b) An outside HR advisory retained from the start (the payroll platform the white paper names offers such services; whether Sŏn's plan includes them is a fact to check, not assumed). Commits Sŏn to a third party in the loop who has "seen many similar situations," which is what the book values HR for.
- (c) An HR seat at a headcount threshold. Commits Sŏn to the seat the white paper's leadership count (WP p. 22) does not include.

Counsel's answer on who may lawfully hold employment records (5.8.2) shapes this.

## 3. What "not holding" means, and the order of reads (5.8.4)

The book: a persistent gap between performance and the role's demands, diagnosed as skill, will, or wrong job (p. 432), with two or three explicit conversations before any formal step (pp. 434 to 435). Prior work built a required order before any conversation touches the individual: capture the incident as a process, classify the failure type, look at the pattern across the seat and shift, make and verify domain fixes, check whether the seat's definition is realistic, check what the house supplied against what the person said they needed, then hear the person, and only after all that form a tentative view (17tn048qfn0). It read the book's "consistently late" example as a case where the person's account comes before any strong response.

Considerations:
- The order is the white paper's failure-mode reference (WP p. 14) made procedural. Its cost is time: a lead who sees a real gap must run six steps before saying so, which collides with the book's "move deliberately" (p. 437) and with the white paper's own "if something is wrong, the person knows before the review" (WP p. 19).
- 4.2.2 already decides the order a diagnosis follows and 4.2.10 the recurrence counts. This decision applies them to one seat and one person. 5.2.9 decides the failure types.
- The objective source. The book's "role charter" is Sŏn's seat row (4.1.15) and readiness rows (3.3.9). A gap measured against those is a fact; a gap measured against a lead's expectation is an opinion.
- Zero-target misses (safety, an unrecognized regular, a ghosted candidate, an open feedback item): prior work read each as a departmental process failure first, escalating to a person only on the same recurrence path as anything else (86akh5uxm). The book's exception for a single egregious act (p. 435) is 5.2.2's, not this chunk's.

Options:
- (a) The full order every time, as prior work built it. (b) The book's: pattern, hypothesis, conversation, with the failure-mode reference run once by the lead at the first repeat. (c) The order runs in full past a recurrence count and in short before it.

## 4. Triggers and who opens (5.8.5)

The book's trigger is the manager's judgment that feedback has become a pattern (p. 432). Prior work built five triggers read from records, opened by the record reaching a count with no one's request, and a long list of what may never open or be cited: a rescheduled check-in (86akh67yy), a recognition tally or hiring scorecard (17tn048qc2e), a customer rating, a designation count, a mentor's or peer's account, a formal review, a lead's or founder's request (17tn048qfnk, 17tn048qcjt). 5.1.9 already hands the first trigger here (a repeated hypothesis with records unchanged after a process fix); 3.3.12 hands the fourth (an unmet ninety-day goal after one extension).

Considerations:
- Opening by record removes the lead's discretion in both directions: a lead cannot open early on a hunch and cannot decline to open when the count is reached. The second half protects the person the lead likes; the first protects the person the lead does not.
- Opening by the lead on the record keeps the count as evidence and the lead as the actor, which is closer to the book and to the white paper's "authority lives in the domain" (WP p. 7 to 8).
- The tension-slack statement ("this seat's duties are being absorbed by coworkers") is the white paper's own diagnostic (WP p. 14). Whether a coworker's report of it counts, given 5.1.3's rule on sources, is a real question. The digest treated a peer's account of a peer as unreliable everywhere.
- What is never a source matters more than what is. The refusals are the policy a candidate can trust.

Options:
- (a) The record opens it; the lead is told. (b) The lead opens it, citing the record, and may not open without one. (c) The lead opens it on judgment, as the book has it, with the record as evidence afterwards.

## 5. Attendance (5.8.6)

The book's example is the consistently late employee who should hear it quickly (p. 435). Prior work built attendance states (worked, missed, late against crew call), a counting window, a set of "the building's questions" the check-in asks before any count is read against the person (the shift shape against the person's stated availability, transit, the schedule's language, crew call against the pre-service window), and refusals: no free-text reason, no lead's note, no read in any room, no per-person comparison (17tn048qfnx). It made the count a fifth trigger, checked only after the questions. 3.4.5 reads the same facts for a minor issue in the first weeks.

Considerations:
- Attendance is the most common performance issue in the trade and the most legally routine. A count-based policy is standard; the questions before the count are not, and are the white paper's "employees are the users" (WP p. 17) applied to a schedule.
- General practice, not Sŏn-specific: points-based attendance policies are widespread in hospitality and retail; they are predictable and defensible, and they are also the policies most often cited as treating a transit failure and a no-call the same. The questions-first design is an answer to that.
- No free-text reason field: it protects the person from a lead's narrative and also removes the place where "my child was in hospital" would be written. 5.8.15 on hardship is where the second half lands.
- Counsel: whether a paid stand-down, an unpaid one, or nothing follows repeated no-shows is 5.10's; what the record may hold is 5.8.2's.

Options: (a) states, window, questions first, refusals, count as a reset parameter, as prior work built it. (b) a conventional published points policy with counsel. (c) (a) with a reason the person may add in their own words if they choose.

## 6. The pauses and the seat repeat (5.8.7)

Prior work named two findings that stop the process as the house's failure: a person hired against a documented hiring-stage concern on a promise of support that was never delivered (86akh7rb0), and a plan extended on a promised operational fix that was never made. It also read a seat that opens the process for a second different person as a seat question first (86akhpvqx; 4.3.12 already reads a domain repeat). A third candidate: a training module or readiness row the person needed did not exist (3.3.28's training-infrastructure line).

The book has no pause; its nearest idea is "context" (p. 432) and adjusting responsibilities rather than the bar (p. 433). The white paper's "management absorbs blame from staff" (WP p. 17) is the reason for one.

Consideration: a pause needs a clearer. If no one may clear it but the person who made the unkept promise, the process stalls on a lead's embarrassment. If the second reader clears it, the function exists.

## 7. The conversation (5.8.8)

The book's Phase 1 is hard feedback plus two sentences: this is now a pattern, and you are not meeting the role's expectations, against an objective source (pp. 439 to 440). Its questions for the relieved person are good and specific: what do you think is going on, do you like this work, is this the right role (pp. 442 to 443). Its rule on "can I succeed" is no flat no, but honesty if it will be hard (p. 439). 5.2.5 already decides the hard conversation's form; this adds the two sentences and the standard.

Considerations:
- Prior work would have the trigger read back from the record in the person's own earlier check-in words, so the first sentence of the conversation is something the person already said. That is "no surprises" made literal.
- The lead's hypothesis about the outcome: the book says form one and keep it to yourself unless asked (pp. 438 to 439). Prior work refused any written hypothesis (17tn048qckt) and any spoken prediction. Whether a lead may say "I think this will be hard" is the one place the two disagree.
- Denial: the book's move from disputing facts to sharing observation (p. 441) is the same move 5.2.5's form makes. If new information changes the assessment, the book says so within a week and restarts or debriefs honestly (p. 442).
- Language and setting: off the floor, on the clock, in the person's language, interpreter by 4.8.4's rule. These are already the house's rules for any hard conversation.

## 8. What is written (5.8.9)

The book: deliver in person, then write it down; a line in the 1:1 doc for a one-off, an email or document for a pattern, shared with HR if escalated; documentation builds trust through a shared record (pp. 435 to 436, 443 to 444). Four email templates (pp. 474 to 477). Prior work refused the letters and any record in the lead's voice, on the principle that no document about a person should be one-sided; it kept the person's own words on the person page and let the stack write dates and citations (17tn048qckt, 17tn048qcjt). 5.2.8 decides the record of a hard conversation; 5.2.4 asks counsel what a record must hold for a later separation to stand.

Options:
- (a) The book's manager-authored recap after each conversation, shared with the second reader. Commits Sŏn to a lead who writes about a person and to the trust cost of a one-sided file, offset by the defensibility counsel may say it provides.
- (b) The stack's citations, the person's words, nothing in the lead's voice. Commits Sŏn to a record that may or may not satisfy counsel, and to a person who controls half the file.
- (c) A joint recap read back and signed by both. Commits Sŏn to the book's "shared record" without the one-sidedness, and to a conversation that ends in writing together.

Counsel's answer decides whether (b) is available. The person's language: whichever option, the record is in a language the person reads.

## 9. Whether a situation is ever a field (5.8.10)

The book's shift from coaching to "a formal performance designation" should never be implicit (pp. 439 to 440), and its review template proposes a designation (workbook p. 468). Prior work's rule: the three situations (moving, holding, in question) are dated states of a seat and person, and no record, list, count, or room sorts a person into one; leadership sees counts by seat class and period only (17tn048qfnz, 17tn048qfn8). 5.4 decides the formal review's designations; 5.7.2 the language for holding a seat.

The practical question is what the person page shows. A window with an open date and a close date is a state whether or not it is called one. The decision is whether that state is visible beyond the person and their lead, and whether a formal review ever carries a rating.

## 10. The plan (5.8.11 to 5.8.13)

The book's PIP: a signed standalone document, four to six weeks, role expectations, areas with dated examples, goals, milestones, resources, weekly checks, an end date, early termination, at-will clause, termination warning, two signatures (pp. 477 to 481). Prior work rejected it whole as a manager-authored case file with a verdict built in, and replaced it with a bounded window: opened by the record, measured in a countable number of shifts, its state on the person page, run through a short paid meeting and biweekly check-ins, closed by a hands-on skills check from a separate assessor, ending in held, not held, a move the person chose, or a hand-off (17tn048qfn1). Sŏn also has the ninety-day plan's form (3.3.10), which already names milestones, assessments, and check-ins.

Considerations, by decision:
- Form (5.8.11). The three candidates commit to different things. The PIP commits to what counsel will most easily defend and to the document the book says most employees who never accepted the feedback end up leaving under (p. 448). The window commits to a build (5.8.23) and to an assessor pool that may not exist for every seat. A second plan in 3.3.10's form commits to the least new machinery and to a plan that was designed for a new hire being used for a person in year three.
- What it is never: the person's first hearing of the gap (the book agrees, p. 478); a document in the lead's voice alone; a prediction; the word "probation." The book's termination warning is the sentence Brandon must decide on: counsel may require it; the white paper's "never assert the unproven" (WP p. 36) cuts against predicting.
- Time (5.8.12). The book's three months, or one, and its issue types with different clocks (pp. 437, 445 to 446). Prior work replaced calendar time with shifts because a part-time person's month is not a full-time person's month. Either can be a reset parameter. Who may extend: the book lets the manager end early (p. 480); prior work let only a made-and-cited fix extend.
- Who judges (5.8.13). The book's problem is real: a PIP that ends in a dispute over how to judge it (p. 446). Its answer is peer input or another manager. Sŏn's answer already exists for hiring and readiness: an assessor who neither taught nor decides (3.2.37, 3.3.9, 4.1.11). Using the same pool here keeps the lead from being coach and judge. Its cost is that behavioral gaps (the book's third type) do not show on a hands-on skills check; for those, the readiness rows may not be the right instrument, and the decision should say what is.

## 11. Outcomes (5.8.14)

The book: improved and stays, moves, or leaves (p. 444); most PIPs where the person never accepted the gap end in departure (p. 448); watch the narrow pass that lands back in the same place (p. 448). Prior work: moves are the person's choice from what is open (a seat change, a period or specialization change, a domain change, a step back to an earlier skill level), never placed by a lead and never a demotion; a "not held" with no move chosen hands a defined package to the departure chunk and nothing else; the decision about staying is never the lead's alone (17tn048qfn7, 17tn048qfp0, 17tn048qg2e). 4.3.7 and 3.2.44 already decide how a person moves.

Considerations:
- "Held": does anything remain on the record, and is a second window inside a stated period read differently? The book's narrow-pass warning argues yes; the person's interest argues for a clean close.
- The step back: the white paper has skill levels that unlock upward and nothing about going down. If a step back exists, its pay effect is 5.5's (17tn048qeu8), and whether the person may choose it is this chunk's.
- The hand-off package: what 5.10 receives is what 5.10 may cite. Prior work made that a closed list. The book's managing-out checklist (pp. 481 to 482) starts from the same package: feedback given, documented, PIP run.
- The lead never decides alone: the book's manager owns the decision with HR informed. Prior work reserved it to both partners with counsel. Counsel's answer on whether that policy creates exposure (5.8.2) matters.

## 12. Hardship (5.8.15)

The book: hardship can sit behind a gap; involve HR because protections exist; do not lower the bar; adjust responsibilities, such as a part-time or less demanding role if temporary (p. 433). Prior work refused a lead-offered lesser role and treated it as an ordinary scheduling request the person raises themselves, not a judgment about their life (17tn048qfn7). 4.4.6 governs what a lead may ask about background.

Consideration: the two positions agree on the bar and differ on who initiates. A lead who may not offer leaves a person who does not know to ask. A lead who may offer is making a read about someone's life. The middle is a policy page (5.8.18) that tells everyone what they may ask for, so the offer is standing and impersonal. Leave and accommodation law is counsel's (5.8.2).

## 13. The team (5.8.16)

The book: low performers drag morale disproportionately (pp. 432 to 433). The white paper: adjacent nodes compensate at cost, visible to everyone but the org chart (WP p. 8). The people carrying the load know before the lead does, and they are the tension-slack statement's source. What they hear back is the decision: the fix the domain is making and that they were heard, and nothing about the colleague. 4.3.9 decides what the team hears at a departure.

## 14. The hiring loop (5.8.17)

Prior work closed a named gap: nothing looped a failed hire back into hiring practice (86akh9t9q). 3.4.6 now decides the hiring retrospective and its triggers; 3.4.10 how hiring data joins later records and what is refused. This chunk only confirms that a window in the first year is a trigger and that the retrospective writes to hiring-process records and never to the person (86akh9tca, 86akh7rb0).

## 15. Counsel (5.8.2)

The questions counsel answers before 5.8.9, 5.8.11, 5.8.14, and 5.8.15 close: at-will practice and what precedes a separation; whether a signed plan, a manager's record, or the person's own words defends one; what a record may hold and for how long; leave and accommodation; a paid stand-down's lawfulness; the custodian of records with no HR seat; whether "the lead never decides alone" creates exposure. 5.2.4 has already asked the record-of-a-hard-conversation half. None of this is guessed at in these files.

## 16. Where Sŏn diverges from the book

- The process starts at the seat and the domain, not the person (WP p. 14), and pauses when the house's own promise was the failure.
- Triggers may be records rather than a manager's judgment, if 5.8.5 goes that way; either way the refused sources are the policy.
- The plan may not be the book's PIP; if it is, it is not the person's first hearing and its termination sentence is a counsel question, not a default.
- The close may be judged by an assessor, not the coach.
- Moves are chosen, not placed; there is no demotion, and any step back's pay is 5.5's.
- The lead never decides a separation alone; what follows "not held" is 5.10's on a closed package.
- Weight: the book gives this topic half its performance material. Prior work argued the steady middle deserves more and this less. Brandon decides the proportion by how much of the prior mechanism he keeps.
