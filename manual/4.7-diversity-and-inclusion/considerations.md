# 4.7 Diversity and inclusion: considerations

Topics follow the book's order (pp. 354 to 360). "WP" is the Sŏn investor white paper, September 2026. "Book" is *Scaling People*. Where the white paper is silent, it says so. Legal questions are named for counsel and not answered here.

## 1. Why this is a manager's job, and the central gap

**What the white paper holds.** Inclusion mechanics without a stated position. The candidate chooses the interview language, "a candidate whose first language is not English can interview with someone who speaks it" (WP p. 17); every interview asks "whether the building is equipped to create the environment this person needs to succeed" (WP p. 18); the paid practical replaces the unpaid stage, which is called "frequently degrading" (WP p. 18); the employee area and lockers are "basic dignity, and dignity is a precondition for presence" (WP pp. 19 to 20); engagement is measured "across psychological safety and values alignment" (WP p. 23). No sentence says who the founders hope the team will be, and no field in the applicant system records who a person is beyond seat-relevant facts (3.1.5, 3.1.33).

**From the digest.** The old work named the gap exactly: the book's chapter assumes an employer has taken a position on composition; Sŏn's founding materials have not. The book's own framing is that diverse teams reach outsize results only once psychological safety lets people speak (Book pp. 354 to 355), that diversity includes socioeconomic background, education, orientation, gender identity, belief, and upbringing (Book p. 355), and that referral hiring quietly clones the team (Book p. 355). Her instruction before acting is to educate yourself on the lived experience of underrepresented groups in your country and company (Book p. 356); 4.7.2 carries that as an action, with the readings left to the founders.

**Considerations.**
- The restaurant industry's own pattern is well documented in general terms: kitchens and dish stations staffed largely by immigrants and people of color, dining rooms and management staffed whiter, with the pay and the visibility following the room. A Korean fine-dining room in Austin will be read against that pattern whether or not the founders say anything.
- Referral hiring is the industry's dominant channel. 3.1.13 decided the referral rule; 3.1.37 shows source share per seat. The book's warning is that referrals become the only channel without anyone deciding it.
- The white paper's "no hierarchy of importance" (WP p. 20) and its treatment of the steward as "not a support role" (WP p. 11) are the closest it comes to a position: the seats the industry treats as lowest are named as structural. That is a statement about seats, not about people.

**Options for 4.7.3.**
- (a) State nothing beyond the conduct statement (3.1.7): what a hire is never selected on, every stage in the chosen language, nothing about who a person is on any record beyond the seat's language field, the house reads its own reach and widens it, no target. Commits Sŏn to being judged on its team rather than its words, to no new data, and to answering "what is your position on diversity" with the conduct statement.
- (b) State an aspiration about reach: the house looks for people wherever they are, interviews in their language, and wants its candidate pool to be as broad as the city it serves. Commits Sŏn to widening reach when the read narrows, without collecting identity data, and to a sentence that can be checked against 3.1.37.
- (c) State an aspiration about composition, in the book's terms: the team should reflect some named breadth. Commits Sŏn to measuring it, which requires self-identification data (4.7.4) and counsel's answer (4.7.5), and to tracking something its hiring process is currently built not to record.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book's diversity work runs on measurement by self-identified group (Book p. 205). Sŏn's hiring was built to read four things and record nothing about identity. Either the founders accept that they will never know the team's composition except by looking at it, or they add a voluntary, walled-off field. Both are coherent; the incoherent position is stating a composition goal and refusing the data.

## 2. Identity data

**What the white paper holds.** Nothing. The applicant system records source, format, language, and status (3.1.33); the person page's refusals (2.3.11) keep identity off it.

**From the digest.** The old work defined what data would exist without self-identification (source, pool tag, chosen language and format, stage outcomes, decline reasons, interviewer, dates) and how it would be read (a weekly hiring look, a monthly calibration, a ninety-day check, an annual reset), with refusals on targets, figures, names, and inferences. It left open whether candidates are ever asked to self-identify and, in a comment, argued for declining for now. That argument is one option below, not a verdict.

**Counsel questions (4.7.5), in substance.** What protected-characteristic data may lawfully be asked, recorded, retained, or acted on in Texas, and on what form; whether a source, stage, and language read is lawful as designed; what language requirement a seat may carry and how it is stated on a posting; and what may be asked about a candidate's or an employee's background or family in an interview, a career conversation (4.4.3), or ordinary conversation. The last one matters because the book's own route to "less visible diversity" is getting to know people's backgrounds (Book p. 355), and a policy against asking about origins or family cuts against it.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Employers that collect self-identification in the United States do it on a voluntary form separate from the application, state that it is not seen by anyone who decides, and use it in aggregate; larger employers are required to report aggregate counts to the federal government, smaller ones are not. Restaurants at Sŏn's size almost never collect it.

**Options for 4.7.4.**
- (a) Never. The reach read runs on source, stage, and chosen language alone. Commits Sŏn to not knowing its composition except by sight, and to answering any outside question about it honestly that way.
- (b) Voluntary, after the hire decision (or after the packet closes for declined candidates), aggregate only, invisible to every assessor, decider, lead, and mentor, never joined to a person page, retention set by counsel. Commits Sŏn to a walled build (4.7.15), to counsel's wording, and to reading a small number honestly, which at a team of forty means most cuts are too small to read.
- (c) Not now; reopen at the first reset (4.7.19). Commits Sŏn to a date and to designing the read so a field can be added without rebuilding it.

## 3. Hiring: the reach read, the held seat, and the interviewer mix

**What the white paper holds.** "No unanswered applications" and "no one is ghosted" (WP pp. 17 to 18); speed is respect, so a decision is made quickly when skills and alignment are there (WP p. 18); leadership is meant to come from within until "the building rarely hires a manager from outside at all" (WP p. 19). Interviewers are trained and interviews assess competencies (WP p. 18). Nothing on the shape of the pipeline or who sits on a loop.

**From the digest.** Hiring-reach monitoring, as background: a pipeline can be checked for whether one source dominates a seat's candidates and whether any stage declines candidates from one source or one chosen language at a different rate, read as a pattern with no target, no name, and no inference about anyone, and acted on structurally (post elsewhere, activate another source, flag a stage or an interviewer for coaching). The book's "don't start interviewing until you have a strong, diverse pipeline" (Book p. 356) raises whether a seat is ever held open for breadth; a held seat is a load on the people covering it. The book's "add someone to your interviewing team who has a very different background from yours" (Book p. 356) raises whether interviewers are ever chosen for their own diversity.

**Considerations.**
- 3.1.7 already decided that a pattern may change outreach and never the bar or a scorecard. 4.7.6's job is the rest: who reads, how often, what counts as a pattern, and the held-seat question.
- The white paper's speed principle and the book's wide-pipeline principle pull against each other. The honest middle is to fill the seat when a candidate clears the bar and widen the reach for the next one, and to say so.
- On the interviewer mix, Sŏn's version of "a different background" may already exist in structure: 3.2.40's outside-domain check on entry-level packets, and a loop that crosses strands. Whether that is the whole rule, or whether the rule also names training background or language, is 4.7.7's. What the book means by "background" includes life experience; what Sŏn can select an interviewer on without reading identity is job-relevant difference.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies that track pipeline shape commonly read source and stage pass-through per role and respond by changing where they post; holding a seat open for pipeline reasons is rare outside large companies and is usually a rule about the slate for senior roles, not for hourly ones.

**Options for 4.7.6.** (a) The hiring manager reads the report at each opening; the calibrating partner reads it monthly; patterns produce the listed acts; no seat waits. (b) The same, with a rule that a leadership seat's slate is not closed until at least two sources have produced candidates. (c) The reach read is the leads' review's, weekly, and the acts are theirs. Each commits Sŏn to the refusals (no target, no name, no act on a candidate).

**Options for 4.7.7.** (a) The outside-domain check and the trained-interviewer class are the whole rule. (b) A loop must include one reader from another strand or domain. (c) A loop must include one reader who speaks the candidate's chosen language natively, where one exists. Each commits Sŏn to never choosing an interviewer for a personal characteristic.

## 4. Performance assessment, reward, and recognition

**What the white paper holds.** "The path is explicit before they start it. Every position has documented competency requirements visible to anyone" (WP p. 11); "no one's advancement waits on a manager's attention or judgment. The system is the advocate" (WP p. 11); advancement is tied to demonstrated mastery and signed off in a competency conversation (WP p. 19); "any qualified team member can create training modules and get paid for teaching" (WP p. 19); mentors are "a proven cultural steward rather than simply the strongest performer" (WP p. 18); designations "mark accountability in the moment" and "are not permanent elevations" (WP p. 10); retention rate, internal promotion rate, and participation in development are tracked (WP p. 23). The white paper does not say how a designation, a module, a mentor pairing, a project, or an event is offered to people, and it names no equity check.

**From the digest.** The book's failure modes are tactical: a project filled by tapping someone informally, a promotion decided without data, a hire because you used to work together (Book pp. 357 to 358). Its fix is a lightweight explicit process: make opportunities visible and let people volunteer; ask why when an expected volunteer does not; agree the criteria; run equity checks like calibration and pay parity, light as a hospital checklist (Book p. 358). The white paper's internal-promotion ambition already implies the leading measure the book names for a diverse leadership pipeline (Book p. 357). A pay-parity read has not been designed and needs 5.5's mechanics and counsel; no figures or mechanics here.

**Considerations.**
- Sŏn has removed the advancement tap: the system schedules the assessment. What remains tappable is everything else: who holds the room designation tonight, who writes the module, who mentors the new cohort, who works the event, who cross-trains first. Those are where the book's failure mode lives at Sŏn, and 4.1.19's rotation floor is the only rule so far.
- Without identity data, the equity checks read by domain, period, strand, source, and chosen language. That is enough to see that late night never produces a designation holder, or that people hired through one source are never signed off, and it is honest about what it cannot see.
- A small unit's numbers identify people. A read of "retention in the kitchen's prep strand" with three people in it is a read of three names.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Posting internal opportunities and letting people apply is standard in larger companies and rare in restaurants, where the manager's tap is the norm; the book's hospital-checklist analogy for equity checks (Book p. 358) refers to the well-documented use of short surgical and central-line checklists to cut error rates.

**Options for 4.7.8.** (a) Every opportunity is posted on the team home with criteria and a window; the lead chooses among volunteers and the record shows who volunteered and who was chosen. (b) Designations rotate by rule (4.1.19) and everything else is posted. (c) The lead offers opportunities by judgment, with a log read at the reset for repeat recipients. Each commits Sŏn to a record; (c) commits it to trusting the log to catch the tap.

**Options for 4.7.9.** (a) The reads above, at the house review, producing process acts. (b) The same plus a pay read across the point system at 5.5's cadence. (c) Calibration in the review (5.4) only, and the rest waits for a year of data. Each commits Sŏn to the rule that a check never produces an act on a named person.

## 5. Running teams

**What the white paper holds.** The front line is "the most sensitive position" (WP p. 09); feedback runs both directions and "team members weigh in on management effectiveness" (WP p. 19); a dedicated channel closes its loop (WP p. 22); the ninety-day plan has regular check-ins (WP p. 18). The Maitre d is accountable to "experience quality and team health" (WP p. 10). Nothing on airtime, on observing a lead, or on how a quiet person is heard.

**From the digest.** The book's airtime rule (give someone who has not spoken a chance to) generalizes at Sŏn to: every person needs a way for their voice to reach someone that does not depend on a room and does not rely on a lead noticing silence. The onboarding question, whether every new person feels prepared to contribute after a month (Book p. 358), is carried by 4.7.10 into the ninety-day check-ins. Whatever the house decides about meetings generally should be checked against inclusion.

**Considerations.**
- Sŏn's rooms are short. The brief has a compressed version (2.3.7) and a team-content slot with a skip rule (4.5.13). The book's "draw out anyone who has not spoken" fits the leads' review and the check-in better than the brief; for the crew, the written channel and the check-in's upward question do the work.
- The person who does not share the room's language is the clearest case. 4.8.5's interpreter designation covers the spoken brief; the written channel in their language covers the rest, if 4.8.2's list is kept.
- The book's instruction to have someone observe you (Book p. 359) has no natural observer at Sŏn: the other lead is a peer, a founder is the boss. 4.6.15's neutral party is the candidate; the founders' own seats are read by the same diagnostic (4.2.12), and there is no reason this observation should stop at the leads.
- Accountability for the environment (Book p. 360) is where the white paper's leading indicators become a lead's business: the pulse by unit, the psychological-safety read (4.5.25), retention by unit. Attaching them to a lead's review (5.4) is the strong form; showing them to the lead in their check-in is the light form.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies that run engagement surveys commonly report results by manager only above a minimum team size (often five or more) to protect individuals; a practice worth borrowing in the form of a rule, not a number.

**Options for 4.7.11.** (a) Two non-room paths for everyone (the check-in's upward question and the channel), plus a chair's duty to draw out in the leads' review. (b) The same, plus a written line from every person weekly (4.8.8). (c) The brief's round (4.5.6) is the mechanism and nothing more is added. Each commits Sŏn to the interpreter or the written channel for the person outside the room's language.

**Options for 4.7.12.** (a) The other lead observes, quarterly, and tells privately. (b) The neutral party from 4.6.15 observes every lead and the founders, twice a year. (c) No formal observation; the pulse and the reads are the observation. Each commits Sŏn to recording nothing about the lead beyond that it happened.

**Options for 4.7.13.** (a) The unit reads enter the lead's review (5.4) with a minimum-size rule. (b) The reads are shown to the lead in their check-in and at the house review, and never enter the review. (c) Only retention and the pulse attach; the rest is the house's. Each commits Sŏn to a bad read starting with the process.

## 6. The closing frames

The book closes with attention to other people in small ways (David Foster Wallace), the impossibility of fully knowing another's experience alongside a shared vision, and Chloé Valdary's three tenets: treat people as human beings, criticize to uplift, root everything in love and compassion (Book pp. 359 to 360). The digest judged these relevant only if a principles document is being written; that is 2.1.5, and the founders may or may not want a line from here in it. The correction rule (4.5.8) is where "criticize to uplift" already lives at Sŏn.

## 7. Where Sŏn diverges from the book, in one place

- The book measures by self-identified group; Sŏn's hiring records nothing about identity unless 4.7.4 changes that.
- The book's opportunity tap is the promotion; Sŏn removed that tap and left the smaller ones (designations, modules, mentoring, events).
- The book's observer is a trusted colleague; Sŏn has no HR and a very small leadership team, so the observer is a peer, a partner, or an outsider.
- The book's team is large enough to read by group; Sŏn's units are small enough that a read by unit is a read of names, so every check needs a minimum-size rule.
- The book's chapter is written by a US-based technology leader about corporate America; Sŏn's context is a Texas restaurant with a kitchen, a dish station, and a dining room, where the industry's pattern is older and better known than any technology company's.
