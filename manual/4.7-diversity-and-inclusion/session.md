# 4.7 Diversity and inclusion: working session guide

## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** The book's short section on diversity, equity, inclusion, and belonging as a manager's job (pp. 354 to 359): hiring, performance and reward, and running teams. At Sŏn the mechanics already exist in pieces (the interview language, the paid practical, competencies visible to anyone, the system as the advocate), and no one has said whether the house states a position on who its team should be, or whether it will ever know. This session asks that, decides whether identity data is ever collected, and then works through the smaller taps the book warns about: who gets the designation tonight, who writes the module, who mentors, who is heard.

**Already decided upstream, so not reopened here.** The hiring conduct statement (3.1.7): what a hire is never selected on, whether identity is recorded beyond language, and that a pattern may change outreach and never the bar. The languages offered (3.1.16, 3.2.15). The pipeline report's fields and refusals (3.1.39). The calibration read (3.2.41) and the outside-domain check (3.2.42). The onboarding read (3.3.27). The rotation floor for designations (4.1.20). Read those `decisions.md` entries first.

**Who else must agree.** Dominic on 4.7.3, 4.7.4, 4.7.6, 4.7.9, 4.7.13, and on any build (4.7.15, 4.7.16). Counsel on 4.7.5 before 4.7.14 or 4.7.15 ship. The chef partner on the kitchen's opportunities (4.7.8).

## 3. Briefs

### 4.7.3 Decide whether Sŏn states anything about who it hopes its team will be, beyond the conduct statement

**The question:** does the house say anything about who it hopes its team will be, or only about how it hires?

**Why it matters now:** the careers page and the first postings go up before the first hire, and silence is a position too. A stated composition goal needs data the hiring process was built not to collect; a conduct-only position needs the founders to be at peace answering "what is your stance" with a description of process.

**Openers:**
- A journalist writing about Korean fine dining in Austin asks who works in your kitchen and who works your floor. What do you want to be true, and what do you want to be able to say?
- Sideways: the white paper says the steward is not a support role and there is no hierarchy of importance. That is a statement about seats. Is there a statement about people behind it, or is that the whole of it?
- Walk the room at month six. Who is in the dining room, who is at the pass, who is on dish. If it looks like every other restaurant in town, did anything go wrong?
- What did the reading (4.7.2) change about how you hear this question?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Conduct only (3.1.7), reach, or composition?
- If reach: can the sentence be checked against 3.1.39? If composition: are you prepared to collect the data (4.7.4)?
- Where does it appear, and who may change it?
- Could the sentence be held by any restaurant in the city? If yes, is it worth stating?

**What the book says:** diverse teams reach outsize results once psychological safety exists; referrals clone the team; assess what backgrounds and viewpoints the team lacks; hiring alone is not enough (pp. 354 to 357).

**White-paper default:** the interview language is the candidate's choice; the building asks whether it can create what the person needs; engagement is measured across psychological safety (WP pp. 17, 18, 23). No position on composition (silent).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most restaurants state nothing; larger hospitality groups commonly publish a values sentence about inclusion without a measurable goal; technology companies commonly publish annual composition reports, which requires the data.

**Options:** (a) conduct only. (b) a reach aspiration, checkable without identity data. (c) a composition aspiration, which commits to 4.7.4 (b). Depth: cons. 1.

**Watch for:** a sentence that asserts a team that does not exist yet (the white paper's rule against asserting the unproven, WP p. 36); a sentence written for the journalist rather than the cook.

**A finished answer:** one of the three, the sentence if there is one, its placement, its owner; Dominic agrees.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.7.4 Decide whether anyone is ever asked to self-identify, and under what limits

**The question:** will a candidate or an employee ever be asked, voluntarily, to state protected characteristics, and if so, under what wall?

**Why it matters now:** the applicant system is being configured (3.1.35). A field added later means a rebuild; a field added now without counsel is a liability. And 4.7.3 (c) cannot be measured without it.

**Openers:**
- A cook fills in the application in Spanish, gets the job, and three weeks later gets an optional form asking about race and gender. What do they think it is for? Who do they think sees it?
- Sideways: the book's bias control in hiring is to review outcomes by self-identified group. Sŏn's hiring reads four things and records nothing about identity. Say out loud which one you are choosing.
- At forty people, most cuts of that data are too small to read honestly. What would you actually do with it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Never, walled, or not now with a date?
- If walled: after the hire decision or after the packet closes; aggregate only; invisible to every assessor, decider, lead, and mentor; never on a person page; retention set by counsel. All of those?
- If never: is the reach read on source, stage, and language enough for you to answer 4.7.3 honestly?

**What the book says:** review hiring outcomes by self-identified group (p. 205); equity checks like calibration and pay parity (p. 358).

**White-paper default:** the system records seat-relevant facts (WP p. 17); silent on identity data.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. US employers that collect it use a voluntary form separate from the application, unseen by deciders, used in aggregate; large employers must report aggregate counts, small ones need not. Restaurants at this size almost never collect it.

**Options:** (a) never. (b) voluntary and walled. (c) not now, reopen at the first reset. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** deciding (b) and building the wall later; deciding (a) and then stating a composition goal.

**A finished answer:** one of the three; if (b), the limits in writing and the handoff to counsel; if (c), the date.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; counsel on the form.

### 4.7.6 Decide the reach read: who reads the pipeline report, at what rhythm, what acts it may produce, and whether a seat ever waits on the breadth of its pipeline

**The question:** the report exists; who looks at it, how often, what may they do about a pattern, and does a seat ever stay open because the pipeline is narrow?

**Why it matters now:** 3.1.7 said a pattern may change outreach and never the bar. Nobody has said who notices a pattern. And the white paper's speed principle collides with the book's wide-pipeline principle on the first lead hire.

**Openers:**
- Every candidate for the Maitre d seat came from one person's network. The best of them clears the bar. Do you hire on Friday, or post somewhere else first?
- Sideways: referrals are the industry's channel because they work. What is the first sign, on the report, that they have become the only channel?
- A stage declines candidates who interviewed in Spanish at a different rate than those who interviewed in English. Who should see that first, and what is the first thing they do?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Reader and rhythm: the hiring manager per opening, the calibrating partner monthly, the leads' review weekly?
- What counts as a pattern: one source dominating; a stage's decline rate differing by source or chosen language. Anything else?
- Acts allowed: post elsewhere, another source, change the process languages, a stage or an interviewer to coaching. Acts refused: a target, the bar, a named candidate.
- The held seat: never; only for a leadership slate; fill and widen for the next?

**What the book says:** build a wide pipeline before interviewing; watch referrals; add an interviewer unlike you (pp. 356 to 357).

**White-paper default:** no unanswered applications, speed is respect (WP pp. 17 to 18); leadership from within (WP p. 19). Silent on the pipeline's shape.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies that track pipeline shape read source and stage pass-through per role and change where they post; holding a seat for slate reasons is mostly a senior-role rule in large companies.

**Options:** (a) hiring manager per opening plus the calibrating partner monthly; no seat waits. (b) the same, with a two-source rule for leadership slates. (c) the leads' review owns it weekly. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** a "pattern" read off three candidates; the read becoming a reason to slow a seat the floor is covering.

**A finished answer:** reader, rhythm, the pattern definition, the two lists of acts, and the held-seat rule.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.7.7 Decide whether an interview loop deliberately includes a reader unlike the hiring manager

**The question:** must a loop include someone who differs from the hiring manager, and in what?

**Why it matters now:** the first loops are the two founders, who share a great deal. The book's fix is a reader who pushes back on your assumptions. Sŏn already has the outside-domain check (3.2.42); this decides whether that is the whole rule.

**Openers:**
- You and Dominic interview the same candidate and agree in ten minutes. What did you both miss, and who in the building would have caught it?
- Sideways: the book means "background" as life experience. You can only choose an interviewer on job-relevant difference. What job-relevant difference gets you most of what she wants?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Is 3.2.42 plus the trained-interviewer class (3.2.14) the whole rule?
- If more: another strand or domain; another training background; a native speaker of the candidate's chosen language?
- The rule that no interviewer is chosen for a personal characteristic: written.

**What the book says:** add an interviewer with a different background and strengths who complements your intuition and pushes back (pp. 356 to 357).

**White-paper default:** trained interviewers, semi-structured interviews on competencies (WP p. 18); silent on the loop's makeup.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Structured-interview programs commonly require at least one interviewer from outside the hiring team; that is a difference of vantage, not identity.

**Options:** (a) 3.2.42 and 3.2.14 are the rule. (b) one reader from another strand or domain, always. (c) one native speaker of the chosen language, where one exists. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** a rule that quietly selects interviewers by identity; a rule the founders cannot meet before the first lead exists.

**A finished answer:** the rule in one sentence and the refusal in one sentence.

**Needs agreement from:** no one beyond Brandon.

### 4.7.8 Decide how opportunities inside the house are opened and filled

**The question:** for everything the house offers that is not an advancement (the room designation tonight, writing a module, mentoring, a working group, an event, a cross-training slot), is it posted and volunteered for, or does the lead tap someone?

**Why it matters now:** the white paper removed the advancement tap; the system schedules the assessment. Every other tap is still in the lead's hand, and the book's failure mode lives precisely there. The rotation floor (4.1.20) is the only rule so far.

**Openers:**
- It is the third month. Who has held the room designation most? Who has never been asked? How would you know?
- Sideways: the white paper pays anyone qualified to write a module. Who writes the first ten? The person the lead thinks of, or the person who wanted to?
- A quiet cook would be a good mentor. The lead does not think of her. What in the system would?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Posted with criteria and a window, always; designations by rotation and the rest posted; or the lead's judgment with a log read at the reset?
- The criteria a volunteer is read on; who chooses among volunteers; the record kept.
- The rule for the lead who notices an expected volunteer did not raise a hand: ask why, help find the path.
- The kitchen's opportunities: the chef partner's.

**What the book says:** make opportunities visible and let people volunteer rather than tapping the person who looks like you; if someone you expected does not volunteer, ask why (pp. 357 to 358).

**White-paper default:** advancement is self-paced and the system is the advocate (WP p. 11); module authorship is open and paid (WP p. 19); mentors are proven cultural stewards (WP p. 18). Silent on how the rest is offered.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Posting internal opportunities is standard in larger companies and rare in restaurants, where the tap is the norm.

**Options:** (a) everything posted. (b) rotation for designations, posting for the rest. (c) the lead's judgment with a log. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** a posting rule so heavy the lead stops offering anything; a log nobody reads.

**A finished answer:** the rule per opportunity type, the criteria, the chooser, the record, and the "ask why" duty; the kitchen marked pending.

**Needs agreement from:** no one beyond Brandon for the floor; the chef partner for the kitchen.

### 4.7.9 Decide the equity checks Sŏn runs, and on what records

**The question:** which light checks does the house run to see whether the same people keep getting the assessments, the designations, the modules, and the pay, read by what, and by whom?

**Why it matters now:** the book's point is that these are checklists, not audits, and that they need to exist before there is a problem. Without identity data they read by domain, period, strand, source, and language; that has to be designed, not improvised.

**Openers:**
- Late night has been open for six months and has never produced a designation holder or an advancement. Where would that show up, and who would see it?
- Sideways: the book compares equity checks to the hospital checklist that cut infection rates. What is the five-line checklist a lead runs before the quarter closes?
- The prep strand has three people. A read of its retention is a read of three names. What is the rule?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The list: assessments scheduled and passed; designations and opportunities for repeat holders; internal-promotion rate against outside hires (3.1.40); calibration in the review (5.4); a pay read across the point system (5.5). Which of these, now?
- Read by: domain, period, strand, source, chosen language. Never by an inferred attribute.
- Rhythm: the house review, the reset.
- A check produces a process act, never an act on a person: written.
- The minimum-size rule.

**What the book says:** a common, agreed way to evaluate who is promoted or given a project; calibration; pay parity analysis; light, not heavy (p. 358).

**White-paper default:** competencies visible to anyone (WP p. 11); advancement tied to mastery (WP p. 19); retention and internal promotion tracked (WP p. 23). No check named.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies commonly report engagement and advancement results only above a minimum group size to protect individuals.

**Options:** (a) all the reads above at the house review. (b) the same plus a pay read at 5.5's cadence. (c) calibration only for the first year. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** a figure creeping into this work (the pay read's mechanics are 5.5's); a read that names a person by being too small.

**A finished answer:** the list, the cuts, the readers, the rhythm, the process-only rule, and the minimum-size rule.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.7.11 Decide how every person's voice reaches someone without depending on a room

**The question:** what are the two or more ways any person at Sŏn can be heard that do not require speaking up in a room, and what does a lead owe the person who never speaks?

**Why it matters now:** the brief is short and the rooms are few. The book's airtime rule assumes a meeting; Sŏn's crew has a brief with a skip rule and a check-in. This decides where the quiet person's signal goes, including the person who does not share the room's language.

**Openers:**
- Name the last person you worked with who had the best read on the room and never said a word in a meeting. How did their read reach anyone?
- Sideways: the white paper says the front line is the most sensitive position. A sensor that cannot transmit is a sensor you paid for and cannot hear. Which of Sŏn's mechanisms is the wire?
- A dishwasher who speaks only Spanish has an idea about the close. Walk it from his head to the leads' review.

**Narrowing questions:**
- The two non-room paths: the check-in's upward question and the feedback channel. A third: a weekly written line (4.8.7)?
- The room duty: the chair draws out anyone who has not spoken, in the leads' review and the brief's round (4.5.6)?
- The person outside the room's language: the interpreter designation (4.8.4) or the written channel in their language, or both?
- What a lead does when the same three people always speak.

**What the book says:** watch airtime, draw out anyone who has not spoken; agenda collection, offsite planning, how a meeting runs, how a decision is made, each is a chance (pp. 358 to 359).

**White-paper default:** the front line is the most sensitive position (WP p. 09); feedback runs both directions (WP p. 19); the channel closes its loop (WP p. 22). Silent on the quiet person.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Well-run kitchens often collect the close's notes in writing from every station precisely because the loudest cook is not the most informed one.

**Options:** (a) two non-room paths plus a chair's duty. (b) the same plus a weekly written line from everyone. (c) the brief's round is the mechanism. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** making the bilingual person the only wire; a duty on the chair that the compressed brief cannot honour.

**A finished answer:** the paths named, the chair's duty stated, the language case answered, and the "same three people" rule.

**Needs agreement from:** no one beyond Brandon.

### 4.7.12 Decide how a lead's own inclusiveness is observed, founders included

**The question:** who watches a lead run a brief and a review and tells them, privately, who they asked, who they corrected, and who they never looked at?

**Why it matters now:** the book's instruction is to assume you can do better and have someone you trust tell you where. Sŏn has no HR and a leadership team of a handful; the observer is a peer, a partner, or an outsider, and the founders should not be exempt.

**Openers:**
- Who has ever watched you run a pre-shift and told you afterwards what you did? What did they say?
- Sideways: the book says ask someone you trust. At Sŏn, the person the Maitre d trusts most may be the person least able to say it. Who else is there?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Observer: the other lead, a founder, the neutral party from 4.6.15?
- Cadence; what they look for (who spoke, who was asked, who was corrected and how, which language the room ran in).
- Told privately, in the check-in the partner holds; nothing recorded beyond the fact it happened.
- The founders' own seats: observed the same way, by whom?

**What the book says:** assume your management can be more inclusive; ask someone you trust to observe and tell you where (p. 359).

**White-paper default:** team members weigh in on management effectiveness (WP p. 19); silent on observation.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Peer observation of meetings with a short checklist is common in teaching and in hospital teams; in restaurants it is rare and usually informal.

**Options:** (a) the other lead, quarterly. (b) the neutral party, twice a year, everyone including founders. (c) none formal; the reads are the observation. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** the observation becoming a record about the lead; the founders exempting themselves.

**A finished answer:** observer, cadence, the look-for list, the telling rule, the no-record rule, and the founders' line.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders, since they are observed too.

### 4.7.13 Decide how a lead is held accountable for the environment they create

**The question:** which unit-level reads attach to a lead, where the lead sees them, and whether they enter the lead's review.

**Why it matters now:** the white paper makes the Maitre d accountable to team health and names the leading indicators; nothing attaches an indicator to a lead. The strong form (into the review) and the light form (shown in the check-in) are different commitments, and small units identify people.

**Openers:**
- Dinner's pulse drops for two months while late night's holds. What does the Maitre d see, when, and from whom?
- Sideways: the book says hold leaders accountable for the environment, not just the composition. What is the difference at Sŏn between a lead whose numbers are bad and a lead whose unit is unwell?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Which reads attach: the pulse by unit, the psychological-safety read (4.5.25), retention by unit, the checks from 4.7.9?
- Where the lead sees them: the check-in, the house review.
- Into the review (5.4)? With what minimum-size rule?
- A bad read starts with the process: written.

**What the book says:** hold yourself and other leaders accountable for the environment each manager creates (p. 360).

**White-paper default:** the Maitre d is accountable to experience quality and team health (WP p. 10); employee NPS, psychological safety, and retention are tracked (WP pp. 22 to 23). Not attached to a lead.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies that put engagement results into a manager's review usually do it above a minimum team size and alongside, not instead of, the manager's own account of the quarter.

**Options:** (a) into the review with a minimum-size rule. (b) shown in the check-in and the house review, never in the review. (c) retention and the pulse only. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** a lead managing the number instead of the unit; a figure sneaking in through retention "cost."

**A finished answer:** the reads, where seen, the review answer with its size rule, and the process-first sentence.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
