# 6.1 Manage your time and energy: working session

## 1. Orientation

This chunk is the first of three from the book's Conclusion, and it is about the founders themselves: their time, their energy, and what the house does and does not depend on them for. The book's advice is personal (map your good and bad days, set boundaries, delegate, protect your best window, disappoint people with your time, ask for help, pp. 485 to 490). At Sŏn most of those tactics already have a mechanism doing their work; what is left is to decide which layer holds each protection, what reads a founder's load when no one stands above, and what each founder writes for themselves.

Working rules for the room: Brandon decides, the facilitator never does. Options and defaults are starting points. Nothing he says about his own energy, hours, working style, or commitments outside the house gets recorded here; the deliverables of this chunk that hold such things (6.1.13) are his and stay out of this repo and ClickUp. If he starts describing what he is like under load, that is material for his own document; note that it came up, not what it was. Brain dumps go to `notes/YYYY-MM-DD-<topic>.md` in his words before they are funneled, and a brain dump about himself is saved only if he asks.

Upstream, already decided or in progress: the working-with-me document's prompts and the self-exercises (1.1.2, 1.1.3, 1.1.6, 1.1.7); the knowledge-transfer list (1.1.12); the tempos ruling and its dinner-quarter consequence (1.4.3); the partners' weekly review, domain read, and page (2.3.4, 2.3.6); the load line on a founder's seat at the reset (4.2.12); the delegation rule and the handover list (4.4.8, 4.4.18); the absence rule in the seat descriptions (2.1.8, 2.1.9); the alert-actor rule (2.2.30); emergency authority during a service (2.2.7); designations (4.1.20, 4.1.21); the two kinds of live speech (5.2.6); the founders' training gates (3.2.25, 5.1.17, 5.4.26, 5.8.25).

Who else must agree: both seated founders on every decision; Dominic writes his own documents (6.1.14); the chef partner's floor and kitchen rule waits on 2.1.10; counsel on the questions collected in 6.3.7.

Why it sits here: the book closes with the manager's own strength because nothing else in it works at full power without it (p. 485). At Sŏn the order is reversed in one respect: the house's protections were built first, chunk by chunk, and this session checks they hold for the people who built them, then leaves each founder to write the rest.

## 3. Briefs

### 6.1.2 Decide whether a founder's protection is a personal discipline, a house mechanism, or both

**The question:** when a founder's bandwidth is protected at Sŏn, is it because the founder guards it, because the house never asks for it at the wrong moment, or both?

**Why it matters now:** every other decision in this chunk sits on this one. If it is discipline, the house builds nothing and 6.1.16 is a short page. If it is mechanism, the house has to test that its mechanisms hold with both founders gone (6.1.19) before the opening gate. The white paper has already answered it for the team ("mechanical, not aspirational," WP p. 17); the question is whether the same answer reaches the people who wrote it.

**Openers:**
- The book's line is "no one else will protect your boundaries for you" (p. 488). At a restaurant built so it does not depend on either founder's memory (WP p. 05), who protects them?
- Sideways: you wrote that Sŏn has built systems for how the founder thinks and knows the trap (WP p. 17). Where would that trap show up first in your own week?
- Think of the last operator you watched burn out. Was it a failure of their discipline, or of the building around them?
- If Dominic's week fell apart for a month and nothing in the house noticed, what would that say about the house?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Is there anything a founder's own discipline protects that no mechanism could? Name it. If nothing, (b) is on the table.
- Is there anything a mechanism protects that a founder would rather keep as their own call? Name it. If something, (a) or (c).
- Should the team ever see the house's layer (6.1.16's team-home half), or only the partners?

**What the book says:** give yourself permission to set boundaries; treat being your best self as part of the job; no one else will do it for you (pp. 487 to 488).

**White-paper default:** bandwidth before results is a rule of the system (WP pp. 12, 17); cooling failure is a system failure, not a character flaw (WP pp. 13, 17); the founders name the trap of building for themselves (WP p. 17). Silent on the founders' own boundaries.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Founder-led companies without a board or a manager above the founders rarely have any mechanism reading founder load; the usual substitutes are a coach, a co-founder check-in, or nothing. The book's own context, a large company with a manager above her, is the exception, not the norm.

**Options:** (a) the founder's own discipline, written only in their document; (b) the house's mechanisms, and the founder writes nothing; (c) both layers, kept apart. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** choosing (b) and then describing the house's mechanisms as things a founder will "make sure" happen; choosing (a) and then asking the leads to protect a founder's time; anything Brandon says about his own boundaries being written down here.

**A finished answer:** one sentence a lead could repeat; if (c), which layer holds which of the protections in cons. 12's table.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 6.1.4 Decide how a founder's load is read between resets, and what of it the other partner sees

**The question:** between resets, does anything read whether a founder's seat is overloaded, and if so, who writes it, from what, and what may it never become?

**Why it matters now:** the loads are heaviest before opening, when the founders hold every function by default (3.1.4, 3.2.6, 3.3.6, 2.3.5). 4.2.12 gives each founder's seat a load line at the reset; that is a quarter away at best. The leading indicators read the team and read the founders nowhere (WP pp. 12, 22).

**Openers:**
- The book's story: she only saw how overloaded she was when she said it aloud in a 1:1 (p. 490). You have no 1:1 above you. Where would you say it?
- Sideways: the white paper sizes the content engine by output per session, not by hours (WP p. 30). That is a load the record can read without asking you anything. What else about your seat is like that?
- If Dominic were carrying too much, what would you see first: a missed review, an ask open for three weeks, a live risk with no daily state? Name the record, not the feeling.
- The earlier work listed what the read may never be (a rating, a case file, a read of health, an input to pay, a trigger under the agreement). Read the list. Is anything missing from it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Between resets: nothing, a line the other partner writes, or a line each founder writes about themselves?
- If a line exists, is it inside the monthly domain read (2.3.4) or separate?
- What are its sources? Only records. Which ones from cons. 3's list, and any others?
- Is the cross-domain ask (2.3.4) the house's whole form of "ask for help," or is something else needed when the overload is inside the asking founder's own domain?

**What the book says:** review incomplete to-dos monthly; the ones that stall belong in two piles; ask for help before you are at the end of your rope, and saying it aloud is how you find out where you are (pp. 486 to 487, 490).

**White-paper default:** nothing reads the center (WP pp. 12, 22); the content engine's ratio is one readable founder load (WP p. 30). 4.2.12 holds the reset line.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Co-founder pairs that last tend to schedule the check on each other rather than leave it to noticing; the common form is a standing conversation, not a record. Sŏn's preference for records over impressions is its own.

**Options:** (a) nothing between resets; (b) a monthly line written by the other partner from records, on the partners' page; (c) each founder writes their own monthly line in the leads' upward form. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** a line whose sources include "how the other partner seems"; a line that becomes an agenda item at the weekly review rather than a written entry; Brandon answering the openers with a description of himself under load, which belongs in his document (6.1.12), not here.

**A finished answer:** the choice; the sources as a list of records; the refusals as a list; the ask confirmed or supplemented; who writes it for the chef seat once there are three partners.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 6.1.5 Decide whether the founders keep one inventory of what sits on each seat, or read the existing lists together

**The question:** the house already keeps four lists that touch what a founder holds (1.1.12, 2.3.5, 4.4.18, 2.2.35). Is there a fifth, one inventory per founder seat, or are the four read together?

**Why it matters now:** several loads the white paper places on a founder's seat are on none of the four: the content engine's cadence and streaming (WP p. 30), the founder consultation and leadership review of content (WP p. 31), the acoustic consultation with an unnamed founder (WP p. 21), the chef partner search, the raise's public-voice component. Each is a standing load with no hand-over in view, and nothing shows it.

**Openers:**
- Read the white paper's line about the acoustic consultation "with a founder who holds an educational background in audio engineering" (WP p. 21). Whose seat is that on, and where is it written?
- Sideways: the book's manager keeps a list of priorities that ladder up to goals (p. 486). A founder's list at Sŏn ladders up to a hand-over date. What is on yours that has no date because no one exists to hand it to?
- If the reset asked "what does Brandon's seat hold by default that it should not," which document would answer today?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Four lists read together, or one inventory per seat?
- If one inventory: is it on the partners' page (2.3.6)? Does each partner keep their own seat's rows?
- The three marks (by design, by default, record-native): keep them, or use plainer words?
- The unassigned rows (acoustic consultation; content review): assign now, or mark open?

**What the book says:** delegate almost everything; what stalls on your list tells you what to hand off or reschedule (pp. 486 to 487).

**White-paper default:** the loads named on the founders' seats (WP pp. 03, 21, 30 to 31); no list of them. The white paper's own habit of naming open items rather than hiding them (WP pp. 15, 22) is the closest thing to a default.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Founders who keep a written "things only I do" list usually keep it privately; making it a shared document among partners is less common and is the distinctive move here.

**Options:** (a) no new list; (b) one inventory per founder seat on the partners' page. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** an inventory that becomes a to-do list for a person; rows with no record that would show them moved; a by-default row with no date and no held decision, which is the thing the inventory exists to catch.

**A finished answer:** the choice; if an inventory, its fields and its home; the unassigned rows assigned or marked open; the chef seat's column named as pending 2.1.10.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 6.1.6 Decide what may carry a founder's load: a hand-over, the stack, a designation, or a person hired to the founder

**The question:** when a load has to leave a founder's seat, where may it go? In particular, may Sŏn ever hire someone to a founder (an assistant, a coordinator, a chief of staff)?

**Why it matters now:** 4.4.8 decided the delegation rule downward. This is the founder's own case, and it is a hiring question that could arise before the first lead if a founder's calendar gives way. The white paper's leadership count removes the back-office administrator by software (WP p. 22) and says nothing about a founder's assistant.

**Openers:**
- The book says almost everything on your plate is a growth opportunity for someone on your team (p. 487). Before the leads exist, who is the team?
- Sideways: the white paper says the operator used to be the integration layer and the stack absorbs that work (WP p. 21). Is a founder's calendar integration work, or something else?
- Picture the person who would be hired to you. What would they hold in their head that the record would then not hold?
- Counterexample: most hospitality groups have a founder's assistant by year two, and it works. What is different here, if anything?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Never, allowed as an ordinary seat, or case by case?
- If allowed: what may the seat never hold (decisions, check-ins, a lead's people, the record)?
- Does the seat have to pass the requisite-variety test (2.2.36) like any other?
- Is a contractor or a bought service (the stack's own vendors) a carrier under the same rule?

**What the book says:** delegate (p. 487); the delegation section at p. 284 covers the how.

**White-paper default:** node overload is fixed by restoring separation, not adding a person above (WP p. 14); growth is reach, not altitude (WP p. 09); the stack removes integration roles (WP p. 22). Silent on an assistant.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Executive assistants and chiefs of staff are ordinary in hospitality groups and in the book's own world. The argument against them here is not that they fail elsewhere; it is that the stack is meant to make them unnecessary, and a founder's inbox may or may not be what the stack was built for.

**Options:** (a) never; (b) an ordinary seat with a role description and no decision rights; (c) case by case at the reset. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** an assistant who becomes the person every hard call routes to; a "coordinator" who holds a lead's people; the decision being made from cost, which is Dominic's to weigh and not a source here.

**A finished answer:** the rule; if any role is allowed, what it may never hold and whose seat inventory it sits on.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 6.1.7 Decide the line between owning the operating system and running it

**The question:** Brandon's seat owns the operating systems (WP p. 03). Where does owning end and running begin, and what happens when a founder crosses the line?

**Why it matters now:** the leads arrive before opening and chair their own review (2.3.13), hold their own people's check-ins (2.2.5), and are read by the reset. If the owning founder also runs those things, the leads learn to wait for him, which is the load-bearing structure the white paper is written against (WP pp. 07 to 08). The same line exists for Dominic and the stack.

**Openers:**
- The white paper's warning is that systems get built for how the founder thinks (WP p. 17). What is the difference between owning a system and being its integration layer?
- Sideways: at Coqodaq (WP p. 22), roughly fourteen leadership positions ran one dinner service. How many of them were running things the owner should have owned and not run?
- If the leads' review happened this week and you were not in the room, what would you need to read afterward? If the answer is "I would need to be in the room," that is the line.
- Picture the first reset. The leads say the system worked. Did you run it or did they?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Owning = the record and its versions (2.1.18, 2.2's registers); running = the leads, the stack, the reset. Accept, amend, or refuse the line?
- Through dinner's first quarter: does the owning founder run named parts by name, each with a date on 4.4.18?
- What are the crossings 6.1.4's line reads? Candidates: chairing the leads' review; placing a check-in by hand; holding a lead's people directly.
- The same for Dominic's seat: configuring the stack versus operating it. And the chef seat, pending 2.1.10.

**What the book says:** protect the window when you do your best work; most urgent meetings exist because a short asynchronous task did not happen (pp. 487 to 488).

**White-paper default:** the Operations Lead "oversees those systems and is accountable to their outputs, not their manual production" (WP p. 10); the operator as integration layer is the work the stack absorbs (WP p. 21).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. The founder who cannot leave the floor of the first restaurant is the standard story of the second restaurant failing; Danny Meyer's account in *Setting the Table* is the best-known version.

**Options:** (a) the line as drawn, from the first hire; (b) a first-quarter exception with named parts and dates; (c) no line; the reset reads overload when it appears. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** choosing (a) and then describing the first quarter as when the founder "gets things right"; a crossing that is never written anywhere; Brandon describing how he personally works best, which is his document's.

**A finished answer:** the line, stated for each founder seat; the crossings listed; the first-quarter exception accepted or declined by name with dates.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 6.1.8 Decide the founders' floor rule: presence, range, and live speech during a service

**The question:** what may a founder do on the floor while a service runs?

**Why it matters now:** the room's designations (4.1.20, 4.1.21) and the two kinds of live speech (5.2.6) are built for leads and the team. A founder in the room during dinner's first quarter is the one person likely to see a mistake and able to fix it faster than the designated holder. Whether they may is the whole question, and the team learns the answer on the first night.

**Openers:**
- You ran opening service at a top room (WP p. 04). On the first night at Sŏn, a runner is about to drop a course at the wrong table and the room designation is across the floor. What do you do?
- Sideways: the white paper says designations "are not permanent elevations above anyone" (WP p. 10). Is a founder standing on the floor a designation, or an elevation?
- If a server defers a call to you because you are standing there, whose failure is it?
- Counterexample: a founder who is never on the floor never sees the room. What does the house lose, and can a record replace it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- No range unless named at the brief; a standing range through the first quarter; or off the floor except as a customer or under a designation?
- Live speech: does 5.2.6's rule bind a founder the same, more tightly, or not at all?
- A call deferred to a founder: a node-overload entry against the mechanism, never against the person?
- Anything a founder observes about a person during service: to the check-in holder only?
- The kitchen: the same rule for the chef partner, or theirs to set (2.1.10)?

**What the book says:** compartmentalize; one bad meeting need not derail the day; place transitions and energizing activity deliberately (pp. 488 to 489). Your biggest strength may be your biggest weakness (p. 489).

**White-paper default:** the Maitre d holds the floor and the team (WP p. 10); the server resolves in their range and the close carries it (WP p. 13); dinner runs alone for a quarter so the heart is solid (WP p. 36). 1.4.3 ruled whether a founder in the room runs on the room's tempo.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Chef-owners commonly work the pass and floor-owners commonly work the door for years; both are the load-bearing model the white paper diagnoses (WP pp. 07 to 08). Rooms that have moved the owner off the floor usually did it by naming who holds the room in their absence, which is what 4.1.21 has done.

**Options:** (a) no range unless named, corrects no one, carries observations to the check-in holder; (b) a standing range through the first quarter, ending at the first reset; (c) off the floor except as a customer or under a designation. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** (b) chosen without an end date; (a) chosen and then a founder "just helping" on a busy night; the rule written for Brandon and not for Dominic or the chef partner; anything about a founder's temperament on the floor, which is not recorded.

**A finished answer:** the rule; the deferred-call rule; the observation rule; the chef seat's version or its pending mark; 1.4.3's dinner-quarter consequence confirmed as consistent with it.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen's version.

### 6.1.10 Decide how each founder's learning tempo is set, and against what

### 6.1.11 Decide the founders' rule for the urgent: what is answered live at the center, and what waits

**The question:** what reaches a founder live, and what enters a channel with a window?

**Why it matters now:** the leads' first weeks set the habit. If a lead learns that a message to a founder gets an answer in ten minutes, that is the rule, whatever the page says. The channels exist (2.3.16, 2.2.30, 2.2.7, 4.6.3); this decides whether the founders are inside them.

**Openers:**
- The book: a report's request almost always feels urgent, and many urgent meetings exist because a short asynchronous task did not happen (p. 488). What is the Sŏn version of that meeting?
- Sideways: the content framework lets eighty percent of content move with no approval and reserves review for the five percent that carries risk (WP p. 31). Apply the same split to what reaches you.
- Name the last thing that reached you as urgent that could have waited a day. What channel should it have gone into?
- The important-but-not-urgent work of your seat (the record's versions, the principles' wording): does it have a closed hour on the calendar, or does it happen when nothing else is?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Nothing live but 2.2.7's list; a listed class a lead may bring live; or each founder's own rule in their document?
- Does every channel that receives the rest actually have a window and an owner? Which ones do not?
- The closed-hour home for the seat's slow work (2.3.31): confirmed, or missing?

**What the book says:** learn to disappoint people with your time; ask for a real deadline; make time for the important-but-not-urgent or it comes back to haunt you (pp. 488 to 490).

**White-paper default:** the floor waits on no one (WP p. 13); the feedback loop closes (WP p. 22); review is reserved for the small share that carries risk (WP p. 31).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Written response-time norms ("acknowledge within a day, close within a week") are common in distributed companies and rare in restaurants, where the phone is the norm. Sŏn's stack was built to change that (WP p. 21).

**Options:** (a) nothing live but emergencies; (b) a short listed class; (c) each founder's own rule, adopted by the house. Depth: cons. 10.

**Watch for:** a listed class that grows; a founder who answers live anyway and teaches the leads the real rule; the Eisenhower matrix adopted as a house instrument, which the earlier work declined.

**A finished answer:** the rule; the channels with missing windows sent back to their chunks; the closed-hour home confirmed on the cadence calendar.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
