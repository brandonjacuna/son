# 6.1 Manage your time and energy: considerations

Context for the working session. The book's Conclusion opens with the manager's own time and energy (pp. 485 to 490). White paper cited as WP p. N. Two rules govern this file. First, nothing here describes a founder: not Brandon's energy, hours, working style, or what he does outside the house, and not Dominic's or the chef partner's. Where the book asks a personal question, this file frames it and leaves it to the founder. Second, a founder's commitments outside the house are referred to only as that, "commitments outside the house," and reasoned about as an availability fact, never described.

## 1. What the white paper holds, and where it is silent

The white paper places the founders precisely. Two own everything outside the kitchen: one the concept, the operating systems, and the experience; the other the capital, the financial architecture, and the technology build (WP p. 03). The chef partner owns the culinary vision as an equal founding partner and culinary execution during service, "which is not the same as culinary direction overall" (WP pp. 03, 10). The founding posture is built to remove dependence on the founders: "a code-bearing organization built so it does not depend on either founder's memory" (WP p. 05); "the mark of these founders is a company built to run without them" (WP p. 06); "the business does not rest on the survival of any one person in it" (WP p. 37).

It sizes one founder load explicitly: the content engine's volume is achievable "without burning the founders out," because "the multiplication, not heroic effort, is what makes the volume real" (WP p. 30). Streaming is "hours with the founders directly" (WP p. 30). The content approval framework carries a founder consultation and a leadership review (WP p. 31). The acoustic work carries consultation "with a founder who holds an educational background in audio engineering" (WP p. 21), unnamed.

It names the trap in the founders themselves: "Restaurant founders build systems for themselves without realizing it, then blame employees when those systems do not work for anyone else. Sŏn has done it too, which is how the team knows the trap" (WP p. 17). And it names the operator as integration layer, holding the book, the numbers, the log, and the calendar "in one person's head, late and from memory," as the work the stack absorbs (WP p. 21).

Its health model is the frame for this chunk: bandwidth is the metabolic rate, "bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results," and cooling failure is running people at capacity "and calls it dedication until it becomes turnover" (WP pp. 12 to 13, 17). The leading indicators that read bandwidth (employee NPS, the cultural labor score, the customer recognition rate, WP pp. 12, 22) read the team. Nothing reads the founders. That gap is the shape of this chunk.

Silent on: any founder's hours, energy, presence by service period, boundaries, or what they do outside the house; who reads a founder's load; whether a founder may act on the floor during service; whether a founder ever has an assistant; the operating agreement, in any form.

## 2. Discipline or mechanism (6.1.2)

The book's answer is personal will: give yourself permission to set boundaries, because no one else will protect them for you (pp. 487 to 488). She names her own guideline (one late work night a week) and her own decision to treat exercise as part of the job.

The earlier work refused "founder operating discipline" as the name of anything Sŏn builds, on the ground that the white paper locates stability in the system, and a founder whose bandwidth depends on their own discipline is exactly the founder the house then depends on. That refusal is presented here as an option, not a ruling. Its case: the house already holds mechanisms that never need a founder at the wrong moment (the absence rule in the seat descriptions, post-service-only escalation, no founder as a default alert actor, the status quo on a reserved-class disagreement, hand-over dates), and a founder's own guideline is what remains after those hold.

Options:
- (a) The book's: each founder sets and guards their own boundaries, written only in their own working-with-me document (1.1.7, 1.1.8). Commits Sŏn to nothing in the house's design, and to a founder's protection lasting exactly as long as their will does.
- (b) The house guards a founder's attention by design, through mechanisms listed in 6.1.16, and the founder writes nothing. Commits Sŏn to building and testing the mechanisms (6.1.19), and leaves the founder's own energy unexamined by anyone, including the founder.
- (c) Both layers, kept apart: the house's layer public to the partners and the team (6.1.16); the founder's layer private (6.1.13). Commits Sŏn to maintaining two lists and to never letting the private one become a house record.

Where Sŏn may diverge: the book has no house layer, because a manager at a large company has a manager and a company that survives her at zero. The founders have neither, so the house layer carries weight the book never asks of it.

## 3. The read between resets (6.1.4)

The book's instrument is the calendar mark and the monthly review of what stalled (pp. 486 to 487), read by the manager alone, with a manager above who can be asked for help (p. 490). At Sŏn, 4.2.12 already decides whether each founder's seat gets a load line at the reset, what its sources may be, who writes it, and where a lead's "what's in my way" lands when their check-in holder is a founder. This decision is about the months between resets, and it starts from the position that nothing reads the center (WP pp. 12, 22).

From the earlier work, worth keeping: the read must read records, never a founder's feelings; it must be held by no one standing above, because no one does; and it must exist before opening, because the loads are heaviest then. Its candidate sources are all records the house already keeps: a knowledge-transfer item past its date (1.1.12); a handover past its week (4.4.18); a cross-domain ask open past two reviews (2.3.6); a live risk's daily state missed (4.1.31); a call deferred to a founder on the floor (6.1.8); the content engine's output-per-session ratio falling below the white paper's own sizing (WP p. 30). Its refusals are as important as its sources: never a rating, a case file, a performance record, a read of feelings or health or outside commitments, an input to pay, a trigger under the operating agreement, a lead's impression of a founder, a survey, or a 360.

Options:
- (a) Nothing between resets. The reset line (4.2.12) is the whole read. Commits Sŏn to a quarter or a year between reads during the period the loads are heaviest.
- (b) A monthly load line written by the other partner, from records only, inside the domain read on the partners' page (2.3.4). Commits Sŏn to each partner reading the other's records monthly and writing one line that concludes only a load and a carrier. When a third partner exists, the line rotates so no partner is another's standing reader.
- (c) Each founder writes their own monthly line ("what is in my way") in the same form the leads write upward (4.2.12, 5.4.13). Commits Sŏn to the founder's own account as the read, which the book would endorse (p. 490, saying it aloud) and which the earlier work distrusted as the founder reading themselves.

The book's "ask for help" has a Sŏn form already: the cross-domain ask with an owner and a date on the partners' page (2.3.4). The consideration for Brandon is whether an ask is enough when the overload is in the asking founder's own domain and there is no one to hand it to yet.

## 4. The inventory (6.1.5)

The book keeps a list of priorities and reviews what stalled monthly (pp. 486 to 487). The earlier work built a "founder load ledger": every function placed on a founder's seat, with the seat, whether held by design or by default, its hand-over date or closing condition, and what reads it at the reset. 1.1's mapping dropped the ledger row from the knowledge-transfer list as machinery. Since then the house has grown three lists that between them hold most of what the ledger held: the knowledge-transfer list (1.1.12, what only a founder knows), the workstream owners (2.3.5, what a founder runs before opening), and the handover list (4.4.18, what passes to each lead and when). The alert register (2.2.33) shows whether a founder is anyone's default actor.

What none of those hold: loads the white paper places on a seat without a hand-over in view. The content engine's cadence and streaming (WP p. 30); the founder consultation and the leadership review in the content framework (WP p. 31); the acoustic consultation (WP p. 21), whose founder is unnamed; the chef partner search (3.1.19); the capital raise, which is Dominic's domain and whose closing date is not this program's to set, with only its public-voice component on Brandon's seat. None of these is a to-do; each is a standing load.

Options:
- (a) No new list. The four existing lists are read together at the reset, and the unlisted loads above are added to whichever list fits. Commits Sŏn to four partial views and to someone assembling them at the reset.
- (b) One inventory per founder seat on the partners' page (6.1.17), each row: what is held, by design or by default, until when or on what condition, who it passes to, and the record that will show it moved. Commits Sŏn to a fifth list, and to the discipline that every by-default row carries a date or a held decision. The three marks from the earlier work are kept in plain words: by design (the seat is the closest competent owner), by default (no one else exists yet, and there is a moment after which continued holding is overload), and record-native (the white paper places it there directly).

The chef seat's column depends on 2.1.10: as a founder, the chef's rows read like the other two; as a lead's seat, like any lead's. Either way the kitchen's loads belong on the same list with the same marks. The earlier work also treated three seat assignments (the hiring role as Brandon's seat, construction and the Operations Lead's pairing as Dominic's, interim authority over the record's versions) as the team's suggestion rather than fact; 3.1.4, 2.3.5, 2.3.14, and 2.1.18 have since decided or will decide them, and the inventory follows those decisions.

## 5. What may carry a load (6.1.6)

The book's tactic is delegation to the team (p. 487). The earlier work added a refusal: a load may leave a founder's seat only by hand-over to a lead or seat, to the stack, to a designation, by contract, or to a new seat added through the structure-change process, and never to a chief of staff, a founder's assistant, a founder's office, or a second holder of the same domain. Each of those was treated as a failure mode: a person hired to a founder becomes the integration layer the stack was meant to absorb (WP p. 21), the web acquires altitude (WP p. 09), and node overload is masked rather than fixed (WP p. 14).

The white paper's own leadership count is the evidence for the refusal: roughly eight leadership lines across the whole day against fourteen around one dinner service, with the back-office administrator "removed entirely" by software (WP p. 22). It is silent on an assistant to a founder.

Options:
- (a) The refusal as written. Commits Sŏn to founders with no personal staff, and to the stack and the leads carrying everything a founder hands off.
- (b) An assistant or coordinator is allowed as an ordinary seat on the seat inventory (4.1.15), with a role description in 4.1's form, no decision rights, no check-ins held, and no access to the record beyond what any seat has. Commits Sŏn to a seat that must survive the requisite-variety test (2.2.34) like any other, and to saying plainly what it is for.
- (c) Decided case by case at the reset, when a load has no other carrier. Commits Sŏn to the question recurring.

How others have handled it: General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Founders of hospitality groups commonly acquire an executive assistant early and a chief of staff later; the roles are ordinary in the industry and the book itself was written by an executive who ran a large office. The white paper's argument is not that such roles are wrong elsewhere; it is that the stack is built to make them unnecessary here. Whether that holds for a founder's calendar and inbox, as distinct from the restaurant's integration work, is the real question.

## 6. Owning and running (6.1.7)

The book protects the productive window and refuses the meetings that exist because a short asynchronous task did not happen (pp. 487 to 488). At Sŏn the sharper form of that question is structural: Brandon's seat owns the operating systems (WP p. 03), and the white paper's own warning is that systems get built for how the founder thinks (WP p. 17). The earlier work drew a line between owning and running: owning is the record and its versions (2.1.18, the registers of 2.2); running is the leads chairing their own review (2.3.13), the stack placing every check-in and review, the room-holding designations holding the room, and the reset correcting. A founder who chairs the leads' review, places a check-in by hand, or holds a lead's people directly has crossed the line, and the read in 6.1.4 should see the crossing.

The same line exists for Dominic's seat: configuring the stack is owning; operating it daily is running, and the Operations Lead "oversees those systems and is accountable to their outputs" (WP p. 10). For the chef seat, culinary direction is owning and execution during service is a designation (WP p. 10); 2.1.10 decides the rest.

Options:
- (a) The line as drawn, from the first hire. Commits Sŏn to the leads running the system from their first weeks, and to the founders reading records rather than attending to learn what happened.
- (b) Through dinner's first quarter, the owning founder also runs named parts, each named on the handover list with a date (4.4.18). Commits Sŏn to a stated exception with an end, and to the risk 1.4.3 named: a house that runs its first quarter on a founder's eye builds an architecture it will have to demolish while open.
- (c) No line drawn; the reset reads overload when it appears. Commits Sŏn to finding out after the fact.

## 7. The floor rule (6.1.8)

The book's tactic is compartmentalizing: do not let one bad meeting derail the day; place transitions and energizing activity deliberately (pp. 488 to 489). At Sŏn the founders' equivalent of the derailing meeting is a service, and the question is what a founder may do while one runs. The white paper gives the floor to the Maitre d, marks accountability in the moment through designations that "are not permanent elevations above anyone" (WP p. 10), lets the server resolve within their own range (WP p. 13), and runs dinner alone for a quarter "so the heart is solid" (WP p. 36). 1.4.3 ruled whether a founder in the room runs on the room's tempo; 4.1.19 and 4.1.20 built the designations; 5.2.6 set the two kinds of live-service speech; 4.5.8 set the rule for correcting a person in front of others.

From the earlier work: a founder on the floor is bound by the same live-service rules as anyone, and more tightly than a lead, because a founder who has run opening service before will want to intervene faster than the room is ready to let them, and the team must learn to hold the room from its own designations, not from a founder's eye. A call deferred to a founder on the floor is a node-overload entry against the mechanism that let the call reach them, never a mark on the person who deferred it.

Options:
- (a) A founder holds no floor range unless named at that service's brief for a designation; makes a live call only inside a range they hold; corrects no one and sends no message about a person during service; carries any observation about a person only to that person's check-in holder. Commits Sŏn to founders who watch a mistake happen and write it to the close.
- (b) A founder holds a standing floor range through dinner's first quarter, by name, ending at the first reset. Commits Sŏn to a stated exception and to the team learning the founder's eye before its own designations.
- (c) The founders are off the floor during service except as customers or under a designation. Commits Sŏn to the cleanest rule and to founders who see the room only from a table.

How others have handled it: General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Danny Meyer's account in *Setting the Table* of opening a second restaurant while the first still leaned on his presence is the standard cautionary story in the industry; the pattern he describes, the founder as the person every hard call routes to, is the load-bearing structure the white paper is written against (WP pp. 07 to 08).

## 8. Commitments outside the house (6.1.9)

The book's own example is her evenings at home, and her guideline follows from it (p. 486). This manual does not do that for any founder. What it can do is treat a founder's presence as a fact the house schedules against, in the same form as any seat's shift shape, and refuse to know anything more.

From the earlier work: each founder states presence by service period and by week; the house reads it as availability and never as a commitment; no mechanism may depend on presence beyond the statement; the read in 6.1.4 reads only consequences on records (a review moved, a live-risk state missed), never the commitment itself; the house must be able to run every service and a full week of the cadence with every founder unreachable (6.1.19). The same treatment extends to Dominic and the chef partner.

Options:
- (a) The statement, in the working-with-me document's presence prompt if 1.1.2 kept it, or as a scheduling entry (6.1.18). Commits Sŏn to scheduling the why session, the founder interview stage, and any founder-attended block from it, and to never recording why.
- (b) Nothing stated; the partners' weekly review carries availability week by week. Commits Sŏn to the schedule depending on a weekly conversation, and to no record the scheduling surface can read.
- (c) The operating agreement governs presence and nothing else is written. Commits Sŏn to a legal instrument doing scheduling work, which 2.1.1 should check it was written to do.

Counsel: whether a founder's outside commitments raise any duty under the operating agreement or in law is a question for 6.3.7, not for this manual.

## 9. The learning tempo (6.1.10)

The book sorts stalled tasks into the ones you should have handed off and the ones that do not fit your schedule (pp. 486 to 487), and tells the manager to ask for help early (p. 490). 1.4.3 handed the founders' own learning tempo here. The white paper's learning system is self-paced and unlocks on a period, for the team (WP pp. 11, 19); interviewers are trained because reading an answer is a learned skill (WP p. 18); and the first-time team is "the objection to answer, not avoid" (WP p. 05).

The founders' training is already dated by what it gates: the interviewer sequence before the first lead's first interview (3.2.24), the coaching module before the first coaching check-in (5.1.17), the review module before the first review (5.4.25), the performance module before any check-in that could open a window (5.8.24), all paced by the hiring calendar (3.2.32). The earlier work's position was that the gates and the cadence set a founder's tempo and no person does. What the gates do not set is anything a founder wants to learn that no act demands.

Options:
- (a) The gates set it and nothing more is scheduled. Commits Sŏn to a founder's learning being exactly what the house requires and no more, as far as the house knows.
- (b) Each founder keeps a dated "what I don't know yet" list (1.1.2's prompt, if kept), revisited each quarter and at the reset, read by the other partner. Commits Sŏn to the founder's own stated agenda being visible to one other person, and to the domain read (2.3.4) being allowed to ask about it.
- (c) A coach or outside peer (6.2.10) sets a rhythm with the founder. Commits Sŏn to a relationship outside the house doing work the partners do not.

## 10. The urgent (6.1.11)

The book's tactics: learn to disappoint people with your time; ask for a real deadline; notice how many urgent meetings exist because a short asynchronous task did not happen; make time for the important-but-not-urgent (pp. 488 to 490). At Sŏn several mechanisms already do this work without asking a founder to hold a line: the server resolves within their range so the floor waits on no one (WP p. 13); the feedback channel closes its loop on a window (2.3.16); alerts have a named actor and a founder is not the default (2.2.30); emergency authority during a service is named (2.2.7); the partners owe each other a response time on a written question (4.6.3); and no build meeting happens during service hours (2.3.2).

What remains is the founders' own rule for what reaches them live. The important-but-not-urgent work of each seat (the record's versions, the mission and principles wording under 2.1.18, the existential-risk register's contents under 2.2.26) has a natural home in the closed hours of the cadence calendar (2.3.31); the consideration is whether it has one in fact.

Options:
- (a) Nothing reaches a founder live except what 2.2.7 names; everything else enters a channel with a window. Commits Sŏn to founders who are hard to reach by design, and to every channel actually having a window and an owner.
- (b) A lead may reach a founder live for a listed class of things. Commits Sŏn to keeping the list short and to reading how often it is used.
- (c) Each founder sets their own reachability in their working-with-me document and the house adopts it. Commits Sŏn to two different rules for two seats, which the leads must learn.

The book's Eisenhower matrix has no house form; the earlier work refused it in favor of one goal per unit (2.2.17) and one thing each partner carries into the week (2.3.4). That is a fair translation, and the matrix remains available to any founder privately.

## 11. Additions to the working-with-me document (6.1.12)

1.1.2 chose the document's prompts, with a yes or no on each of the earlier work's additions (the domain's edges, what lives only in the founder's head, how the founder decides, load signals, dependencies on the other partners, what the founder does not know yet). This chunk adds candidates from the book's own tactics, each stated as a prompt and never as content:

- Presence by service period and by week (6.1.9), the only candidate the house schedules against.
- The protected window: when the founder does their best work, so the partners' calendar avoids it (p. 488).
- Reachability during build hours (6.1.11).
- What the founder looks like under load, from the outside, and what they would want the other partner to do about it (p. 490; the earlier work's prompt H). This is the founder's half of the read in 6.1.4, compared at the reset with the record's half, neither overriding the other.
- The learning list (6.1.10; the earlier work's prompt K).
- The protection order as a private appendix (6.1.13), of which only the presence fact leaves the document.
- The strength-and-its-cost line (p. 489; pp. 48 to 50 in Chapter 1): a strength the house depends on in the founder personally is a knowledge-transfer item with a date (1.1.12).

The earlier work also fixed a timing rule: each founder's issued version reaches a lead before that lead's first check-in with that partner. That is 1.1.5's and 3.3.24's, and is only noted here.

## 14. Where Sŏn may diverge from the book

- The book assumes a manager above and a company that survives the manager at zero. The founders have neither, so the house builds a layer the book never asks for (6.1.2, 6.1.16) and tests it (6.1.19).
- The book's remedies are acts of will (boundaries, delegation, compartmentalizing). Sŏn's are mechanisms first, and the founder's will is what remains. That is the white paper's own logic applied to its authors (WP p. 17).
- The book's "delegate" is a manager's act. At Sŏn delegation lives on a register and a handover list with dates; a founder does not delegate in the moment, and may refuse to have anyone to delegate to (6.1.6).
- The book forgives mistakes because a growing company forgets them. Sŏn's record does not forget; it versions (WP p. 13). What that does to a founder is 6.3.5's question.
- The book has the manager read themselves. Sŏn's read of a founder, if any, is written by the other partner from records, and the founder's self-read sits beside it without either winning (6.1.4, 6.1.12).
