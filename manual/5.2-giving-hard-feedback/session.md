# 5.2 Giving hard feedback: working session

## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** How a hard thing is said to a person at Sŏn: the words, the room, the timing, and whether anything is written down. The book gives the words (pp. 391 to 395). Sŏn has to decide the rest, and one of its decisions, whether a lead ever writes a record of the conversation, is where the house's "no one writes about a person but the person" leaning meets employment law. That is why counsel is asked before the record decision, not after.

**Already decided upstream.** The shape and entry point of a hypothesis (5.1.5, 5.1.7); the early check-in (5.1.8); the rule for correcting a person in front of others in a meeting (4.5.8); the unblocking path for two people (4.5.10); the destination for each kind of thing a person needs to say, including a serious violation (4.5.11); where an unmet plan goal goes (3.3.12); a minor recurring issue in the first weeks (3.4.5); the check-in's refusals (2.3.9); the person page (2.3.11); what a lead may write about a person (4.4.2).

**Who else must agree.** Both seated founders on the form, the timing, the record, and the failure types. The chef partner on the kitchen's version of live-service speech and failure types. Counsel on the breach list, the record, and retention.

**Why it sits here.** 5.1 produces the theory; this chunk is what happens when the theory is a gap. 5.3 makes the ordinary flow of feedback strong enough that these conversations are rare and unsurprising. 5.8 and 5.10 begin where this chunk's rules say coaching has stopped working.

## 3. Briefs

### 5.2.2 Decide the boundary between hard feedback and a serious breach that is never handled as coaching

**The question:** which acts are never a coaching conversation, where does a report of one go, and what is the one conversation the house allows with a third person in the room?

**Why it matters now:** the conduct policy has to exist before the first hire, and 4.5.11 named a destination for "a report of a serious violation" without defining one. "Blame the process" (WP p. 17) has no stated exception; this writes it.

**Openers:**
- Name the things that, if you saw them on the floor tonight, you would not be having a check-in about. Say them plainly. That is the list.
- Sideways: a cook shows up impaired once. Process or person? Now a server pockets a cash tip meant for the pool. Now a runner shoves a colleague in the pass. Where does each sit, and does the list change with the second time?
- The earlier work described one three-person conversation: the person's lead, a second lead as witness, off the floor, on the clock, in the person's language. Is a witness protection for the person or for the house? Both?

**Narrowing questions:**
- A closed list, counsel's standard list, or a list plus a catch-all?
- Who is told, how fast, and where does the report live?
- Does what follows belong wholly to 5.10?

**What the book says:** when self-awareness does not improve a change may be needed (p. 395); the book does not treat misconduct in this section.

**White-paper default:** the first question is what allowed this, not who failed (WP p. 17). No exception named.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Hospitality employee handbooks routinely list conduct that leads to immediate separation; counsel will have a Texas-standard version. "Just culture" frameworks reserve individual consequence for reckless conduct and coach the rest (cons. 8).

**Options:** (a) a short closed list, everything else coached; (b) counsel's standard list as is; (c) a list plus a catch-all. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** a catch-all that swallows the coaching system; a list so short that a lead has no ground to stand on when something ugly happens.

**A finished answer:** the list in Brandon's words, the destination and speed, the witness conversation accepted or changed, and the questions for counsel written.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; counsel reviews.

### 5.2.5 Decide the form of the hard conversation: the two openings, the cycle, the last resort, and what it never concludes with

**The question:** what does a lead say first, what do they say when the person does not see it, how many rounds, and what sentence may they never end on?

**Why it matters now:** the guide (5.2.12) and the module (5.1.15) teach this verbatim. The founders will use it with the leads before any lead uses it with anyone.

**Openers:**
- Think of the last hard conversation you had with someone who worked for you. What was your first sentence? Was it a question, or was it the news?
- The book's bad opener is "do you think that meeting could have been better?" (p. 393). What is the floor's version of that sentence, the one that tells the person you have already decided?
- Sideways: a lead says "you've gotten sloppy on the sequence lately." What record could the person open to check that? Now: "on the fourteenth and the twenty-first the close shows the sequence broke at table nine; both nights the kitchen refired." Which sentence can the person answer?
- The person denies it twice and you have the records. The book says state your impression plainly with an example (pp. 394 to 395). What is the sentence after that one? That is the sentence the never-conclude list is about.

**Narrowing questions:**
- The earlier work's form, the book's without a list, or the form plus a consequence on a second raising?
- The never-conclude list: a verdict, a label, a prediction, pay, a count. Add or remove?
- The person's proposal before the lead's, always?

**What the book says:** neutral open question; if denied, a specific empathetic observation owned as perception; cycle; plain statement as last resort; shared account before solutions; people are more self-aware than you expect (pp. 392 to 395).

**White-paper default:** develop rather than judge; no surprises (WP p. 19). No form.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Situation-Behavior-Impact is the standard shape for the observation; *Radical Candor* and *Crucial Conversations* both teach starting from shared facts before meaning.

**Options:** (a) the earlier work's form and list; (b) the book's form, no list; (c) the form, with a consequence allowed on a second raising. Depth: cons. 1.

**Watch for:** a form so scripted it reads as HR; a "last resort" that becomes the first move on a busy night.

**A finished answer:** the two openings in house words, the cycle and last resort, the never-conclude list, and the sequence rule.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.2.6 Decide the two kinds of live-service speech, and what a repeated instruction means

**The question:** what may anyone say to anyone during service, and what does it mean when the same instruction has to be given to the same person twice in one night?

**Why it matters now:** the shift brief (2.3.7) and the designation cards (4.1.23) are being written; they need to say what a designation holder may say. 4.5.8 decided the meeting version; this is the floor version.

**Openers:**
- "Fire the four-top." "Watch your tone with the four-top." Say both in the pass at 8:40. What is different about them, grammatically? That difference is the rule.
- Sideways: the best expediter you ever worked under. How many words did they use per call, and did any of them ever have a person as the subject?
- You have told the same runner "twelve first, then nine" three times tonight. What is wrong: the runner, the sequence card, or the training plan? What do you do at 8:41, and what do you do at the close?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Instruction versus feedback as written, or with one allowed in-the-moment sentence for safety?
- Any designation holder may instruct within their task?
- A repeat is logged at the close as a process or readiness signal, never a correction in the moment?
- The kitchen's version: same rule, pending the chef partner?

**What the book says:** praise publicly, criticize privately (p. 396); specific and objective over emotional and generic (p. 394).

**White-paper default:** designations mark accountability in the moment (WP p. 10); management absorbs blame; post-mortems private (WP p. 17); the customer never feels at fault (WP p. 26).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Kitchen call-and-response ("heard," "behind," "hands") is the oldest form of the operating instruction, and disciplined rooms keep anything about a cook for after service.

**Options:** (a) the distinction as written; (b) plus one allowed quiet sentence for safety or a customer-facing standard; (c) the book's meeting rule applied to the floor. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** an instruction delivered in a tone that makes it feedback; a rule that leaves a lead unable to stop something unsafe.

**A finished answer:** the distinction in house words, who may instruct, the repeat rule, and the kitchen's version marked pending.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen.

### 5.2.7 Decide where and when hard feedback is said: only at the check-in, or also in person between check-ins

**The question:** if something needs saying on Saturday and the check-in is in three weeks, may the lead say it privately on Sunday and write nothing, or must it wait for a check-in, early-placed?

**Why it matters now:** the earlier work left this unresolved on purpose. Both halves of the module depend on it, and 5.1.8's early check-in only makes sense as the release valve for one of the answers.

**Openers:**
- Saturday something happened. Sunday you see the person in the walk-in. What do you do? Now imagine you are the person: which would you rather, the walk-in on Sunday or a placed check-in on Wednesday?
- Sideways: everything said in the walk-in lives only in the lead's memory. The person cannot read it back. Is that a small cost, or the private notebook the whole design refuses?
- The argument against check-in-only: candor becomes a scheduled event and the placement itself feels like a summons. Does that match your experience of being called into an office?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Check-in only, in person between allowed, or in person for time-bound things only?
- The brief private heads-up: always allowed?
- Never the floor, never a group, on the clock, in the person's language, interpreter per 4.1.23 and 4.8?

**What the book says:** managers wait too long (p. 382); the conversation needs privacy and time (pp. 392 to 395).

**White-paper default:** post-mortems are private and process-focused (WP p. 17). No timing.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most feedback guidance says soon and private; few employers constrain a manager to a scheduled slot. Sŏn's check-in-only leaning would be unusual, and its reason (readability by the person) is the thing to weigh.

**Options:** (a) check-in only with the early check-in; (b) in person between, said once, the person's line the only record; (c) split by time-boundness. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** choosing (a) without 5.1.8 allowing an early check-in; choosing (b) and finding leads "mentioned it" weekly with nothing ever reaching the page.

**A finished answer:** one of the three, the heads-up rule, and the four constants confirmed.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.2.8 Decide whether a hard conversation leaves a written record, and whose words it holds

**The question:** after a hard conversation, does anything exist on paper beyond the person's own check-in line, and if so who wrote it?

**Why it matters now:** 5.8 and 5.10 need to know what they inherit, and counsel (5.2.4) has to have answered first. The workbook's four letters are the industry's default; the earlier work refused them.

**Openers:**
- Read the workbook's recap letter (pp. 113 to 116). Whose voice is it in? If you received it, would you feel heard or filed?
- Sideways: imagine the person's own line is the only record and, a year later, the same matter is on its fourth raising and the house needs to act. What does the file look like? Is that enough? Counsel says what?
- A jointly written recap, both sign. Is that partnership, or a signature under duress?

**Narrowing questions:**
- None, the person's line, the lead's recap read back, a joint recap, or a record only from the second raising?
- Who reads it, and for how long is it kept?
- The three follow-on letters: here, 5.8's, or nowhere?

**What the book says:** the workbook supplies the recap and follow-on letters; the chapter itself says nothing about a record.

**White-paper default:** no surprises (WP p. 19); the person page holds the person's words only (2.3.11).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most US employers keep a manager-written note of performance conversations because counsel asks them to. That is the practice Sŏn would be departing from, and counsel's written answer is the ground for departing or not.

**Options:** (a) none; (b) the lead's recap, read back; (c) a joint recap; (d) nothing until the second raising, then 5.8's. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** deciding this on principle before counsel answers; a "read back" that is a signature rather than an agreement.

**A finished answer:** one of the four, readers and retention, and the follow-on letters placed; or Brandon's leaning recorded as pending counsel.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; counsel's answer in hand.

### 5.2.9 Decide the failure types and the response to each, including a judgment call inside someone's range that went wrong

**The question:** when something goes wrong, how does the house sort it, and which sort ever reaches a person as a conversation?

**Why it matters now:** the shift close (2.3.8) is capturing incidents from the first training service; whoever reviews them needs the sort. And the decision-rights range (3.3.17) promises trust in judgment; this is where that promise is kept or broken.

**Openers:**
- A server comps a dessert to a table that was, in hindsight, fine. Inside their range. What do they hear about it? The white paper's answer is nothing, comps are a tool to be used (WP p. 26). Does that hold when it is the third one this week?
- Sideways: the sequence broke at nine, the runner was new, the printer jammed, the host double-sat. Whose failure? The earlier work's answer is nobody's, it is a complex failure and gets a process fix. Does that feel right or evasive?
- A known step was skipped. First time: the process gets a placement fix. Same role, same step, after the fix: now it is a conversation. Is the second-time rule right, or too slow?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Three types, two, or the just-culture three?
- Which reach the person, and only after what?
- A judgment call inside the range is never corrected as an error?
- Counted by type per period, never by person?

**What the book says:** a delivery issue is raised as a specific observation with an offer of help (pp. 393 to 394).

**White-paper default:** the server resolves within their range, the close captures it, the lead reviews, the process is updated (WP p. 13); identify the failure mode before intervening at the people level (WP p. 14); what allowed this, not who failed (WP p. 17); comps are a tool, not damage control (WP p. 26). Stated outright.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Edmondson's basic, complex, and intelligent failure types; just culture's error, at-risk, reckless; blameless post-mortems in software operations (cons. 8).

**Options:** (a) the three types with the recurrence rule; (b) two types; (c) just culture's three. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** "intelligent failure" becoming a shield for a pattern of bad calls; "complex" becoming the sort everything lands in.

**A finished answer:** the types in house words, the response to each, the recurrence rule, the counting rule, and the kitchen's version marked pending.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen.

### 5.2.10 Decide how management absorbs blame in practice: who owns the fix, how a reporter is protected, and what a lead says in a group

**The question:** the white paper says management absorbs blame from staff (WP p. 17). What does a lead do on Tuesday that makes that true?

**Why it matters now:** the first close records and the first brief are being written; if a capture can name a person as cause, the person page has a back door on day one.

**Openers:**
- A runner writes in the close "I dropped the sequence at nine." Tomorrow's brief. What does the room hear? Who owns the fix?
- Sideways: a manager stands in pre-shift and says "last night was on me." Is that absorbing blame, or performing it? What does the room actually learn?
- A founder's own domain fails (1.2.3). Same rules?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The reviewing seat owns the fix, always?
- A capture naming a person is rewritten before it is typed?
- In a group: the process and the fix, never a name, never a performed apology?
- One-line public owner statement at the close, or not?

**What the book says:** team feedback can be public and data-driven; individual feedback private (pp. 396 to 397).

**White-paper default:** management absorbs blame; post-mortems private (WP p. 17); the customer never feels at fault (WP p. 26); crisis communication is transparency about what happened, the impact, and the fix (WP p. 31). Stated outright.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Blameless post-mortem write-ups name an owner for each action item and no cause-person; that is the model.

**Options:** (a) the three rules; (b) the three rules plus a one-line owner statement at the close. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** absorption becoming a lead's silent accumulation of fault with no fix; the rewrite rule erasing what actually happened.

**A finished answer:** the three rules accepted or changed, the group sentence, and the founder case by reference to 1.2.3.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.2.11 Decide how a failed readiness row and a retraining requirement are delivered

**The question:** how does a lead tell someone they are not ready, and how does a lead tell an interviewer they cannot interview again until retrained, without either becoming a verdict?

**Why it matters now:** the first readiness reads run in training services before dinner opens (3.3.51); some rows will fail. The interviewer rule (3.2.14) is already set.

**Openers:**
- A row fails. You sit with the person and the form. What is your first sentence? The earlier work's answer is that you read the assessor's words and ask what happened before you say anything of your own. What would you want to add, and why?
- Sideways: you were once told you were not ready for something. What did the person say that made it survivable, or not?
- An interviewer's scorecard came back with no read. Is that about them, or about the form? Which do you say first?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Row by row, assessor's words, the lead adds nothing, ask first, the plan's next step follows?
- Retraining as a record fact at the person's own check-in, form question first?
- The hiring-decision contradiction stays with 3.2.10 and 3.2.41; two-person disagreements stay with 4.5.10?

**What the book says:** specific, objective, an offer of help (pp. 393 to 394).

**White-paper default:** no one touches a table until ready; validation is a system fact (WP p. 19).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Competency sign-off systems in healthcare training deliver a failed item from the checklist, not from the assessor's opinion, for the same reason.

**Options:** (a) the earlier work's forms as written; (b) the same, with the lead permitted one sentence of their own read after the person has spoken. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** the lead's "one sentence" becoming the verdict the form was built to avoid.

**A finished answer:** both forms in house words, and the two referrals confirmed.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
