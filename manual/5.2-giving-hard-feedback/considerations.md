# 5.2 Giving hard feedback: considerations

What the white paper holds, what the old work argued that is worth keeping, the options, and where Sŏn may diverge from the book. Topics follow the book's order (pp. 391 to 395), with the Sŏn-specific questions the book leaves to the manager at the end. "WP p. N" cites the September 2026 white paper; "cons. N" cites a section here.

## 1. The conversation's form: explorer, not lecturer

**White paper.** Performance systems "develop people rather than judge them" (WP p. 19). No surprises (WP p. 19). Silent on how a hard thing is said to a person.

**The book.** Open with a neutral question about a specific recent event, never one that presupposes the answer. If the person acknowledges, move to root causes and solutions together. If they deny, offer a specific, empathetic observation owned as your perception ("holding up a mirror"). Cycle a few times. As a last resort, state your own impression plainly with an example. Agree on the problem before anyone offers a solution; a change the person names is the one that sticks (pp. 392 to 395).

**From the earlier work.** Kept as a way of talking, not a form. The first opening is about a specific service ("how did Saturday go for you"). The second is a spoken situation-behavior-impact observation citing dated records ("on these two dates the close shows X; both times Y followed"), never a trend statement ("you've gotten worse"), because a trend is unreadable from any record. The last resort is kept; the verdict that would naturally follow it is refused. The never-conclude list: a verdict, a label, a prediction, pay, any count.

**Options for 5.2.5.** (a) The earlier work's form with the never-conclude list as written. (b) The book's form without a fixed list, trusting the module and the guide. (c) The earlier work's form but allowing the lead to state a consequence when the same thing is on its second raising, which is where 5.8 begins. Each commits the module (5.1.15) to teaching it with house examples.

## 2. Two kinds of speech during service

**White paper.** Designations "mark accountability in the moment" (WP p. 10). Management absorbs blame from staff; post-mortems are private (WP p. 17). The customer never feels at fault (WP p. 26). Silent on what a lead may say to a person during service.

**The book.** Praise publicly, criticize privately (p. 396). Specific and objective beats emotional and generic (p. 394).

**From the earlier work.** An operating instruction has the work as its subject and a present action as its verb ("fire the four-top," "reset twelve"), and is complete the moment it happens. Feedback about a person has the person as subject and a pattern as predicate, and asks for a change in the person. The first is native to service, and anyone holding a designation may give one inside their task. The second is never said in front of staff or customers during service. An instruction repeated to the same person on the same task in one service is a process or readiness signal, logged at the close, not a cue to correct the person.

**Where Sŏn may diverge from the book.** The book allows a norm violation to be named in the moment (4.5.8 decides that for meetings). The earlier work refused any in-the-moment remark about a person's manner or style during service, on the ground that the floor is the one place a correction is always public. 4.5.8 and 5.2.6 should agree.

**Options for 5.2.6.** (a) As above. (b) As above, with one allowed in-the-moment sentence for safety or a customer-facing standard, said quietly and followed at the check-in. (c) The book's rule, naming the norm in the moment and the pattern privately, applied to the floor as to meetings.

## 3. Where and when: the unresolved conflict

**White paper.** Post-mortems are private and process-focused (WP p. 17). Silent on timing.

**The book.** Managers wait too long (p. 382). The conversation needs privacy and time (pp. 392 to 395).

**From the earlier work.** Two positions were never reconciled. One allowed a hard observation to be said privately in person between check-ins and written nowhere until the next check-in, because forbidding it makes candor a scheduled, ambush-feeling event. The other reserved every hard observation for the check-in (early-placed if needed), because anything said and not recorded lives only in the lead's memory, unreadable by the person, which recreates the private notebook the design refuses. The earlier work leaned to the narrower reading but flagged it as Brandon's to decide. Both agreed on: never the floor, never a group, on the clock, in the person's language, and a brief private heads-up that a conversation is coming is always allowed.

**Options for 5.2.7.** (a) Check-in only, with the early check-in (5.1.8) as the release valve. Commits Sŏn to 5.1.8 allowing the early check-in, and to leads sitting on things for days. (b) In person between check-ins, privately, said once, and the person's own line at the next check-in is the only record. Commits Sŏn to trusting leads with unrecorded conversations. (c) In person between check-ins for anything time-bound (a safety practice, a customer-facing standard), check-in for everything else.

## 4. The record

**White paper.** No surprises (WP p. 19). The person page is written only at the check-in, in the person's words, read back (2.3.11). Silent on records of conversations.

**The workbook** (pp. 113 to 116) supplies a recap letter after a hard conversation (situation, observable behaviors, impact) and three follow-on letters: progress, back on track, insufficient progress. These are the paper trail most employers keep.

**From the earlier work.** No written recap in any form. The person's own line, as long as they choose, is the only record. Reasoning: a lead's summary is the lead's voice about a person, which conflicts with the person being the only author of their record, and it becomes a narrative outside the person's control. The three follow-on letters were handed to a later chunk under the constraint that any record still holds only the person's words. The earlier work also flagged the tension it could not resolve: formal processes (5.8, 5.10) may need a trail, and counsel has not said what Texas practice needs.

**Why counsel comes first (5.2.4).** Whether a record in the person's own words can serve where a manager's record is customary is a legal question, not a design preference. Financials and legal advice are not sources for this work; counsel's written answer is.

**Options for 5.2.8.** (a) None beyond the person's line. Commits Sŏn to defending later decisions from the person page and the captures alone. (b) The workbook's recap, written by the lead and read back, kept beside the person page. Commits Sŏn to a lead-authored record and to a retention rule. (c) A jointly written recap, both sign, kept beside the page. (d) Nothing on a first raising; a record begins only when a matter is on its second raising, and belongs to 5.8. Each commits 5.2.13 (the form, if any) and 5.8 to a different starting point.

## 5. Failure types: what reaches a person at all

**White paper.** The implementation example: the server resolves within their range, the close captures it, the lead reviews, the process is updated, the next server inherits the fix (WP p. 13). Identify the failure mode before intervening at the people level (WP p. 14). What allowed this, not who failed; management absorbs blame; post-mortems private and process-focused (WP p. 17). Comps and generosity are pre-authorized; judgment is trusted (WP p. 26).

**From the earlier work.** Three types, from Amy Edmondson's taxonomy: a basic failure (a known process not followed) gets a process placement fix and reaches the person as a hypothesis only if it recurs on the same role after the fix; a complex failure (several factors, no single wrong step) gets a process fix and deliberately has no person in it; an intelligent failure (a judgment call inside someone's range that did not work) is absorbed by the system and the person is not corrected, because correcting good-faith judgment is the failure the white paper's range exists to prevent. Types are counted per period, never per person. A capture that names a person as cause is rewritten to describe the process before it is typed. The lead reviewing owns the fix.

**Blame absorption as mechanism.** "Management absorbs blame" (WP p. 17) was translated into three rules: the reviewing seat owns the fix; the reporter is never read as the cause; a lead never performs taking blame in a group, since that is still a sentence about fault. 1.2.3 decides whether a founder's own domain failure gets the same post-mortem.

**Options for 5.2.9 and 5.2.10.** For types: (a) the three as above; (b) two only, process and judgment, with the complex case folded into process; (c) the "just culture" three from aviation and healthcare (error, at-risk behavior, reckless behavior), which put more weight on the person's choice. For absorption: (a) the three rules as above; (b) the rules plus a public one-line owner statement at the close ("the sequence is mine to fix"), which some rooms find steadying.

## 6. Inherited hard conversations

**Failed readiness row** (86akhb2x5). The hardest onboarding conversation. The earlier work's form: read the assessor's own words together, row by row; the lead adds nothing to the row; ask what happened before saying anything; the plan's next step follows on its own. The lead never says a sentence whose subject is the person and whose claim the plan record does not already hold.

**Interviewer retraining** (86akh7r6z, 3.2.13). Delivered at the interviewer's own check-in as a fact from the record, framed first as a possible gap in the form, not the interviewer's judgment.

**A hiring decision that contradicts every earlier stage** (86akh7r6z). Never delivered to any person; a calibration matter between leads (3.2.9, 3.2.39).

**A disagreement between two people** (17tn048qck1, 17tn048qc2b). Its work content is a joint item; its person content is each person's own check-in. 4.5.10 holds the unblocking path; there is no three-way conversation in this chunk.

**Feedback about a duty** (86akhcz71) is 5.1.10's. **A declined recognition** is 5.3.8's.

## 7. The serious breach

**White paper.** Silent. The blame-the-process rule (WP p. 17) has no stated exception.

**From the earlier work.** "The non-negotiable breach" was a name without a definition anywhere. The only three-person conversation the house permits was described (86akht2k9): the person's lead delivers the flag with a second lead as witness, off the floor, on the clock, in the person's language. What follows is 5.10's. 4.5.11 sets the destination for a report of a serious violation.

**Why before the first hire.** A conduct policy is an employment document counsel will want in place before anyone is hired, and a candidate may reasonably see it (WP p. 18 promises nothing withheld). It is the one part of this chunk that is legal first and coaching second.

**Options for 5.2.2.** (a) A short closed list of acts, reviewed by counsel, everything else coached. (b) Counsel's standard list for Texas hospitality employers, adopted as is. (c) A list plus a catch-all ("conduct that makes continued work unsafe for others"), which is common and which counsel may or may not favor.

## 8. How others have handled it

General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- The Situation-Behavior-Impact form (Center for Creative Leadership) is the standard shape for the second opening.
- Amy Edmondson's *Right Kind of Wrong* (2023) is the source of the basic, complex, and intelligent failure types; her earlier work on psychological safety is where "blameless" comes from.
- "Just culture" in aviation and healthcare (James Reason; Sidney Dekker) distinguishes error, at-risk behavior, and recklessness, and reserves individual consequence for the last.
- Blameless post-mortems in software operations, described in Google's SRE writing, record what happened and what allowed it without naming a person as cause; the practice is public and widely copied.
- Professional kitchens have long separated the live call ("behind," "hands," "fire") from any conversation about a cook, which is 5.2.6's distinction in practice.
- Most US employers keep a manager-written record of performance conversations because employment counsel asks them to; Sŏn's leaning against one is a real divergence and is why 5.2.4 comes first.
