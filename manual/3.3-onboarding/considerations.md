# 3.3 Onboarding: considerations

What the white paper holds, what the earlier work argued, the options, and where Sŏn may diverge from the book. In the book's order. Nothing here is a decision; each option says what choosing it commits Sŏn to. "WP p. N" is the white paper; "pp." alone is the book.

## 1. What onboarding is for

**The book.** Onboarding covers the why (mission and story), the what (priorities and goals), and the how (behaviors and principles), and produces nine things: community with other new hires, leadership's vision, business knowledge, insight into users, the operating structure and goals, a picture of the org, key processes, where the team fits, and the ability to self-educate (pp. 216 to 217).

**The white paper.** A ninety-day plan with milestones, skill assessments, feedback, and regular check-ins, "so early success is planned rather than hoped for" (WP p. 18). Competency validation: no one touches a table until ready (WP p. 19). Performance systems develop rather than judge, and a formal review never contains new information (WP p. 19). Silent on what onboarding may conclude about a person.

**From the earlier work.** The most useful thing it did was state onboarding's intended outcome as behavior, so it is testable: at the plan's close a person holds their seat alone at service tempo, states their decision-rights range and escalation triggers unprompted and has used the range, says "customer" in speech and writing, can speak to every dish from having tasted it, has opened the learning platform unprompted, has said at each check-in what was in the way, and can name the next seat in their path. It also drew a hard line around what onboarding may not conclude: a verdict on the person, a level or pay outcome, a "fit" judgment (no such category exists at Sŏn's hiring, per 3.1.5 and 3.2.2), month-six performance, or whether the hire was a mistake (that is 3.4).

**Options for 3.3.2.** (a) Adopt the behavior list as the plan's outcome and the refusal list as its boundary. Commits every later mechanism in this chunk to producing facts, not judgments, and pushes anything judgment-shaped to Chapter 5. (b) Adopt the book's nine outcomes as written, which are broader (community, industry knowledge, org picture) and softer. Commits Sŏn to outcomes it cannot test at ninety days, and to a curriculum for them. (c) Both: the behavior list as what the plan tracks, the nine as what the curriculum covers. Commits Sŏn to keeping the two lists apart in the plan block.

## 2. Who runs onboarding

**The book** assumes a manager who knows the curriculum, adds team context, holds the first meeting, and owns the ramp (p. 216); for a leader, a buddy, a guide, an HR partner, and a coach join (p. 219).

**The white paper.** The Maitre d owns team development and training (WP p. 10). A mentor for every new person (WP p. 18). The practical assessment auto-schedules with the appropriate domain lead (WP p. 11). No one manager holds everything; authority lives in the domain (WP p. 7).

**From the earlier work.** The manager's onboarding functions were split across five holders with stated ranges: the domain lead (holds the check-in, names the mentor, places paired services and the first solo, writes on the person page only at the check-in, does not teach modules or run the readiness read); the mentor (a peer, paid as shift, teaches beside the person, writes nothing); the assessor (trained, never the mentor, scores the read, does not decide); a founder (the why session, pre-opening and through the first quarter); the system (creates accounts, delivers materials, places check-ins, records completions, refuses a start date or a solo without its conditions). The reasoning: no single point of failure, and the same separation of duties 3.2.35 uses for the practical.

**Options for 3.3.3.** (a) The five-holder split. Commits Sŏn to training assessors and mentors as separate roles and to a system that can enforce the gates (3.3.32). (b) The domain lead holds everything except the why session, as the book's manager does. Commits Sŏn to leads who teach, assess, and judge their own reports, which 3.2.35 refused for hiring. (c) The Maitre d holds training for the whole floor, per WP p. 10, with the Operations Lead's domains borrowing it. Commits one seat to a load 4.1.35 would have to watch.

## 3. Why before how: the why session and the founding documents

**The book.** Leadership takes part, ideally in a recurring session with stories of the principles in action; stories, not bullet points (p. 216).

**The white paper.** "Why comes before how": orientation to mission, values, and behavioral standard before operational training (WP p. 18). Brandon is the public voice and the origin of the operating concepts (WP p. 3). The structure a new person enters is fully described: the web with no top or bottom, new people entering at the outside edge as the most sensitive position, the two co-leads, domain ownership, "blame the process" (WP pp. 7 to 14, 17).

**From the earlier work.** A proposed order: the mission and what it was chosen against; each principle with one real moment; the behavioral standard and the language rule ("customer"); the structure drawn from the person's own position outward; stories that make structural points, not founder narratives. Delivered in person by a founder until a successor is named; the domain lead may deliver the structure part; the mentor is present and delivers none of it. The risk named: the session cannot be delivered until 2.1.17 exists, and the temptation is to fill the gap with placeholder language.

**Options for 3.3.5 (the gate).** (a) Founding documents finished before any cohort start date; the two leads exempt. Commits the founders to a document deadline ahead of the hiring calendar, and accepts that start dates slide if it is missed. (b) No gate; the first cohort hears version one and the finished version at the mechanism reset. Commits Sŏn to a first cohort onboarded on an unfinished why. (c) A gate on the mission and principles only. Commits 2.1 to finishing 2.1.2 and 2.1.5 first.

**Options for 3.3.6 (the deliverer).** (a) Brandon, in person, until a named successor. Commits his calendar to every start date. (b) Either founder. Commits Dominic to telling the story too. (c) A founder for the first period, then the Maitre d, whose seat owns team development (WP p. 10). Commits the Maitre d's onboarding to include learning to tell it.

## 4. Before the first shift

**The book.** Prep work (paperwork, equipment) happens asynchronously before the program starts (p. 217). For a leader: a welcome with personal context and pre-start reading (p. 220).

**The white paper.** Before arrival: information delivered, expectations set, mentor connected, locker assigned, uniform and tools ready "down to the shoes" (WP p. 18). Full uniforms company-paid; smart lockers; a real employee area with break space, "basic dignity, and dignity is a precondition for presence" (WP pp. 19 to 20). Rippling for payroll and HR (WP p. 20). The candidate already holds a single sheet with the org chart, their lead, pay, communication expectations, and the training plan with pay dates (WP p. 18).

**From the earlier work.** Two things worth keeping. First, the book's "prep work done asynchronously" read as the house's prep, not the person's unpaid work: nothing beyond legally required paperwork is required before the first paid hour; what is sent ahead is delivered, not assigned. Second, the arrival note, generated at acceptance: the first shift as a paid orientation block, the mentor's name and seat, the seat description and candidate sheet, the plain-language range, the first two weeks' schedule with the check-in visible, a link to (not an assignment of) the first modules, and the person page. Start-date conditions: mentor connected, uniform and locker ready, first modules built, first check-in on a window the lead works, range filled, never a new period's first service, never coverage before readiness; a date that cannot be met is a training-infrastructure load. "Mentor connected" was defined: named at acceptance, introduced by the system in the person's language, one message from the mentor, overlapping shifts for two weeks; the mentor never sees the application file.

The physical dependency is real: the employee area and lockers must be in the build plan, and the uniform fitted at a paid block before the start date. The earlier conflict over garment lists rested on brand documents, not a source here; what remains is the deadline (3.3.44).

**Options for 3.3.7.** (a) Nothing required except legal paperwork. Commits the first shift to carrying the accounts, the tour, and the reading. (b) The first modules required before day one, unpaid. Commits Sŏn to unpaid work at the start of a relationship built on "if it matters, Sŏn pays for it" (WP p. 19); counsel should see it. (c) The first modules offered and paid if completed. Commits payroll to a pre-start paid category.

**Options for 3.3.16.** (a) The full list, system-enforced, interim forms marked. Commits 3.3.32 to confirming the tools can refuse a date. (b) A lead's checklist. Commits the house to a lead's memory (WP p. 5). (c) A shorter list for the first cohort. Commits Sŏn to a written date on which the full list takes over.

## 5. The operational training sequence and the readiness read

**The book.** Crenn: people get thrown onto the job; understanding the whole operation, not just your station; training as a continuation, not a one-time event (pp. 216 to 217). Function-specific programs start in week one (p. 216).

**The white paper.** The industry's model is "shadow someone for two shifts, catch fragments, get thrown in before ready, fail, quit, repeat," which it calls designed-in failure (WP pp. 18 to 19). Competency validation: no one touches a table until ready (WP p. 19). Advancement is a competency conversation with the appropriate lead who signs off (WP p. 19). Silent on the steps in between and on who scores.

**From the earlier work.** The sequence reuses 3.2.23's interviewer training: modules, a live walkthrough, one observed service, paired services at a count set per seat by its lead, then solo. The solo is gated by a readiness read scored by a trained assessor who is neither the mentor nor the teacher; a lead never certifies their own reports. The outcome is binary, "ready" or "not yet," never a score or a verdict on presence or speed. "Not yet" names rows to redo; a second "not yet" on the same rows becomes a training-infrastructure question before it is a question about the person. "Service-ready" means a passed read for one seat in one service period. Pre-opening, the read happens in a training service and reads procedure, not load; a load read after the first month of real service reads load. No one's first solo is opening night. The kitchen's content waits on the chef partner; the sequence and the assessor's separation were held as fixed regardless.

**Options for 3.3.8.** (a) The five-step sequence with a per-seat paired count. Commits every domain lead to setting and defending a count, and the schedule to carrying two bodies in one seat for those services. (b) The book's shape, function-specific programs in weeks one and two with the lead judging readiness. Commits Sŏn to the lead's judgment as the gate, which is what WP p. 19 argues against. (c) The sequence with a fixed paired count for all seats. Commits Sŏn to a number that is wrong for some seats in one direction or the other.

**Options for 3.3.9.** (a) Binary outcome, independent assessor, "not yet" escalates to infrastructure on repeat. Commits Sŏn to a trained assessor pool before the first cohort (4.1.14) and to a second attempt's cost. (b) A scored form read by the lead. Commits Sŏn to numbers that will be read as a rating, against WP p. 19. (c) The mentor and the lead jointly, as 3.2.23 does for interviewers. Commits the mentor to judging the person they taught.

## 6. The ninety-day plan and its check-ins

**The book.** The manager decides what the new hire needs in the first weeks; the first meeting sets initial priorities and flags the career conversation a few months out (pp. 216, 219).

**The white paper.** A ninety-day plan with expectations and goals, regular check-ins, course correction (WP p. 18). Feedback both directions; no surprises (WP p. 19). 2.3.9 and 2.3.10 decide the check-in's form and intervals; 2.3.11 decides the person page; 2.2.14 decides how development goals are set.

**From the earlier work.** The plan as tracked milestones on the person page, not a free-text document: one goal stated as an end state ("holds the seat solo, with the range stated, by the plan's close"), a milestone list running from orientation through modules, walkthrough, observed, paired, tasted knowledge, the read, first solo, load read, range used, platform use, development path, and close; the person's stated needs from hire with one line per check-in on whether the house met them; the check-in dates. A fixed ninety days. A 30/60/90 deliverables plan was rejected: a cook's first thirty days produce a station reliably held, not a deliverable. Excluded: rating fields, free text about the person, a "fit" milestone, pay, any probation label. Nothing in the plan is new against what the person was told at hire, except tasted knowledge (the menu did not exist) and the internal tools.

Six check-in foci (2.3.10 decides the intervals): whether the house delivered what the person said they needed; paired services and where the modules contradict the floor; the read, line by line; the load read and whether the range has been used; the development path; closing the plan and moving to the standing cadence. No check-in carries a verdict, pay, probation, or a comment on manner.

Extension: once, by the lead with the person, to a named date, only when a milestone was blocked by something the house failed to provide. A second extension escalates. An unmet goal after the extension goes to 5.8 and 5.10; the plan never concludes that a person should leave.

**Options for 3.3.10.** (a) Tracked milestones with an end-state goal. Commits the person page (2.3.11) to carrying the block and the system to writing what it can see. (b) The book's shape: a lead-written first-weeks plan with initial priorities. Commits Sŏn to plans that vary by lead and live in the lead's memory. (c) Milestones for hourly seats, a written plan for leads. Commits the lead onboarding (3.3.45) to a different form, which it may need anyway.

**Options for 3.3.12.** (a) One extension, only for a house failure, then 5.8. Commits leads to naming the failure in writing. (b) Extensions at the lead's discretion. Commits Sŏn to plans that drift and to a lead's judgment as the gate. (c) No extension; an unmet plan goes straight to 5.8. Commits 5.8 to handling cases that were the house's fault.

## 7. The mentor, and the opening cohort

**The book** has a buddy and a guide for leaders (p. 219) and a colleague's duties to a newcomer (pp. 221 to 224). It does not describe a mentor for every hire.

**The white paper.** Every new person is paired with a mentor, "a proven cultural steward rather than simply the strongest performer," who answers questions and makes the early experience reflect the standard (WP p. 18). Silent on eligibility, load, and the first cohort.

**From the earlier work.** Eligibility: closed their own plan with the goal held (pre-opening, a passed training-service read), no unresolved conduct issue, mentor module complete, same or adjacent seat, said yes; a roster per domain on the seat inventory (4.1.14), no ranking. Naming: the domain lead, at offer, from the roster; never themselves, the assessor, someone off-roster, or someone already carrying an open plan; matched on language and schedule overlap, never on rapport, which reproduces whoever is already in the room. No eligible mentor means no start date. Mentoring is paid shift time; anything beyond is 5.5's. The mentor teaches what modules do not hold, is present at the walkthrough and paired services, models the standard, shows where concerns go; never scores the read, writes on the page, gives a verdict, assigns or disciplines, delivers the why session, sits in the check-in, or sees the application. Either party ends the pairing by telling the lead, with nothing recorded.

The opening cohort problem: a "proven" mentor cannot exist before opening. The earlier work proposed two waves: a first wave small enough for the leads and the kitchen's owners to mentor through training services, and a second wave mentored by the first once training-service records show the procedures reproduced, the first wave's mentor status provisional until reviewed after opening. It argued against one wave mentored only by leads (overload while they stand up their domains) and against outside trainers (not in the planned team size). The decision belongs with 3.2.32 and 3.1.18; the chef partner holds the kitchen's half.

**Options for 3.3.15.** (a) Two waves. Commits the hiring calendar to two start-date clusters and to enough training services between them, and commits the first wave to a heavier ask. (b) One wave, mentored by the leads and founders. Commits the leads to mentoring while building their domains, and the founders to the floor. (c) One wave with a small interim group hired early as trainers and folded into seats after opening. Commits headcount the white paper does not describe and a title that dissolves. (d) One wave, mentor field left empty until after opening. Commits Sŏn to opening without the thing WP p. 18 promises every hire.

## 8. The decision-rights range as an onboarding output

**The white paper.** The server owns the table relationship and resolves it "using their authorized judgment and generosity range" (WP p. 13); staff are generous without asking permission; comps are an offensive tool (WP p. 26). 2.2.2 and 2.2.4 decide the rule and the register; 3.1.36 already treats a failed unprompted statement of the range as an onboarding failure.

**From the earlier work.** Five checkpoints: the range said aloud at the offer; read together and repeated back on day one; taught with two worked examples in a module; observed then handed off during paired services; tested unprompted at the readiness read and referenced again at the load-read check-in. Every seat with any range goes through all five, not only customer-facing ones. Unset thresholds are said to be unset, never invented. Refusals: never present the range as case-by-case permission; never imply under-use is safer; never teach a trigger without naming where it goes; never teach a figure that has not been set. The structure is drawn from the person's position outward at orientation and again in miniature whenever a module names who decides something.

**Options for 3.3.17.** (a) All five checkpoints for every seat with a range. Commits 3.1.29's offer conversation, the orientation block, a module, the paired services, and the readiness form each to carrying it. (b) Taught in a module and tested at the read. Commits Sŏn to a range that is known but not practiced before the first solo. (c) Delivered by the lead in the first conversation only. Commits Sŏn to the lead's telling, unrecorded.

## 9. Modules, the platform, and synthesized media

**The white paper.** The learning platform is "engaging and personalized and constantly expanding"; any qualified team member can author and is paid; completion earns; competency validation gates the table (WP p. 19). Employees are the users; systems are built for a 22-year-old server six months in, with continuous feedback (WP p. 17). Heavily deterministic software over a thin layer of AI; bought where settled, built where it is the edge (WP p. 21). The white paper does not define a module, "qualified," approval, languages, or what stands in before the platform exists.

**From the earlier work.** A module contains: the procedure as steps; the seat and situation; the reason it exists; a real-person recording where physical, never with customers present; any range it touches with its trigger; language, author, approver, version; the readiness row it feeds. It exists in every language the seat's incumbents need, fits one check-in's length on a paid shift, reads on a phone, and is revised through a versioned change when the floor contradicts it. It refuses: a script beyond a courtesy line or two; a procedure with no decision where the seat holds one; a skill that develops with experience taught as executable; a scoring quiz; a developing competency taught as required at entry. "Qualified" means holding the validated competency; the domain lead approves their area, the chef partner the kitchen, food safety and compliance need counsel input; no self-approval. The first set: by the leads, the kitchen's owner, and the founders; only what reaches the read; two readers side by side; version one. Pay constraints carried to 5.5: authors paid for an approved module, learners through completion tracking, mentoring as paid teaching time.

Capabilities to confirm in the bought tools (the white paper names only Rippling, WP p. 20): completion read automatically; paths versioned; each account in its language; authored content held for approval; the schedule refusing a solo without a passed read.

Synthesized media: a training-video tool with a synthesized presenter was bought. The earlier objection rested on a brand principle, not a source here. What remains is Sŏn's own question: WP p. 21 keeps AI off anything load-bearing and WP p. 36 refuses to assert the unproven; a synthesized person telling a new hire how the house works sits near both. The earlier position, if allowed: procedural narration only, disclosed, never the why session or the customer.

**Options for 3.3.18.** (a) The standard as the earlier work wrote it. Commits every author to a real-person recording and every domain lead to approving modules. (b) A lighter standard: steps, seat, language, version, approver. Commits Sŏn to modules that may teach a procedure without the decision inside it. (c) The platform vendor's template. Commits the standard to whatever the tool holds.

**Options for 3.3.19.** (a) No synthesized presenter or voice in training. Commits Sŏn to real people on camera, and to the purchased tool going unused. (b) Procedural narration only, disclosed, never the why session. Commits Sŏn to a line that has to be policed per module. (c) Allowed. Commits Sŏn to a position on synthesized media it will be asked about elsewhere (the phone).

## 10. Tasted knowledge and the menu

**The white paper.** Deeper knowledge of key products is a book outcome (p. 217); the white paper's claim is readiness before the table (WP p. 19) and the chef partner's ownership of the food (WP p. 3). 2.3.29 has the chef partner set the menu change cadence and its training window.

**From the earlier work.** Tasted knowledge means the person has tasted every current dish, can say what it is and why it is made that way in their own words when asked (and say nothing when not asked), and never uses filler. The readiness row tests only those three things, nothing about timing or intuition. No floor read passes this row before the cohort has tasted every dish on the fixed menu with the chef's explanation, in a training service. The number of training services depends on menu length, kitchen readiness, and cohort size, which need the chef partner and the founders holding the calendar. A menu that changes between tastings and opening reopens the row for the changed dishes. A menu fixed too late for tastings is a sequencing failure that delays opening. Once open, any menu change adds a tasting before anyone serves the dish alone. The old term for this was borrowed from brand material and is not used here.

**Options for 3.3.21.** (a) The narrow definition as a readiness row, with the delay rule. Commits the chef partner to a menu date ahead of the tasting window and the founders to holding opening to it. (b) Menu knowledge taught from written dish notes, tasting when possible. Commits Sŏn to servers describing food they have not eaten. (c) The row exists but is not a gate for opening. Commits opening night to servers who may not have tasted everything, against WP p. 19.

## 11. Internal moves and lead changes

**The book.** A short survey at every team change (initiated by whom; developing or redirecting; did the managers talk; satisfaction; more impact), read over time for "bad mobility" (pp. 217 to 218). A three-step manager transition (preview, joint meeting, follow-up) with a sample agenda (p. 218, p. 247).

**The white paper.** Cross-training is career development; internal succession is the goal (WP p. 19). Cross-strand fluency is the real development marker and is paid for (WP p. 11). The next skill set unlocks automatically after a set period; the assessment auto-schedules; a ready-now pool exists (WP p. 11). Internal promotion rate is tracked (WP p. 23). 4.1.10 defines the fluencies; 4.1.11 decides who signs across domains.

**From the earlier work.** Two timers run from day one: the plan's ninety days and the seat's own unlock timer. Every track is visible and any module may be started; the practical assessment for a second domain waits until the first plan has closed with its goal met. Shorter plans for a period or domain move: the same sequence at the receiving domain's pace, inside the existing check-in cadence, no new ninety-day plan, no repeat of the why session. The transitions meeting was replaced by the new lead reading the person's page before the next check-in and reviewing it with the person, nothing passing between leads outside that record. The mobility survey was replaced by existing records (the unlock, the transfer, the period move) plus the standing upward question.

**Options for 3.3.29 to 3.3.31.** Shorter plan: (a) the same sequence, shortened, no repeat of the why; commits the receiving lead to a paired count for someone already on staff. (b) A full plan for a domain change; commits Sŏn to a second ninety days for a kitchen-to-floor move. Lead changes: (a) the book's meeting; commits both leads and the person to a scheduled conversation. (b) The page read plus the next check-in; commits Sŏn to a person page rich enough to carry it. Mobility: (a) the survey at every move; commits someone to reading it. (b) Existing records and the upward question; commits 2.3.15 and 4.1.11 to carrying it.

## 12. The working-with-me document and the first conversation

**The book.** Every manager writes one and uses it in the first meeting, which sets mutual expectations across six items: onboarding context, operating approach, management style (with the document shared and the report invited to write their own), communication preferences, initial priorities, the upcoming career conversation. Not everyone is ready to write their own; share first to set the tone (pp. 218 to 219; template p. 257).

**The white paper** is silent. 1.1 owns the founders' documents (1.1.7, 1.1.8), the chef partner's (1.1.9, 1.1.13), who reads them (1.1.5), and the kit (1.1.11). 1.1.4 decides how the work-style vocabulary is used beyond the founders. This chunk does not rebuild any of that.

**From the earlier work.** The founders' exchange is diligence, not onboarding, and is never required of hourly staff; leads write these, frontline staff read them. For a later-hired lead: read the founders' documents before the first shift, write their own between weeks four and six (once they have seen the operation), review it at plan close, readable by their team. The work-style grid may appear only as self-description inside a leader's own document, never as an instrument on anyone, which 1.1.4 now decides.

**Options for 3.3.24.** (a) Weeks four to six, with the 1.1.11 kit, read by the lead's team. Commits the lead's plan to a milestone for it. (b) Before the first shift. Commits a lead to describing how they work at Sŏn before they have. (c) At plan close. Commits the team to a quarter without it.

**Options for 3.3.26.** (a) Hourly hires read their lead's document and are invited, not asked, to write their own. Commits the kit to fitting an hourly seat. (b) Read only. Commits Sŏn to a one-way document. (c) Neither. Commits Sŏn to a first conversation without the book's centerpiece.

**Options for 3.3.25.** (a) The six items split between the paid orientation block (context, priorities, the range) and the first check-in (working together, the document, the career heads-up). Commits 2.3.18's kit to a first-check-in variant. (b) All six on day one. Commits day one to a long conversation. (c) The plan block replaces it. Commits Sŏn to expectations set by a page, not a person.

## 13. New lead onboarding

**The book.** Same objectives, more invested, because a leader's impact is felt immediately (p. 219). The New Leader Experience: welcome email with personal context, pre-reads, prescheduled 1:1s, a buddy and a guide, the Hogan assessment, a coach, first-month actions, a 90-day 360; for critical roles, a short standup with the leader's manager daily or every other day. The hardest part is knowing when to move from listening to acting (pp. 219 to 221). Six objectives mapped to tactics (p. 220). A week-by-week schedule (pp. 257 to 259). A direct report's duties to a new leader (pp. 221 to 224).

**The white paper.** The leads are co-leads of parallel systems serving the same center (WP p. 10). Internal succession is the goal, to the point where the building rarely hires a manager from outside (WP p. 19). Silent on a lead's first months.

**From the earlier work.** The two pre-opening leads arrive weeks before the dinner gate (3.2.32 holds the date), into a building with no service. Their onboarding: pre-reading (prior decisions, seat descriptions, the founders' working-with-me documents), the why session from a founder, the leads' review from day one. A later outside lead gets the co-lead as guide and reads the domain's history. A lead's plan goal is a state of their domain running, not a personal unlock. The six objectives mapped: excitement, declined as manufactured; context, by pre-reading; connections, by the standing meetings; listening versus acting, by a quiet period; alignment with principles, by the working-with-me document rather than a psychometric instrument (refused on 3.2.2's reasoning; 1.1.3 decides the founders' own case); course correction, by the lead's check-in with a partner (2.3.14). Declined as separate mechanisms: a coach, a 360 (the standing upward question covers it), welcome emails and an onboarding point person (the arrival note and the plan cover them), "loaning social capital" from above (a lead's authority lives in the domain, WP p. 7).

**Options for 3.3.22.** (a) Map each objective to an existing mechanism and decline the rest, as above. Commits Sŏn to the standing meetings and the partner check-in doing the work the book gives to a program, and to a quiet period whose length someone sets. (b) Run the book's program as written, minus the assessment. Commits Sŏn to a coach, a 360, and a schedule for two people before opening. (c) The map for the two pre-opening leads, and the fuller program for any outside lead after opening, when a team exists to receive them. Commits Sŏn to two versions of the kit.

**Options for 3.3.23 (a coach).** (a) None; the partner check-in covers the function. (b) One for an outside lead hired after opening, none for the two pre-opening leads. (c) One for each lead's first quarter. Financials are not a source here; each option's cost is Dominic's to weigh.

## 14. Closing the loop on onboarding

**The book.** Pulse questions during rapid hiring ("new people on my team are being onboarded quickly and effectively"), a manager survey at milestones (six weeks, twelve weeks, six months), and a hiring-manager retrospective when a hire goes wrong (pp. 225 to 226; the retrospective is 3.4's).

**The white paper.** Leading indicators over lagging ones (WP p. 22). Employee NPS tracked regularly, a structured feedback channel with the loop closed (WP p. 22). Retention, internal promotion, and participation in development tracked with the same seriousness as cost (WP p. 23).

**From the earlier work.** A monthly onboarding read computed from plan data instead of a survey: time from start to readiness, share passing the read first time, "not yet" counts by category, extensions and infrastructure issues with their fixes, and whether the house met what people said they needed at hire; no target number, no name on any line; first run at the mechanism reset (2.3.30), then monthly. A standing training-infrastructure line at the leads' review that logs every extension, second "not yet," load-read failure, missing mentor, and slipped start date, each with the broken link, a fix, an owner, and a date, never a person as cause; a recurring pattern escalates (a mentor overloaded twice is a staffing signal; a module the floor keeps contradicting is a content signal).
