# 3.3 Onboarding: working session guide

One chunk, one session (this one may take two sittings; the agenda marks a natural break). Brandon decides; this guide prompts. "Cons. N" points to `considerations.md`; task numbers to `tasks.md`.

## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** Everything between a signed offer and a person holding their seat alone: the first content they receive, what arrives before their first shift, the training sequence, the readiness read, the ninety-day plan and its check-ins, the mentor, how a person comes to hold their decision-rights range, the training modules and the platform, tasted knowledge of the menu, a lead's first months, and how a change of lead or a move between domains is handled. It also decides how onboarding is measured.

**What is already decided upstream.** 2.3.9 to 2.3.11 fix the check-in, its intervals, and the person page; this chunk fills the plan into them. 3.1.31 gives every candidate a sheet with the training plan and pay dates at the first interview, so the training sequence and the plan's shape have to exist before the first hire. 3.2.24 fixed the interviewer training sequence (module, live session, observed, paired, solo) that the operational sequence reuses. 3.2.34 holds the hiring calendar as gates; the two leads arrive weeks before the dinner gate. 3.2.37 separated the practical's assessor from its teacher and its decider; the readiness read inherits that separation. 4.1.11 and 4.1.14 decide who runs an assessment and put assessor and mentor pools on the seat inventory. 1.1 owns the working-with-me document and its kit (1.1.7, 1.1.11); this chunk only decides when a later lead writes one and whether hourly staff read theirs. 2.1.17 is the founding document the why session is told from. The white paper commits Sŏn to a mentor for every hire, a ninety-day plan, onboarding that begins before the first shift, why before how, uniforms and lockers ready, and "no one touches a table until they are actually ready" (WP pp. 18 to 20).

**Who else must agree.** Dominic on anything that sets headcount or calendar (the cohort's waves, 3.3.15; a coach, 3.3.23) and on tool capabilities (3.3.32). The chef partner on tasted knowledge and the kitchen's training content (3.3.21, 3.3.48), and on the kitchen half of the cohort's mentoring. Each lead, once hired, confirms the paired count and readiness rows for their seats.

**Why it sits here.** The book puts onboarding after hiring and before hiring mistakes, and Sŏn's calendar agrees: the two leads are the first hires and are onboarded into an empty building, then the cohort is trained in training services before dinner opens. Most of this chunk's decisions have to be made before the first offer because the candidate sheet describes them.

Ask where he wants to start. The agenda is a default order.

## 3. Briefs

### 3.3.2 Decide what onboarding is for: what a person holds at ninety days, and what onboarding may never conclude

**The question:** on day ninety, what can a new server, runner, or cook do and say that they could not on day one, and what will Sŏn refuse to conclude about them from those ninety days?

**Why it matters now:** every mechanism in this chunk builds toward the first list and must not produce the second. The candidate reads the training plan at the first interview (WP p. 18), so the outcome has to be sayable to a stranger.

**Openers:**
- It is a Tuesday in month four. A server who started with the cohort is working a section alone. Describe what you see that tells you onboarding worked. Now describe what you would see if it had not.
- Sideways: at Coqodaq, opening service, who was ready on night one and how did anyone know? What did "ready" mean there, and who said it?
- Which of the book's nine outcomes (p. 217) would you be embarrassed to find missing at ninety days? Which do you not care about?
- A lead says at week twelve, "she is fine on procedure but I am not sure she is a Sŏn person." What does the plan let that sentence become? Anything?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Is the outcome a behavior list (holds the seat solo, states the range, says "customer," has tasted every dish, has opened the platform, has said what was in the way, names the next seat), or the book's nine, or both kept apart (cons. 1)?
- Which of these can the system see, which only the lead can see, and which no one can see at ninety days (intuitive read of a room, cross-domain fluency, a ceiling)?
- Say the refusal list out loud: no verdict, no level, no pay outcome, no fit judgment, no "was this a mistake." Which one is he tempted to keep?

**What the book says:** a well-designed program produces nine things, from community with other new hires to the ability to self-educate (p. 217); the curriculum is the fastest possible ramp to success (p. 216).

**White-paper default:** a ninety-day plan with milestones, skill assessments, feedback, and check-ins, "so early success is planned rather than hoped for" (WP p. 18); no one touches a table until ready (WP p. 19); performance systems develop rather than judge (WP p. 19). Silent on what onboarding may conclude.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Stripe's core onboarding is a weeklong curriculum for every hire regardless of level, with function programs alongside (p. 216). Restaurants at the top of the field commonly run a fixed training period ending in a written or practical test before a server takes a section; the test is usually the lead's to pass.

**Options:** (a) behavior list as the plan's outcome, refusal list as its boundary; (b) the book's nine as written; (c) both, kept apart. Depth: cons. 1.

**Watch for:** an outcome that is really a judgment in disguise ("shows ownership"); an outcome no one can observe; a refusal list he agrees to and then breaks in the extension rule (3.3.12).

**A finished answer:** two short lists, both sayable to a candidate, with each outcome tagged by who can see it.

**Needs agreement from:** no one; the chef partner should hear it for the kitchen's seats.

### 3.3.3 Decide who runs onboarding: the functions, and who holds each

**The question:** the book's manager holds the whole ramp. At Sŏn, who holds the check-in, who names the mentor, who teaches, who assesses, who tells the story, and who records?

**Why it matters now:** the seat inventory (4.1.14) needs to know whether assessors and mentors are pools; the readiness read (3.3.9) needs to know whether the lead may score it; the tool build (3.3.32) needs to know what the system enforces.

**Openers:**
- Picture the Maitre d in week three of the cohort's training. List everything they are doing that day for new people. Now cross out what they could not possibly do well for eight people at once.
- Sideways: in a kitchen you have worked in, who actually taught a new cook the station, and who decided they were ready? Were they the same person? What did that cost?
- If the person who taught you also grades you, what do you stop telling them?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Five holders (lead, mentor, assessor, founder, system) with boundaries, or the lead holds it all, or the Maitre d holds training for the whole floor (cons. 2)?
- Which function may never be held by the same person as another (teacher and assessor; mentor and check-in holder)?
- What does the system hold that no person should have to remember (completions, dates, the refusal of a solo without a passed read)?

**What the book says:** the manager knows the curriculum, adds what the hire needs, holds the first meeting (p. 216); a leader also gets a buddy, a guide, an HR partner, a coach (p. 219).

**White-paper default:** the Maitre d owns team development and training (WP p. 10); a mentor for every new person (WP p. 18); assessments schedule with the appropriate domain lead (WP p. 11); authority lives in the domain, and the system holds what people know (WP pp. 7 to 8).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Larger hospitality groups often separate a trainer role from the manager and certify trainers; the manager still signs off. Airlines and hospitals separate the instructor from the check examiner as a rule.

**Options:** (a) the five-holder split; (b) the lead holds all but the why session; (c) the Maitre d holds floor training for every domain. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** a split that leaves no one owning the ramp as a whole; the founder as a permanent holder of anything; a function assigned to "the system" before 3.3.32 confirms it can.

**A finished answer:** a table of functions and holders with one boundary sentence each, and the two pairings that may never coincide.

**Needs agreement from:** the chef partner for the kitchen's holders.

### 3.3.4 Decide the curriculum's three parts and their order: why, what, how

**The question:** what does a new person hear first, second, and third, and where do this quarter's goals and the service period's charter enter?

**Why it matters now:** the why session (3.3.35) is written to this order; the arrival note and day one are built around it.

**Openers:**
- A new runner's first hour. What is the first sentence anyone says to them that is not logistics?
- Sideways: the white paper says the industry hangs culture on a poster (WP p. 18). What was on the wall at the last place you worked, and did anyone ever say it aloud?
- The "what" (priorities and goals) is the part the earlier work skipped. Does a new server need to know dinner's charter metrics in week one, or is that noise?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Why, then what, then how, as the book has it; or why, then how, with the what arriving at the first check-in?
- What does "the why" contain: mission, principles, behavioral standard, the structure the person has entered? All on day one, or the structure when their domain exists?
- Where do the goals (2.2.27) and the charter (2.1.15) get read by a new person, and by whom?

**What the book says:** the why of the company, the what of the business, the how of behaviors; leaders tell stories (p. 216).

**White-paper default:** why before how; mission, values, and behavioral standard before operational training (WP p. 18). Silent on the what.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most structured hospitality onboarding opens with a founder or brand story and closes with the station; the middle (goals) is rare at the hourly level.

**Options:** (a) all three on day one, in the book's order; (b) why on day one, how through the sequence, what at the first check-in; (c) why and the structure on day one, the what folded into the shift brief (2.3.7) from the first observed service. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** a day one so full the why is rushed; a "why" that is really a "how" (procedures dressed as values).

**A finished answer:** three named parts, an order, and a place for each.

**Needs agreement from:** no one.

### 3.3.5 Decide whether the founding documents must be finished before any cohort start date, and whether the two leads are exempt

**The question:** if 2.1.17 is not finished when the cohort's start dates arrive, do the dates move or does the cohort hear an unfinished why?

**Why it matters now:** this is a calendar rule, and 3.2.34 needs it now. It is also the first test of whether "why before how" is a rule or a preference.

**Openers:**
- It is six weeks before the dinner gate. The principles are at version 0.8 and the mission has two candidate sentences. The first cohort starts Monday. What happens?
- Sideways: the earlier work made the same trade for compensation (3.2.18: the architecture before the leads' interviews). Did that deadline hold? What did holding it cost?
- The two leads arrive early partly to help finish the founding work. Are they onboarded on a draft, and is that fine because they are writing it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Gate on the whole founding document, on the mission and principles only, or no gate (cons. 3)?
- If gated, who calls the slip and where is it recorded (the training-infrastructure line, 3.3.28, or the hiring calendar)?
- If not gated, when does the cohort hear the finished version, and does hearing two versions cost anything?

**What the book says:** the why session covers the mission and the story, and the operating principles (p. 216); founding documents give every system a common purpose (p. 71).

**White-paper default:** why precedes how (WP p. 18), which implies the why exists first. Silent on the failure case.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies that write their principles after hiring commonly report that the first cohort's version becomes the real one regardless of what is later published; Stripe's mission arrived as a phrase people kept quoting (pp. 73 to 74).

**Options:** (a) gate on the founding document, leads exempt; (b) no gate; (c) gate on mission and principles only. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** a gate agreed here that 2.1's calendar cannot meet; an exemption for the leads that quietly becomes an exemption for the first wave.

**A finished answer:** a rule with its cost stated, and the leads' case settled.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic, since it can move the opening.

### 3.3.6 Decide who delivers the why session, and for how long a founder does it in person

**The question:** who tells the story, to every wave and every later hire, and when does it stop being Brandon?

**Why it matters now:** the deliverer's calendar becomes a start-date condition (3.3.16).

**Openers:**
- The fortieth hire, eight months after opening, starts on a Tuesday morning. Who is in the room with them for the first hour, and what do they say?
- Sideways: you have been telling this story to the build audience on camera for months (WP p. 29). Is the internal telling the same story? What is in it that never goes on camera?
- Dominic telling the why. Say what that sounds like. Does it need to sound like yours?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Brandon only, either founder, or a founder then the Maitre d (cons. 3)?
- What triggers the handoff: a date, the first mechanism reset, the successor having delivered it beside a founder N times?
- Which part may a domain lead deliver (the structure from the person's position outward), and what does the mentor do in the room (present, silent)?

**What the book says:** leadership should take part, ideally in a recurring session, telling stories of the principles in action; stories are what land (p. 216).

**White-paper default:** Brandon is the public voice and the origin of the operating concepts (WP p. 3); the Maitre d owns team development (WP p. 10). Silent on the handoff.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Founder-led orientations are common in the first year and commonly fall away without a named successor; companies that keep them tend to record the founder once and pair the recording with a live leader, which sits against 3.3.19 here.

**Options:** (a) Brandon until a named successor; (b) either founder; (c) a founder for the first period, then the Maitre d. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** "Brandon, indefinitely," which makes every start date depend on one calendar; a recording standing in for the person.

**A finished answer:** a deliverer, a handoff trigger, a successor or a parked item with a closer.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic if he is a deliverer.

### 3.3.7 Decide what is asked of a person before their first paid hour

**The question:** beyond the paperwork the law requires, is anything required of a new hire before they are on the clock?

**Why it matters now:** the arrival note (3.3.39) says it in one line, and the first shift's length depends on it.

**Openers:**
- The arrival note lands in a new runner's inbox. It has a link to three modules. Is that homework?
- Sideways: the book's line is "prep work happens asynchronously." Whose prep? Read it both ways.
- WP p. 19: "if it matters, Sŏn pays for it. Employees should not subsidize it." Does time count?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Nothing required; modules required unpaid; modules offered and paid if done (cons. 4)?
- If offered and paid, can payroll hold a pre-start paid category, and has counsel seen it (0.1)?
- What does day one then have to carry (accounts, tour, the why session, the range read aloud)?

**What the book says:** paperwork and equipment are handled before the program starts so the program is not spent on them (p. 217).

**White-paper default:** the house's prep precedes the first clock-in: information delivered, expectations set, mentor connected, locker and uniform ready (WP p. 18). Silent on anything asked of the person.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Unpaid pre-start training is a common source of wage claims in hospitality in the United States; the safer common practice is either nothing required or pre-start time paid.

**Options:** (a) nothing required; (b) modules required, unpaid; (c) modules offered and paid. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** "optional" reading that everyone understands is expected.

**A finished answer:** one sentence for the arrival note.

**Needs agreement from:** counsel's read if (b) or (c).

### 3.3.8 Decide the operational training sequence and how the paired-service count is set per seat

**The question:** what are the steps from arrival to holding a seat alone, and who sets how many paired services a given seat needs?

**Why it matters now:** the candidate sheet shows the training plan with pay dates at the first interview (WP p. 18), so the sequence must exist before the first posting.

**Openers:**
- Walk a new server from their first module to their first solo table, step by step, at the speed it would really happen. Where does it break?
- Sideways: Crenn's complaint is two shifts of shadowing (pp. 216 to 217). The white paper says the same (WP pp. 18 to 19). What did the shadowing you received actually teach, and what did you learn only by failing at a table?
- A runner and a server. Same number of paired services? A line cook?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The five steps (modules, walkthrough, observed, paired, solo) as 3.2.24 has them; the book's looser weeks-one-and-two; or the five steps with one fixed count (cons. 5)?
- Who sets the count per seat: the domain lead, with a written reason on the seat's plan (3.3.36)? Who may change it?
- How does the schedule carry two bodies in one seat for paired services, and who pays attention when it cannot?

**What the book says:** function-specific programs start in week one and run into week two; training is a continuation, not a one-time event (pp. 216 to 217).

**White-paper default:** competency validation; no one touches a table until ready (WP p. 19). Silent on steps.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. High-end dining rooms commonly run a trail of several shifts per position, with the count varying by room and by position and ending in a test; kitchens commonly run a station handoff of days to weeks depending on the station.

**Options:** (a) five steps, count per seat; (b) the book's shape; (c) five steps, one count. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** a count set by what the schedule can afford rather than what the seat needs; a sequence written for the floor and assumed for the kitchen.

**A finished answer:** the steps in order, the count rule, and a sentence a candidate can read.

**Needs agreement from:** the chef partner for the kitchen's steps.

### 3.3.9 Decide what "service-ready" means and how the readiness read runs

**The question:** what exactly clears a person to hold a seat alone, who scores it, and what happens when they are not yet ready?

**Why it matters now:** it is the gate the white paper promises (WP p. 19), and 4.1.14 needs to know whether assessors are a pool.

**Openers:**
- A server's readiness read. Who is standing there with the form, and did that person teach them? Say why it matters or does not.
- Sideways: 3.2.37 already decided the practical's assessor neither teaches it nor decides the hire. Is the readiness read the same principle, or is a new hire different from a candidate?
- Someone gets "not yet" twice on the same rows. What is the first question, about them or about the module?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Binary outcome with an independent assessor, a scored form read by the lead, or mentor and lead jointly (cons. 5)?
- "Service-ready" as one seat in one period, or something broader?
- Pre-opening, the read happens in a training service and reads procedure, not load. Is a separate load read after the first month of real service part of the plan (3.3.10)?

**What the book says:** the manager knows when the hire is ramped; the sidebar warns that a new person can look ready on procedure and not on judgment (pp. 216, 221 to 224).

**White-paper default:** advancement is a competency conversation with the appropriate lead who signs off (WP p. 19); assessments schedule with the domain lead (WP p. 11). Silent on the assessor's separation.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Certification systems in aviation and healthcare separate instructor from examiner as a rule; restaurants rarely do, and the lead's sign-off is the norm.

**Options:** (a) binary, independent assessor, repeat escalates to infrastructure; (b) scored form, the lead; (c) joint mentor and lead. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** a "binary" outcome with a hidden score; "service-ready" drifting to mean "fully trained."

**A finished answer:** a one-line definition, an assessor rule, an outcome form, and the "not yet" path.

**Needs agreement from:** the chef partner for kitchen reads.

### 3.3.10 Decide the ninety-day plan's form: its goal, milestones, and what it refuses

**The question:** is the plan a document the lead writes or a set of milestones the page tracks, what is its goal, and what may never appear in it?

**Why it matters now:** 2.3.11's person page and 3.3.41's build depend on the answer; the candidate sees the plan's shape at the first interview.

**Openers:**
- Write a new cook's ninety-day goal in one sentence. Now check: is it a deliverable or a state?
- Sideways: the earlier work threw out the 30/60/90 deliverables plan because a cook's first thirty days produce a station reliably held, not a deliverable. Does that hold for the Maitre d too?
- The plan says "probation" nowhere. Does a candidate believe that?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Tracked milestones with an end-state goal; the book's lead-written plan; or milestones for hourly seats and a written plan for leads (cons. 6)?
- Fixed ninety days, or shortened by an early solo?
- The exclusions: ratings, free text, "fit," pay, probation. All of them?
- Is anything in the plan new to the person compared with the candidate sheet? Name the exceptions (tasted knowledge, the tools).

**What the book says:** the manager decides what the hire needs in the first weeks; the first meeting covers initial priorities and flags the career conversation (pp. 216, 219).

**White-paper default:** a ninety-day plan with expectations, goals, check-ins, course correction; milestones documented from the start; no surprises (WP pp. 18 to 19). Silent on form.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Knowledge-work companies commonly use 30/60/90 plans with deliverables; hospitality commonly uses a training checklist signed off per station. The milestone form sits between them.

**Options:** (a) tracked milestones; (b) the book's document; (c) split by seat class. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** a milestone that is really a rating ("demonstrates warmth"); a goal only the lead can see met.

**A finished answer:** the goal form, the milestone list, the length rule, the exclusions, and the "nothing new" check with its exceptions.

**Needs agreement from:** no one.

### 3.3.11 Decide what each check-in inside the plan covers

**The question:** with 2.3.10's intervals fixed, what is each check-in in the plan for, so no lead has to remember?

**Why it matters now:** 3.3.41 writes a brief per check-in into the check-in kit; the plan's data (3.3.27) comes from these.

**Openers:**
- Week two. The lead has fifteen minutes with a new server. What is the one question worth asking that early?
- Sideways: which check-in in your own first ninety days anywhere would have changed something if it had happened?
- The week-four check-in was proposed as the place where a new person says where the module is wrong about the floor. Who receives that?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Take the six foci as proposed (the house's delivery; paired services and module gaps; the read line by line; the load read and range use; the development path; closing), or change them?
- What does no check-in carry (a verdict, pay, probation, manner)?
- Where does the plan's cadence hand to the standing cadence (2.3.10)?

**What the book says:** the first meeting sets mutual expectations; the career conversation follows in a few months (pp. 218 to 219); course correction is the point of check-ins (p. 217).

**White-paper default:** regular check-ins and course correction (WP p. 18); feedback runs both directions (WP p. 19).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Structured onboarding commonly fixes check-ins at 30, 60, and 90 days with a set agenda; the two-week rhythm is less common and closer to a probationary review, which is what the exclusions guard against.

**Options:** (a) the six foci as proposed; (b) the book's single first meeting plus the standing 1:1; (c) the six foci with the development-path conversation moved to 5.x. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** a focus that asks for the lead's impression; a check-in that has no question the person can answer.

**A finished answer:** one focus per check-in, the refusals, the handoff point.

**Needs agreement from:** no one.

### 3.3.12 Decide the plan's extension rule and where an unmet goal goes

**The question:** can a plan run past ninety days, on what grounds, and what happens when the goal is still not met?

**Why it matters now:** it is the seam between this chunk and 5.8 and 5.10, and the place where the refusal list from 3.3.2 is most tempting to break.

**Openers:**
- Week twelve. A cook has not passed the read. The reason is that the Korean-language module for their station never existed. What happens? Now the same week, the module existed and they did the work and still are not there. What happens?
- Sideways: the white paper's first question is "what allowed this to happen," not "who failed" (WP p. 17). At what point does that question run out?
- If the plan can never conclude that someone should leave, who can, and when do they hear about it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- One extension only for a house failure, then 5.8; extensions at the lead's discretion; no extension (cons. 6)?
- Who grants it and with whom in the room (the lead and the person, at a check-in)?
- What is written down when the goal is unmet: a fact, or a recommendation?

**What the book says:** most hiring mistakes surface months in as a performance issue and are handled as one (p. 224); course correction is the plan's purpose (p. 217).

**White-paper default:** blame the process, fix the process (WP p. 17); performance systems develop rather than judge (WP p. 19). Silent on extensions.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Probation periods are commonly extendable once by the manager; the distinction between an extension for the house's failure and one for the person's is rarely written down.

**Options:** (a) once, for a house failure, then 5.8; (b) lead's discretion; (c) none. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** an extension that is really a soft probation; a "house failure" category so broad nothing is ever the person's.

**A finished answer:** the rule, the grantor, the grounds, the count, and the sentence that routes the rest to 5.8 and 5.10.

**Needs agreement from:** no one; 5.8 and 5.10 inherit it.

### 3.3.13 Decide who may be a mentor and how one is named

**The question:** who is eligible to mentor, who names them, when, and by what matching rule?

**Why it matters now:** the mentor is named at offer and appears on the arrival note; 4.1.14 needs the roster's rules.

**Openers:**
- The best server you ever worked beside. Would they have been a good mentor? Say why, and notice whether the reason is about them or about how they treated the newest person.
- Sideways: WP p. 18 says "a proven cultural steward rather than simply the strongest performer." Name the difference in one sentence a lead could use.
- A lead matches a new hire with a mentor because "they'll get along." What does that rule select for over a year?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Eligibility: closed their own plan with the goal held, no unresolved conduct issue, mentor module done, same or adjacent seat, said yes. Add or drop any?
- The namer: the domain lead, from the roster, at offer; never themselves, the assessor, off-roster, or someone already carrying an open plan. Hold all of those?
- Matching on language and schedule overlap only, never rapport?
- No eligible mentor: no start date, logged as infrastructure?

**What the book says:** a buddy from the team and a guide for "how does this place really work" (p. 219).

**White-paper default:** a proven cultural steward for every new person, connected before arrival (WP p. 18).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Buddy programs commonly fail on load (the same three people mentor everyone) and on matching by affinity; programs that last usually cap open pairings per mentor and rotate.

**Options:** (a) the rules as proposed; (b) the lead's choice, no roster; (c) the roster with matching left to the lead. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** an eligibility rule that no one meets for the first year; "cultural steward" becoming a personality read.

**A finished answer:** the eligibility list, the naming rule, the matching rule, the no-mentor outcome.

**Needs agreement from:** the chef partner for kitchen mentors.

- Same sequence, shortened, no new ninety-day plan, inside the existing check-in cadence; or a full plan for a domain change (cons. 11)?
- Who sets the paired count for someone already on staff: the receiving lead?
- What is recorded on a pass, and who signs across domains (4.1.11)?

**What the book says:** internal moves deserve the same attention as new hires (p. 217).

**White-paper default:** cross-training is career development; internal succession is the goal (WP p. 19); the next skill set unlocks after a set period and the assessment auto-schedules (WP p. 11).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Cross-training in restaurants is usually informal; formal internal transfers in larger companies commonly get a shortened onboarding with the receiving manager's checklist.

**Options:** (a) shortened sequence, no repeat of the why; (b) full plan for a domain change, shorter for a period.

**Watch for:** a period plan so light that the early morning opens on dinner's habits, which is the failure the white paper opens with (WP p. 2).

**A finished answer:** the steps, the cadence, the why session's status, the record on a pass.

**Needs agreement from:** the chef partner for kitchen moves.

### 3.3.30 Decide how a change of domain lead is handled for a person

**The question:** when a person's lead changes, what happens between the old lead, the new lead, and the person?

**Why it matters now:** it is after opening in practice, but the person page (2.3.11) has to be able to carry it from the start.

**Openers:**
- The Maitre d leaves in year two. Twelve servers get a new lead. What does the new lead know about each of them on their first day, and from where?
- Sideways: the book's transition meeting has the old manager, the new manager, and the person in one room (p. 247). What gets said in that room that would not be written on the person's page? Should it be?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The book's three steps; the new lead reads the page before the next check-in and reviews it with the person; a mix (cons. 11)?
- Anything passed between leads outside the person-visible record? Nothing, or a named list?

**What the book says:** preview the move, hold a joint transition meeting, the new manager follows up; agenda covers current work, strengths and development, ambitions, ways of working (p. 218, p. 247).

**White-paper default:** the system retains what people knew when they leave (WP p. 8).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Manager handoffs commonly happen as a private conversation between the two managers; the person is rarely in the room. The book's version is the less common one.

**Options:** (a) the book's meeting; (b) the page read and the next check-in; (c) both.

**Watch for:** a "nothing passes between leads" rule that leads honor in writing and break in the walk-in.

**A finished answer:** the mechanism and the rule on what passes.

**Needs agreement from:** no one.

### 3.3.31 Decide whether the internal mobility survey runs, or what existing records answer it

**The question:** the book asks five questions at every team change to catch "bad mobility." Does Sŏn ask them, or do its records already answer?

**Why it matters now:** it decides what 4.1.11 and the transfer records need to capture.

**Openers:**
- Three servers have moved to the early morning in two months. Running to it, or running from dinner? How would you know?
- Sideways: the standing upward question in every check-in was proposed as answering this for everyone, movers or not. Does it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The survey as written; a shorter form; answered from the unlock record, the transfer record, and the upward question (cons. 11)?
- Who reads it, and on what rhythm (the domain read, 2.3.4)?

**What the book says:** five questions to the person, both managers, and the people team; read over time; do not over-index on one or two unhappy answers (pp. 217 to 218).

**White-paper default:** feedback both directions with the loop closed (WP p. 22); internal promotion rate tracked (WP p. 23).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Internal mobility surveys are rare outside large companies; the signal is usually read from transfer requests and exit interviews.

**Options:** (a) the survey; (b) shorter; (c) existing records.

**Watch for:** a record that captures the move but not the reason.

**A finished answer:** the answer and the reader.

**Needs agreement from:** no one.
