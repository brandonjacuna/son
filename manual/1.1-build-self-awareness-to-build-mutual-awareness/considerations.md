# 1.1 Build self-awareness to build mutual awareness: considerations

What the white paper holds on each topic, what the old extraction work argued that is worth keeping, the options, and where Sŏn may diverge from the book. In the book's order. "WP p. N" is the white paper; "pp. N" is the book. This is a Chapter 1 chunk: everything here prepares a conversation whose answers are each founder's own. Nothing in this file is a founder's value, style, strength, or working habit.

## 1. Where this sits: trust, and the founders' particular case

**The book.** The four principles of Chapter 1 are about one thing, building trust; self-awareness is the first and underpins the other three (pp. 36 to 37). Her frame is a manager and their reports, who see each other daily and correct by observation.

**What the white paper holds.** The founding team is three equal principals with separate domains, none reporting to another: the concept, the operating systems, the experience, and the public voice; the capital, the financial architecture, and the technology build; the culinary vision (WP pp. 03, 05). Trust toward the team is extended by the structure in advance rather than earned one conversation at a time: a server's judgment and generosity range are pre-authorized before the first shift (WP pp. 13, 26), and "authority lives in the domain" (WP p. 07). The white paper is silent on trust among the founders themselves.

**Kept from the old work.** Two observations. First, co-founders with separate domains will rarely watch each other work: one is in the room and on camera, one in the model and the build, one in the kitchen. A manager and reports can correct by observation; these three cannot, so whatever the book expects observation to supply has to be supplied some other way. Second, the founders' self-awareness has a structural target the book does not name: the white paper says "restaurant founders build systems for themselves without realizing it, then blame employees when those systems do not work for anyone else. Sŏn has done it too, which is how the team knows the trap" (WP p. 17). A founder who talks to think will tend to build meetings; one who thinks to talk will tend to build documents. The house needs to know which it is getting.

**The boundary the old work held, kept in full.** Each founder's values, work style, strengths and failure modes, and how each wants to be disagreed with are not in any source and are not written by anyone but the founder. The team may propose a question; it may not record an answer. An earlier attempt did, and it was undone.

## 2. Values

**The book.** Values drive behavior, often without the person knowing why (Eli and transparency, pp. 38 to 39). Naming a value lets a conversation move to the level of the value rather than the decision, which she calls going meta (p. 40). The exercise: imagine yourself at eighty; ten values, then five, then three; for each, the activities that show it, a positive outcome, and a trade-off (pp. 64 to 66). She adds that a team can share its top three and that shared values "can then become your mission" (p. 64).

**What the white paper holds.** The company's intent is stated throughout (labor as an asset, employees as the users, blame the process, no hierarchy of importance, WP pp. 16 to 20), and orientation puts the mission and the values before operational training (WP p. 18). Those are the company's. The white paper holds no founder's personal values, and no source does.

**Kept from the old work.** Two design points for the exercise. Values are more useful stated as activities or decision rules than as single words: "excellence" says little; "I do not open a service period until the one before it is steady" is something the other partners can work with. And the trade-off is the part that matters; a value with no trade-off has not been thought through. One practical reason the founders' values matter to the house: when a founder is a blocker, it is usually a value being defended without being named, and three partners who can say "I am holding this because of X" can negotiate at that level.

**Options for the values exercise (feeds 1.1.3).**
- (a) Each founder does the book's exercise alone, keeps the working private, and brings only the three values with activities and trade-offs to the document and the exchange. Commits Sŏn to the exercise as a personal instrument and nothing more.
- (b) The founders do it together in one sitting, as she suggests for teams (p. 64). Commits the founders to sharing the working, not only the result, and risks the values converging in the room.
- (c) Skip the exercise; each founder states their values from experience. Commits Sŏn to values named from self-image rather than from the exercise's reduction, which is the failure she warns about.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book offers turning a team's shared values into its mission. The white paper already carries the company's intent, and 2.1 owns the mission and the principles. The old work declined the aggregation: the founders' personal values are theirs and are not summed into the company's. That is a choice for Brandon, not a rule; it belongs with 2.1.2 and 2.1.5 rather than here.

## 3. Work style: the instruments, and their use beyond the founders

**The book.** The most familiar axis is introvert to extrovert; her test is whether you talk to think or think to talk (p. 40). She maps most assessments onto two axes, introverted to extroverted and task to people, producing four working styles, Analyzer, Director, Promoter, Collaborator, none better than another; fit depends on the moment (pp. 41 to 44). She names DiSC, Myers-Briggs, and Insights Discovery, and credits the last for giving her a vocabulary for herself (pp. 44 to 46). The exercise: place yourself on the grid for home and for work (p. 67). Later in the book she gives a caution about typologies becoming labels used on colleagues, which is the reason the team-use question is here.

**What the white paper holds.** The floor's vocabulary for difference is the two strands: the outward-facing strand's intelligence is "relational and expressive and emotionally attuned under load," the inward-facing strand's is "architectural and coordinated and timing-precise," and they are "different jobs requiring different types of intelligence," not a promotion sequence (WP pp. 10 to 11). Hiring reads documented competencies "rather than gut feeling" (WP p. 18). Progression is documented competencies anyone can see (WP p. 11). The founders' domains are complementary by design, and one founder's domain "did not come from hospitality" (WP pp. 03, 05). The white paper names no personality assessment for anyone.

**Kept from the old work.**
- Placing oneself three times, at work, at home, and under load, rather than twice: the under-load placement is where founder collisions happen, and the differences between the three are the useful part.
- The grid is shared vocabulary, not a verdict. It does not decide what a person can do.
- A refinement offered as an input, not a conclusion: the common claim that service selects for Directors and Promoters describes the outward-facing strand; the inward-facing strand is where the Analyzer's intelligence lives in service, and the strand structure already reserves that place.
- The strand question as an addition to the grid: which strand's intelligence the writer recognizes as their own, and whether they hold both.

**Options for the instruments (1.1.3).**
- (a) The book's three exercises with the old work's additions: three placements; a trade-off and a guardrail per strength; a column for whether the house depends on the strength in the founder personally. Commits Sŏn to a home-made instrument that costs an afternoon and no money, and to the kit carrying it.
- (b) The book's three exercises exactly as written (pp. 64 to 68). Commits Sŏn to the same at lower effort, and loses the under-load placement and the dependency column.
- (c) A formal assessment (Insights Discovery, DiSC, or similar) for the founders and later the leads, with a facilitated debrief, in addition to the exercises. Commits Sŏn to a cost, a facilitator, and a proprietary vocabulary the kit would have to license or paraphrase; gives the founders an outside read they did not write themselves.
- (d) No instrument; the conversation alone. Commits Sŏn to answers from self-image, which is what the exercises exist to correct.

**Options for use beyond the founders (1.1.4).**
- (a) Founders and leads only, inside their own documents, with one upward use: a lead may refer to their own placement when talking to a founder about themselves. The four names never appear on a person's file, a schedule note, a review line, or in a floor conversation about someone else. Commits Sŏn to the strands as the only vocabulary for difference on the floor, and to hourly staff never being asked to write the document.
- (b) Founders and leads only, no exceptions. Commits Sŏn to the same with a simpler rule and loses the one use the old work thought harmless.
- (c) Shared vocabulary for the whole team, taught at orientation, as she describes at Stripe (p. 45). Commits Sŏn to a second vocabulary for difference beside the strands, and to policing its use on a roster that changes nightly; her own caution about labels applies in full.
- (d) No typology at all, including among the founders. Commits Sŏn to describing difference in plain words only, and loses the shorthand the book says makes the conversation possible.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book treats the grid as a team vocabulary. Sŏn already has one for the floor, and it is about the work, not the person. Whichever option is chosen, the rule should say explicitly that the grid is never used in hiring, strand or role assignment, review, or pay, because the white paper's hiring and progression systems are built on documented competency and a typology would contradict them (WP pp. 11, 18).

## 4. Skills, capabilities, and the founding team's gaps

**The book.** Skills are tactical and demonstrable; capabilities are the innate ability to apply skills in a new situation (p. 46). Capability generalizes; skill does not (pp. 47 to 48). Your biggest strength is your biggest weakness, and teams become mirrors of their managers, so a manager who does not know their blind spot hires it into the team. The fix is to hire and elevate complements, not duplicates, and to build personal mechanisms that force you out of your comfort zone, like her rule of asking a question before acting (pp. 48 to 50). The exercise: list strengths, split into innate and acquired skills and capabilities (pp. 67 to 68).

**What the white paper holds.** The house's answer to the first-time-team objection is architecture, "a code-bearing organization built so it does not depend on either founder's memory" (WP pp. 05 to 06). Knowledge that lives in people is the first failure mode named (WP p. 13). A second unit is handed a system, not a founder's memory (WP p. 16). Any qualified team member can create training modules and is paid to teach (WP p. 19). The white paper does not apply any of this to the founders' own strengths, and it holds no inventory of them.

**Kept from the old work.** Two additions to the book's inventory. The cost sentence, "this strength is also a weakness when...", completed for each strength, with the guardrail built against it or "none yet." And the dependency column: does the house currently depend on this strength in the founder personally, or has it been moved into the system? A strength the house depends on personally is the founders' own version of the failure the white paper names, and every such strength is an item for the list in section 8. The innate-versus-acquired split has a house use too: acquired strengths are the teachable ones, and the learning platform will want to know which those are.

**The founding team's gaps.** The book's remedy for mirrors is to hire complements. The founding domains are complementary by design (WP pp. 03, 05, 09); whether the people are complementary in how they work, and what strengths the founding team lacks entirely, is not in any source. The exchange (1.1.10) is where that is found, and its output belongs to 4.1 (what the two leads' role descriptions should carry) and 3.1 (what recruiting looks for). This is the concrete house consequence of the chapter and is easy to lose in a conversation about personalities.

## 5. The working-with-me document: form and content

**The book.** For anyone who fails the self-awareness test (pp. 50 to 51), she points to two exercises: writing a working-with-me document, and career conversations with reports. The document is hers to describe in later material; here it is named as the next step.

**What the white paper holds.** Nothing on the document. Three facts it holds shape what a Sŏn version would carry: each founder owns a domain outright and is accountable to its outcomes (WP pp. 03, 07 to 08); the house must not depend on a founder's memory (WP pp. 05 to 06, 13); and the founders hold the standard for systems built for users who are not the founders (WP p. 17).

**Kept from the old work.** The argument for a written document over conversation alone: it is the book's own move (move what a person knows out of their head and into something others can read) applied to the founder's self-knowledge, so the other partners do not learn it by collision. Three jobs a Sŏn version would do that a standard one does not: it is the seam between domains that never overlap; it is the first document an incoming chef partner reads that is about the people rather than the concept; and it is an inventory of what still lives only in a founder's head, which is what makes it a founding instrument rather than a personality profile.

The eleven prompts the old work proposed, each with what it asks and what a weak answer looks like:
- A. My seat and its edges: what I own, where it ends (the three or four decisions nearest the edge that belong to someone else), what I am accountable to. Weak: a title and a list copied from the white paper, no edges.
- B. What lives in my head: what I know that the house depends on and that is not written anywhere the house can read it; for each, what breaks if I am unreachable for a month and by when it will be in the system. Weak: "most of it is in the white paper," or a list with no dates.
- C. How I work: the placements and what the differences mean for others; talk to think or think to talk; how I take in information; what gives and drains energy; hours, and which service periods I will and will not be present for. Weak: "a mix of all four," no hours, no presence by service period.
- D. How I decide: in my domain, what I decide alone and tell no one, alone and tell after, consult on first, and treat as joint; what I need to decide; how fast, and what others should do when they need faster; how I change my mind; what a decision from me looks like. Weak: "I'm collaborative," no categories, no speed.
- E. How I disagree, and how to disagree with me: how I raise a problem with another founder's work; how I want to hear I am wrong, in what form, setting, from whom, how soon; what I do when angry and what others should do; what I will and will not say in front of the team. Weak: "I welcome feedback," no story.
- F. My strengths, and what each costs: from the inventory, with the cost sentence and the guardrail; which strengths the house depends on in me personally. Weak: costs that are secretly strengths.
- G. My three values at work: from the exercise, with activity, outcome, and trade-off; where I expect them to collide with the others' or with a house decision. Weak: three words from a list.
- H. What I look like under load: the behaviors others will see before I notice; what tips me in; what helps; what I want the others to do, and what I will do when I see it in them. Weak: "I handle pressure well."
- I. How to reach me: which channel for what; honest response times; hours I am not reachable; during a service period I am working, what reaches me and what does not. Weak: "text me anytime."
- J. What I need from each of you, and what you can expect from me: two or three things I need from each other domain, in what form, how often; what each can expect from me, as checkable commitments. Weak: needs as complaints, expectations as virtues.
- K. What I do not know yet: the prompts I could not answer honestly and why; the date I will revisit this.

Prompt D deserves a note: it is each founder's draft of the rule the white paper does not hold. The white paper gives one joint decision (whether lunch is ever built, WP p. 36) and no general rule for what is domain-owned, what is joint, and how a deadlock among three equal partners resolves. The drafts are input to 2.1.13; the document does not set the rule. Prompt E is the personal half of 1.2.1. Prompt I is reachability only; when the partners meet is 2.3's.

The old work also named what the document must refuse to be, which is worth carrying as a set of commitments rather than rules: not a seat description (2.1 writes those; prompt A points to one when it exists); not a statement of company values; not a review instrument or scorecard, and never used as one; not binding on the other partners (what a founder asks for in J is a request, not a term); not the partners' rhythm or the decision rule; no financial figures ("it lives in the model"); no performed conviction, which means describing the self one is rather than the self one intends to be; and never filled in on anyone's behalf.

**Options for the form (1.1.1).**
- (a) A written document each founder writes alone on one shared structure, exchanged among the partners. Commits Sŏn to a document that has to be kept current, to comparable documents (edges, decision drafts, dependencies side by side), and to a kit the leads follow.
- (b) A facilitated conversation with no document, perhaps with an outside facilitator. Commits Sŏn to nothing written, which means a new partner or lead learns the founders by being told or by collision, and the inventory of what lives in a founder's head is not made.
- (c) A shared formal assessment plus a debrief conversation. Commits Sŏn to a vendor's vocabulary and a report the founders did not write; covers work style well and edges, decisions, and the inventory not at all.
- (d) A document followed by a conversation on it (1.1.10). Commits Sŏn to both costs and is what the old work assumed.

**Options for the content (1.1.2).**
- (a) The book's three components only: values, work style, strengths and gaps. Commits Sŏn to a short document that says how each founder is and nothing about the seams between domains or what only the founder knows.
- (b) The eleven prompts above. Commits Sŏn to two to four pages per founder, an afternoon of exercises first, and the prompts B and D feeding 1.1.12 and 2.1.13.
- (c) A chosen subset, decided prompt by prompt. Commits Sŏn to deciding, for each addition, whether the house needs it now.

**Revision.** The old work proposed: dated and versioned; revised when the seat changes materially and at minimum after dinner's first quarter, when each founder will have learned how they work in a room that exists. An alternative is a fixed annual date. Either is a one-line choice inside 1.1.2.

## 6. Who reads it

**What the white paper holds.** The two leads are parallel co-leads, neither reporting to the other, serving the same center (WP p. 10). The candidate receives at the first interview a sheet with "communication expectations" among other things (WP p. 18). A ninety-day plan with regular check-ins runs for every new person (WP p. 18). Silent on what a lead is told about how the founders work.

**Kept from the old work.** A full version among the partners; a shorter version issued to each lead when seated, keeping the prompts a lead can use (the seat and its edges, how I work, how I decide, how I disagree, under load, how to reach me, what I need and what you can expect, rewritten for the lead as reader) and withholding the ones that are the partners' business (what lives in my head, strengths and costs, values, what I do not know yet). The issued version reaches a lead before that lead's first check-in with that founder. Each lead is asked to write their own on the same structure, for the founders and the other lead, adding one prompt the founders' version does not need: how they will discuss development and progression with the people in their domain. It is never issued below the leads, and an hourly person is never asked to write one.

**Options (1.1.5).**
- (a) Partners in full; each lead a shorter version, before their first check-in; each lead writes their own. Commits Sŏn to two versions of each founder's document and to the kit carrying both.
- (b) Partners only. Commits Sŏn to the leads learning the founders by observation and instruction, and to the leads not writing one.
- (c) One document for partners and leads alike. Commits each founder to writing for the leads' eyes throughout, which changes what gets said in the prompts about the other partners.
- (d) A version for every hire, as part of orientation. Commits Sŏn to a founder document in front of a line cook on week one; the white paper's orientation is about the mission and the standard, not the founders (WP p. 18).

## 7. The chef partner's timing

**What the white paper holds.** The chef partner is an equal founding partner who owns the culinary vision, "freed from carrying the rest of the business alone" (WP pp. 03, 05). Whether lunch is ever built is a decision among all three partners (WP p. 36). The white paper does not say when or how the three come to know each other, and the chef search is named as a real event in the build (WP p. 29).

**Kept from the old work.** The timing question only bites for the chef. Brandon and Dominic are already partners and can write theirs now. The chef's preamble, whenever it is used, is written to an unfilled seat as questions, not as a description, and it should tell the incoming partner plainly that the founders have an open question about how the chef seat's authority is described, which 2.1 resolves with the person seated.

**Options (1.1.9).**
- (a) Diligence: the incoming chef partner reads both founders' documents and writes their own before the partnership is signed. Commits Sŏn to showing a candidate partner two personal documents during a search, and to the chef writing about a house they have not yet worked in.
- (b) Onboarding: the chef partner reads the founders' on arrival and writes theirs some weeks in, once they have seen the house. Commits Sŏn to signing a partnership before the three have exchanged, and to the chef's document being about a real room.
- (c) Split: the founders' documents are read before signing; the chef's is written after. Commits Sŏn to asymmetric disclosure for a period, which the chef partner should be told is deliberate.

## 8. What lives only in a founder's head

**What the white paper holds.** "The mark of these founders is a company built to run without them" (WP p. 06). The memory failure mode: institutional knowledge lives in people, every departure takes knowledge with it, nothing compounds (WP p. 13). When a key person leaves, the knowledge "does not leave. It lives in the system" (WP p. 14). The claim is made about the whole house and is never checked against the founders themselves; the white paper holds no instrument for that.

**Kept from the old work.** Prompt B, consolidated across the founders into one running list: what is still only in a founder's head, what breaks if they are unreachable, and when it moves into the system. The old work called it a knowledge-transfer register; in plain words it is a to-do list for the system. Concrete to the point of discomfort: "the concept" is not an item; "the reason the host stand was removed and what replaces its functions" is. "The model" is not an item; "the labor assumption by service period and where it came from" is. Include relationships (the vendor who returns one founder's call) and reasons, not only decisions, because the white paper holds many decisions and fewer of the reasons behind them, and the leads will need the reasons when they extend a system. Reviewed on the partners' rhythm (2.3), closed item by item. A later extension tied its movement to a founder-level read of load: an item whose date has passed, or a month with nothing moved, is a signal about the center. What that founder-level read should be is 6.1's question.

**Options.** Keep it as one list across the founders (comparable, reviewable, the direct test of the founding claim); or leave each founder's list inside their own document and review them separately; or drop prompt B and test the claim another way in 2.2. Each is a one-line choice inside 1.1.2 and 1.1.12.

## 9. Where Sŏn may diverge from the book

- The book's unit is a manager and reports who observe each other daily. Sŏn's founders are peers in separate domains who rarely observe each other. Whatever form 1.1.1 chooses has to carry what observation would otherwise supply.
- The book offers shared team values as a route to a mission. 2.1 owns the mission; the founders' personal values can stay personal.
- The book treats the style grid as team vocabulary. Sŏn's floor vocabulary for difference is the strands, and its hiring and progression read documented competency (WP pp. 11, 18), so the grid's reach is a real decision (1.1.4), not an assumption.
- The book's remedy for mirrors is to hire complements. At Sŏn the first two hires are the leads, and the house opens near full size and stays there (chunk 0's standing note), so the founding team's gaps are filled once, early, and carefully, or they are built into the structure for years.
- The book points anyone who fails the self-awareness test to career conversations with reports. The founders have no reports at this level; the peer version is the exchange in 1.1.10, and career conversations belong to 5.1 and 5.4 for the team.
