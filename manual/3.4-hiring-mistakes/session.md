# 3.4 Hiring mistakes: working session guide

One chunk, one short session. Brandon decides; this guide prompts. "Cons. N" points to `considerations.md`; task numbers to `tasks.md`.

## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** Two things. First, what Sŏn does when a hire is wrong early: the grounds, who acts, how fast, what the team is told, and how a minor recurring issue in the first weeks is handled. Second, how Sŏn learns from hiring over time: the retrospective when a hire does not hold, the read on interviewers, the questions asked of the team and of declined candidates, and the join between hiring records and what happens in the first ninety days. A hire that goes wrong months in is a performance question and belongs to 5.8 and 5.10.

**What is already decided upstream.** 3.2.7 names who may end a candidacy outright and on what grounds; the early-reversal rule extends that past the offer. 3.2.30 writes the offer with counsel; whatever this chunk decides about withdrawal goes into it. 3.2.39 and 3.2.41 decide the calibration read and its record; the interviewer read and the join build on them. 3.1.37 builds the pipeline report; time in process and the funnel live there. 3.3.10 to 3.3.12 fix the plan, its check-ins, and the extension rule; 3.3.27 computes the onboarding read; 3.3.28 puts a training-infrastructure line on the leads' review. 2.3.15 sets the employee pulse's rhythm. 0.1 gets counsel's list of employer duties in Texas. 2.1.5 writes the behavioral standard, which stands in for a handbook until one exists.

**Who else must agree.** Counsel on the withdrawal and separation steps (3.4.4). Dominic on anything that touches the applicant system's configuration or a payroll step. The leads, once hired, on the retrospective they will run.

**Why it sits here.** The book closes the hiring chapter with it, and the calendar agrees: the grounds and the withdrawal process must exist before the first offer, the retrospective before the first plan can go unmet, the reads before there is data to read.

Ask where he wants to start. The agenda is a default order.

## 3. Briefs

### 3.4.2 Decide what counts as grounds to reverse a hire before the start date or in the first weeks

**The question:** what, seen between a signed offer and the end of the first weeks, means the hire is over rather than a matter for feedback?

**Why it matters now:** the offer letter is written with counsel before the first hire; the grounds have to be in it or behind it.

**Openers:**
- A cook signs on a Friday. On Sunday they post a photo of the kitchen's build with a caption about the pay. Is that the end?
- Sideways: at the best room you worked in, what did someone do in their first week that should have ended it and did not? What did keeping them cost?
- Read the book's four signals aloud (p. 224). Which one would you hesitate on? Why?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The four signals plus the behavioral standard, written for a candidate to read; a handbook once one exists; only what counsel confirms (cons. 1)?
- Where is the line between grounds and feedback-worthy (3.4.5)? Say it with an example on each side.
- Does the list differ for a lead?

**What the book says:** the four signals; anything violating the handbook or showing extremely poor judgment; the main thing is to do something (pp. 224 to 225).

**White-paper default:** blame the process first, and post-mortems are private (WP p. 17); the behavioral standard is heard before operational training (WP p. 18). Silent on grounds.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most employers rely on at-will employment and an introductory period rather than a written list of early grounds; a written list is the less common, more candid choice.

**Options:** (a) signals plus standard, written; (b) handbook or judgment; (c) counsel's list only. Depth: cons. 1.

**Watch for:** "poor judgment" as a catch-all that means "we changed our minds."

**A finished answer:** a short list in plain words, tied to the standard or a legal duty, with the feedback line drawn.

**Needs agreement from:** counsel's read; the chef partner should hear it for kitchen hires.

### 3.4.3 Decide who acts when grounds exist, how fast, and what the team is told

**The question:** when grounds exist, who makes the call, who reads it second, how quickly, and what does the team hear?

**Why it matters now:** the two leads are parallel (WP p. 10), so a lead's own hire needs a rule; the team note is a template written now.

**Openers:**
- Week one, a server has been rude to two runners in front of customers. The Maitre d hired them. Who decides, and by when?
- Sideways: WP p. 17 says management absorbs blame from staff. When someone leaves in week one, what does the team need to hear so that they do not fill the silence themselves?
- The book says do not overexplain (p. 224). What is the minimum sentence?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The domain lead with a partner as second reader, and a lead's own hire to the partners; both leads jointly; a partner in every case before opening (cons. 1)?
- Speed: inside a day, inside the week?
- The note: sender, sentences, what it never says.

**What the book says:** rescind or terminate swiftly depending on start date and jurisdiction; a short note; a negotiated departure where termination is hard (pp. 224 to 225).

**White-paper default:** authority lives in the domain (WP p. 7); post-mortems are private and process-focused (WP p. 17).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. In most restaurants the general manager decides alone; a second reader is uncommon and is the main protection against a fast wrong call.

**Options:** (a) lead plus partner; (b) both leads; (c) a partner before opening.

**Watch for:** a second reader who is only informed; a note that names a reason.

**A finished answer:** an actor per window, a second reader, a speed, a note template's sender and length, the lead's-own-hire rule.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic if a partner is the second reader.

### 3.4.5 Decide how a minor recurring issue in the first weeks is handled

**The question:** a new hire is late three times in two weeks. What happens, in what words, and where is it written?

**Why it matters now:** the check-in briefs (3.3.41) carry it, and it is where "blame the process first" meets a person who keeps being late.

**Openers:**
- Say the sentence the lead uses. Now say it again without a verdict in it.
- Sideways: the white paper's cooling failure (WP p. 13). What would you check before deciding lateness is about the person: the shift shape, the bus, the call time in a language they read?
- At what count does a pattern stop being a process question?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The check-in line with process causes first and a named count; the book's same-day direct feedback; both (cons. 2)?
- What is recorded and where (a fact on the person page, never a note in a lead's pocket)?
- The handoff to 5.2 and 5.8: at what point, and who says so?

**What the book says:** give the feedback quickly and directly, watch, part ways with speed if it continues (p. 224).

**White-paper default:** no surprises (WP p. 19); blame the process first (WP p. 17).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Attendance policies with a points system are common in hourly work and are commonly experienced as the thing the white paper calls calling burnout a character flaw; the process-first read is the uncommon path.

**Options:** (a) check-in line, causes first, named count; (b) the book's path; (c) both.

**Watch for:** a "process cause" checklist that becomes an excuse machine, or a count so high the team carries the load for months.

**A finished answer:** the path, the words, the record, the count, the handoff.

**Needs agreement from:** no one.

### 3.4.6 Decide the hiring retrospective: what triggers it, who runs it, what it asks, and where it is kept

**The question:** when a hire does not hold, how does Sŏn find out what the hiring process missed, without making it about the person?

**Why it matters now:** the first plan that goes unmet will arrive within months of the first cohort's start, and the retrospective form (3.4.11) has to exist by then.

**Openers:**
- A server's plan closes unmet after the extension, and onboarding was not the cause. Someone has to ask whether the interviews should have seen it. Who, in what room, reading what?
- Sideways: the book's Welch story (p. 225) is about knowing whose read was right. Have you ever known that about yourself as an interviewer? How?
- What is the difference between a retrospective and a blame session, in one sentence a lead could hold onto?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Triggers: an early reversal; an unmet plan after the extension; a departure inside a window. All three (cons. 3)?
- Runner: the hiring manager (3.1.4) with a second reader?
- Questions: the book's five, in Sŏn's words, in the order that checks onboarding causes first?
- Record: lives with the calibration records, readers named, never on the person page?

**What the book says:** the hiring manager runs a quick retrospective and shares it with recruiting (p. 225).

**White-paper default:** post-mortems are private and process-focused; the system gets fixed so it does not happen again to anyone (WP p. 17).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Hiring retrospectives are rare in restaurants; in technology companies they are usually informal and unrecorded. A written, triggered form is uncommon.

**Options:** (a) three triggers, hiring manager plus second reader, fixed questions, record with calibration; (b) the book's informal version; (c) early reversals only. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** a retrospective that only ever finds the onboarding at fault, or only ever the interview.

**A finished answer:** triggers, runner, questions, record and readers.

**Needs agreement from:** the leads, once hired.

### 3.4.7 Decide the interviewer read: whether interviewer judgment is tracked over time, and what may be done with it

**The question:** does Sŏn learn whose hire and no-hire calls hold up, and what happens with that knowledge?

**Why it matters now:** 3.2.41's calibration record has to store the interviewer's recommendation in a form that can be joined later, or the read is impossible from the first hire.

**Openers:**
- Two years in. Sŏn knows that one lead's "strong hire" calls held up and the other's did not. What should happen? What should not?
- Sideways: 3.2.13 already sends an interviewer back to paired interviews. Is that the whole use of this read, or is there another?
- WP p. 18 says reading an answer is a learned skill. What tells the learner whether they have learned?

**Narrowing questions:**
- What is read: volume, decisiveness, agreement with the decision, later plan outcomes of their hires (cons. 4)?
- Who sees it: the calibration read's runner and the interviewer themselves? The leads' review?
- What may change: training, class membership. What may never change: pay, standing. Hold both?
- How is it kept from becoming a ranking (per interviewer, shown to that interviewer, never listed together)?

**What the book says:** interviewer stats from the ATS; Welch's example; interviewer recommendations against longitudinal performance (pp. 225 to 226).

**White-paper default:** interviewers are trained (WP p. 18); the stack captures what memory would lose (WP p. 21). Silent on tracking.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Larger technology companies commonly track interviewer calibration and route outliers to retraining or shadowing; few publish it internally, for the reason 3.4.7's last question names.

**Options:** (a) the full read, seen by the runner and the interviewer, changes training only; (b) volume and decisiveness only; (c) none.

**Watch for:** a read that quietly decides who interviews for lead seats, which 3.2.13's class rule should decide in the open.

**A finished answer:** what is read, from where, who sees it, what it may and may never change.

**Needs agreement from:** no one; 3.2.41 must carry the field.

### 3.4.8 Decide whether candidates are surveyed on the hiring process, declined and hired, from which stage, and what is asked

**The question:** does Sŏn ask the people it said no to how it went, and from which stage?

**Why it matters now:** the first declines happen in the leads' search, before the applicant system has volume; the survey step is configured once (3.1.33).

**Openers:**
- A candidate did the paid practical and was declined. A week later, three questions arrive. What do you want to know from them that no one inside can tell you?
- Sideways: the book calls a rejected candidate's experience a customer experience (p. 226). WP p. 18 says the candidate treated well at the no returns next year. Does a survey help that, or does it feel like one more ask?
- If a declined candidate writes something harsh and true, who reads it, and what changes?

**Narrowing questions:**
- From the first interview onward; from the practical onward; none yet (cons. 7)?
- Questions: a recommend question and two open ones at most?
- Sender and timing: automatic, after the decline message (3.2.21), in the candidate's language?
- Reader and rhythm; and the rule that a response never changes pool standing (3.1.12).

**What the book says:** survey anyone from the first interview onward; Stripe asks whether they would recommend applying and about the process; the results improve the process for everyone after (p. 226).

**White-paper default:** no one is ghosted; a candidate treated well at the no returns (WP p. 18).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Candidate experience surveys are common in larger companies and almost unknown in restaurants; response rates are modest everywhere.

**Options:** (a) from the first interview, three questions; (b) from the practical; (c) none yet.

**Watch for:** a survey sent before the decline message lands; a question that asks the candidate to rate themselves.

**A finished answer:** stage, questions, sender, timing, reader, rhythm, and the pool rule.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic on the applicant system step.

### 3.4.9 Decide the hiring and onboarding questions in the employee pulse

**The question:** which of the book's team questions about hiring does Sŏn ask, given that the onboarding read already computes part of it?

**Why it matters now:** 2.3.15's pulse is being built; questions added later break the series.

**Openers:**
- "The people we've hired in the last six months meet or exceed the caliber of our current team" (p. 226). Ask that of a server in month four. What are they really answering?
- Sideways: which of the book's eight questions can the plan data already answer? Cross those out. What is left?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Caliber and "we act when someone is not delivering," dropping the onboarding question; the full set; none (cons. 5)?
- Scale and rhythm as 2.3.15 has them?
- Reader: the domain read (2.3.4)?

**What the book says:** four engagement prompts and five pulse prompts, 1 to 5 (pp. 225 to 226).

**White-paper default:** employee NPS regularly; engagement across psychological safety and values alignment (WP pp. 22 to 23).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Hiring-quality questions in engagement surveys are common in technology companies; the "we act when someone is not delivering" item is the one most often reported as the lowest score.

**Options:** (a) caliber and acting only; (b) the full set; (c) none.

**Watch for:** a question the team cannot answer without a comparison it does not have.

**A finished answer:** the questions, checked against the onboarding read, with a reader.

**Needs agreement from:** no one.

### 3.4.10 Decide how hiring data is joined to what happens after: which milestones, from which records, and what is refused

**The question:** how does Sŏn learn whether its interviews predict anything, from facts it already records, without surveying managers or mining interview notes?

**Why it matters now:** the join only works if 3.2.41 and 3.3.41 store things in a joinable form from the first hire.

**Openers:**
- The first cohort's plans have closed. Lay the calibration records beside the plan milestones. What question do you ask first?
- Sideways: the book's heavy version reads the word "resilient" in interview notes against later performance (p. 226). WP p. 21 keeps AI off anything load-bearing. Is a machine's read of an interviewer's adjectives load-bearing?
- A manager survey at six weeks asks the lead how the hire is doing. What does the plan block already know that the lead would be guessing at?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Computed from plan facts against hiring records, per seat class and stage, no names; the book's manager survey; both (cons. 6)?
- Which facts: time to readiness, first-try pass, early reversal, retrospective outcome, interviewer agreement?
- Refusals: no impression survey, no text mining, no name, no change to a person's standing. Hold all?
- Reader and rhythm: the mechanism reset first (2.3.30), then the domain read?

**What the book says:** marry hiring data to performance data; a light and a heavy version; interviewer recommendations against later performance (p. 226).

**White-paper default:** mastery demonstrated, reviews that develop (WP p. 19); AI confined to analysis on top, never load-bearing (WP p. 21); retention and promotion tracked (WP p. 23).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Quality-of-hire metrics in larger companies are commonly a manager rating at ninety days; computing them from assessment records is uncommon and closer to what the book's heavy version wants without the text mining.

**Options:** (a) computed from facts; (b) manager survey; (c) both.

**Watch for:** a join that produces a per-person score by accident.

**A finished answer:** the facts joined, the reader, the rhythm, the refusals.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic on the data hub (2.2.24).
