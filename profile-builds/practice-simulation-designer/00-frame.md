# Stage 0 frame: Practice and Simulation Designer

## Frame (one sentence)

This specialist decides how Sŏn's team rehearses hospitality before they meet it on the floor: which form of practice (branching scenario, role-play, perception drill, simulation, observed trail, debrief) transfers for a given skill, how consequences, feedback, and fidelity are designed, and how the practice is specified tool-neutrally so it renders to Trainual, SCORM, Synthesia, or a live pre-shift.

## Template type

Type 2 and Type 1 blend: creative in writing scenarios and scripts, analytical in choosing and diagnosing practice. Emphasize cue table, decision rules, worked examples, anti-patterns. Voice matters only in scenario and script writing.

## The gap it fills

- The Instructional Designer chooses the medium on load and feedback grounds and states "video models, a coach corrects." It does not design the practice itself: consequence structure, branching, fidelity, debrief.
- The Educational Materials Author and Editor writes prose in one voice; dramatic scenario and script writing is a different craft.
- Hospitality's hardest skills are social judgment under ambiguity and performance under load. Practice is where those are built, and without an owner, practice defaults to multiple choice and video.
- Brandon's stated aim is interactive modules with real options. This seat is the judgment behind that. Tool capabilities themselves live in `adapters/` and are refreshed, so the profile encodes judgment that does not go stale.

## Judgment to encode (Stage 1 research targets)

1. **Choosing the practice form.** When a scenario, a role-play, a perception drill, a simulation, or a live trail transfers; when each fails.
2. **Scenario design.** Action mapping and scenario-based learning: starting from what people do, realistic dilemmas, consequences shown not told, recoverable failure paths, avoiding test-wise tells (the longest answer, the obviously kind option).
3. **Expert comparison.** Decide-then-compare methods (ShadowBox style) for judgment with no single right answer.
4. **Behavior modeling and role-play.** What the evidence says makes modeling and rehearsal work (credible models, practice, feedback, transfer support), and what an AI presenter can and cannot do as a model.
5. **Fidelity.** Which features of the floor a practice must reproduce for near transfer, and which it can drop.
6. **Debriefing.** Structured debrief after simulation and after real service; debrief as the place learning happens.
7. **Perception drills.** Contrast sets and cue-identification practice.
8. **Situational judgment items.** Writing them well, and the line where they become a gate (the Assessment seat's).

## Candidate corpus (to curate to 5 to 15 in Stage 1)

Moore, *Map It* (action mapping and scenarios); Clark, *Scenario-Based e-Learning*; Aldrich on simulations; Taylor, Russ-Eft and Chan (2005) meta-analysis of behavior modeling training; Klein and colleagues on ShadowBox and decision training; Rudolph and colleagues on debriefing with good judgment (simulation-based education); situational judgment test literature (Weekley and Ployhart; Lievens); Kellman on perceptual learning; aviation and medical simulation fidelity research. Check each for judgment content before keeping it.

## Stage 7 behavioral test candidates

1. Design practice for recovering after a kitchen error reaches a table. (Tests: form choice, recoverable paths, canon recovery authority.)
2. Critique a scenario where the strong answer is the longest and kindest choice. (Tests: test-wise tells, distractor design.)
3. A request for a high-production AI-video simulation to teach reading a table. (Tests: functional task alignment over physical resemblance; cheapest aligned form.)
4. Design the debrief after a role-play where the learner froze. (Tests: advocacy with inquiry, felt safety, frames.)
5. Decide whether a proposed situational judgment item belongs in practice or in the readiness gate. (Tests: the seam with the Assessment seat.)
