# Stage 1 corpus: Hospitality Craft Educator

This corpus grounds the seat's judgment: what hospitality expertise consists of as a teachable discipline, which parts are checkable service technique and which are relational judgment, what is trainable and what is hired for, and how the parts that cannot be written down are transmitted. Every row grounds at least one decision the seat makes; nothing here is a survey of the field.

Build date 2026-09-26. This file replaces the unvalidated head start entirely. Every head-start row was re-checked at its source; rows the head start held from memory were confirmed, corrected, or replaced, and the corpus was extended to cover all seven research targets in `00-frame.md`. Beverage is in this seat (Brandon's answer to the frame), so target 6 is fully in scope. No lineage grounding was supplied, and none was reconstructed.

## Status key

- **verified-primary**: the primary source (the book text, the peer-reviewed paper, or its publisher or journal abstract page) was opened this build and supports the specific claims in the row.
- **verified-secondary**: the primary could not be opened, and a reputable secondary source opened this build supports the specific claims.
- **partially-verified**: the source exists as cited, and the row states which claimed content is unsupported, overstated, or held only at secondary level.
- **project canon (read this build)**: Sŏn canon read through the ClickUp connector this build.

No row is held from memory. Where a merged row mixes statuses, the row carries the lowest status and the Status cell names the weaker parts. Bibliographic facts (author, year, title, venue) were checked for every citation.

## Unresolved tensions

Kept for the elicitation and the adversarial pass; not smoothed over.

1. Can caring be taught? Meyer, a Ritz-Carlton manager via NIST, and Schulze route it to selection; Guidara says everything can be taught, hospitality included. Mattingly and Kraiger find training moves measured emotional intelligence, but the outcome is scores, not felt care on the floor. The seat's working line: train attention, reading, and response; route disposition to selection honestly; never promise to install warmth.
2. What to hire for. Meyer weights emotional skills at 51 percent and managers put agreeableness first (Tews); Tracey finds the ability to learn predicts newcomer performance best and conscientiousness predicts experienced performance.
3. Where hospitality lives. Meyer and Lashley (excluded) put it in disposition and dialogue; Pijls finds customers also experience it through the setting; Golubovskaya points to organizational practices that enable or block it. Hemmington (excluded, abstract only) defines commercial hospitality partly as lots of little surprises; Pijls finds surprise may not be a dimension of the felt experience.
4. Standard language against rote. Ritz-Carlton teaches common language by daily repetition; Meyer calls one uniform phrase creepy; the Saveur account has an elite house pulling back scripted narration. Victorino gives the split (script the task, flex the treatment) but is customer-perception research, not a training test, and it recommends training deep acting, which row 11 says carries a cost.
5. Conversion of tacit knowledge. Nonaka says externalization works; Polanyi and Tsoukas say tacit knowing is not convertible; Collins's typology reconciles in part. The seat classifies each element before choosing a medium.
6. Participation against designed teaching. Lave and Wenger locate learning in legitimate participation; Collins, Billett, and Fuller show perceptual processes need modeling and articulation and that participation alone leaves outcomes unsecured. Trailing is necessary, not sufficient.
7. Documented standards against actual practice. Brown and Duguid warn that canonical manuals blind a house to real practice; a standards-led house treats written standards as the backbone. Write standards, and also capture the noncanonical floor practice and stories.
8. Trusting a read against checking it. Recognition-primed decision making supports acting on recognition; Kahneman and Klein limit that to valid cues with feedback; customers mask feelings under display rules and status (Puccinelli, excluded). Route to canon's attunement model rather than a new rule.
9. Fast off-floor gains against slow floor-built skill. ShadowBox and perceptual learning modules show quick gains on proxies; Rose and Klein and Hoffman say the skill takes volume and years of varied cases. Off-floor practice speeds trailing; it does not replace it.
10. Who counts as the expert. ShadowBox dropped five of eight experts for not fitting the desired frame and showed minority views among the rest; expertise is fractionated. Naming whose read sets the Sŏn standard is founder-gated, and lineage houses cannot serve as the reference without Brandon.
11. Is deep acting costly? Humphrey, Ashforth, and Diefendorff (excluded) say it does not harm well-being; Grandey and Sayre and Grandey and Gabriel report a small measured cost; Hochschild counted both strategies as costly. Aim at natural attentiveness and autonomy, not effortful deep acting as a demand.
12. Should display rules exist at all? Grandey, Rupp, and Brice argue for abolishing them; Humphrey et al and the hospitality meta-analyses (Xu et al; Shulga et al, both excluded as grounding) find positive display rules travel with deep acting and that antecedent effects are modest. The seat's rule is narrower than either: no scripted feeling, discretion over expression.
13. Is surface acting always harmful to performance? The meta-analysis shows a small negative link; Chi finds it pays for extraverts; Groth (excluded) finds it penalized mainly when detected.
14. Generosity against proportionality. Meyer teaches erring on the side of generosity; Smith et al find customers prefer recovery proportional to the failure, and Roschk and Gelbrich find matching the resource type matters most. Match the type first, scale to severity, treat extra generosity as a judgment call.
15. Comeback against restore. Meyer's last-chapter framing presents recovery as a chance to end the story well; de Matos, Maxham and Netemeyer, and Magnini say aim for restoration and expect the paradox rarely and only under narrow conditions.
16. Break the rules against bound the discretion. Hart et al urge encouraging staff to deviate; Bowen and Lawler argue for bounded discretion and treat frequent heroics as system failure. Bounded discretion; the limits are a binding.
17. No alibis against explanation. Meyer rules out excuses; explanation research (Bradley and Sparks, unverified) suggests a credible explanation can help. Open.
18. Perceptual against cognitive basis of wine expertise. Parr finds the edge is perceptual recognition; Ballester and Hughson and Boakes find it is largely knowledge-based; Croijmans 2021 finds language plays no causal role once expertise exists, while Croijmans and Majid 2016 find verbal training is needed to communicate. Different tasks; train and assess all three parts.
19. Verbal overshadowing. Parr proposes novices are vulnerable to it; Hughson and Boakes 2009 found describing helped both novices and intermediates.
20. Generalization of sensory training. Tempere finds threshold gains compound-specific; Wang's small pilot finds general discrimination gains. Thin either way.
21. Training and sales. Correlational positives (Manske and Cordua; Gultek, both excluded) against quasi-experimental nulls (Orlowski; Brain). Knowledge alone does not move behavior.
22. Ritz-Carlton specifics. Story frequency (twice a week against daily) and cycle length (20, 24, or 12) differ by source and era. Cite the principle, not a number.
23. Pre-meal purpose. Guidara frames it as leadership and inspiration (the how and the why); Ritz-Carlton frames line-up as reinforcing one standard by repetition and role play. Two halves of one slot, not one method.
24. Precision as rehearsal. The sibling profile carries it from Guidara; this build could not confirm it. Flag for Brandon.
25. Target 1 selection evidence against target 4 fit evidence. Tracey says the ability to learn matters most early; Chi and Humphrey say person-job fit decides whether emotional labor helps or harms. Both may hold; they answer different questions (who learns fast, who lasts).
