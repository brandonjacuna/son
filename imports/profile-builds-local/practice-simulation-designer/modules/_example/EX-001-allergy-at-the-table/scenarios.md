# Scenarios

## Scenario S1: It's fine if it's just a little

**Setup.** A two-top on a busy night. As you finish the menu overview, one customer says, "Oh, I'm a little allergic to shellfish, but honestly it's fine if there's just a bit in something. I don't want to be difficult." Their friend laughs and says they always say that.

**Cues present.** The customer is minimizing to avoid seeming difficult. "A little allergic" is a severity judgment the customer is inviting you to share. The friend's laugh adds social pressure to let it go.

### Decision 1
Prompt: What do you do?
- A. Smile, say "no problem, I'll steer you away from the obvious shellfish dishes," and take the order. -> go to 1A
- B. Say "not difficult at all," ask what exactly they avoid and whether traces or shared cooking matter, repeat it back, and tell them you will flag it with the kitchen. -> go to 1B
- C. Tell them you will note it, then decide it is minor and skip the kitchen confirmation because the night is busy. -> go to 1C

### 1A
Consequence: The customer orders a dish with no visible shellfish. It arrives with a sauce that contains a fermented shellfish ingredient. Twenty minutes later they mention their throat feels scratchy.
Feedback: You accepted the customer's severity judgment and routed nothing. "Steering away from the obvious" misses the allergens that are not visible.
Expert rationale: The expert hears "a little allergic" as "allergic." Severity is never ours to judge, and the dangerous allergens are often the invisible ones.
Next: Decision 2

### 1B
Consequence: The customer relaxes. They say it is a true allergy, they react to traces, and they had not thought about shared oil. You flag it and confirm with the kitchen. The kitchen names two safe options and one to avoid.
Feedback: You made it easy for them not to minimize, and you routed it before anything fired.
Expert rationale: "Not difficult at all" removes the social cost of being honest. The three questions surfaced the detail (traces, shared oil) the customer had not volunteered.
Next: End (strong)

### 1C
Consequence: The ticket carries a note, but the kitchen never confirms. A garnish on the dish is prepared on a shared station.
Feedback: A note is not a confirmation. Skipping the kitchen step on a busy night is exactly when it matters most.
Expert rationale: The expert treats the confirmation as part of firing the order. No confirmation, no fire, regardless of volume.
Next: Decision 2

### Decision 2
Prompt: You realize the allergy was not fully routed. The food is already on its way. What now?
- A. Stop the dish, tell the lead immediately, and go to the table to explain and offer a safe option. -> go to 2A
- B. Say nothing and watch the table closely in case they react. -> go to 2B

### 2A
Consequence: The dish is pulled before it lands. You tell the customer plainly what happened and what the kitchen can make safely. They thank you for catching it.
Feedback: A mistake caught and owned before harm is a recovery. This is the right move every time.
Expert rationale: The expert moves fast and tells the truth. Saying "we caught something and want to make sure you're safe" builds more trust than a perfect first pass would have.
Next: End (recoverable)

### 2B
Consequence: The dish lands. The customer eats. You spend the rest of service watching and hoping.
Feedback: Watching is not a safety plan. The dish should never have reached the table.
Expert rationale: The expert never trades the customer's safety for avoiding an awkward moment.
Next: End (failure)
