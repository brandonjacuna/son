[15.0|Unnamed-T3] If the founding documents are like a house’s support structure, then
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] the operating system is like a house’s mechanical system. Like a
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] mechanical system, the operating system contains various
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] substructures that work together to make up the whole: the wiring
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] and plumbing of your house.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] An operating system is a set of norms and actions that are shared
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] with everyone in the company. These shared systems and
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] parameters are essential to growth and success. Keystones like an
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] annual plan, quarterly goals, and regular communications allow

[15.0|Unnamed-T3] the highest level. These systems can then replicate down through

[15.0|Unnamed-T3] questions and the same pushback every time. This is my answer to
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] those questions. While you can easily find lots of tactical advice
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] online about goal-setting processes, here I want to focus on the
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] spirit of what we’re actually trying to achieve and how to know if

[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Why we set goals
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] These are the goals of the goals, if you will.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Define success: Goals are statements about successful end
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] states. What are you trying to do, and how would you know if
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] you did it? Plans are sets of activities. Executing on a plan may
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] help you achieve a goal, but it’s not the goal itself. To maximize
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] your odds of achieving a successful outcome, start with the end

===== PDF PAGE 105 =====
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] in mind and work backward to the activities most likely to get
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] you there.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Focus: There are always far more things we could be doing than
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] we have capacity for. Productive teams clearly distinguish the
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] most important things from all of the other good ideas, and they
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] relentlessly focus on those top priorities. This also helps ensure
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] that all of the different parts of the team are operating in concert
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] rather than independently working on related things.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Allow for autonomy: Aligning around and committing to a
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] shared definition of success creates accountability. Doing so
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] without mandating specific activities allows accountability to
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] coexist with autonomy and creativity.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] What good goals look like
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Remember: FOCUS(S)! Good goals are focused, concise, and
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] comprehensible. Everyone on the team should be able to easily
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] memorize the team’s goals. This is important because the progress
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] you make in a quarter or year is the result of thousands of
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] independent decisions. Too many goals or too many details interfere
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] with this. People can remember about three to five things.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Focus on the most important things: Your goals should help
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] you identify and avoid the distractions. Use plain English that is
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] easily understandable to anyone with a passing familiarity with
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] your team’s strategy. This helps with strategic clarity,
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] memorability, and communicability. Jargon often masks strategic
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] gaps.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Objectively assessable: Everyone on the team should have the
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] same understanding of what success looks like and what it
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] doesn’t. Goals don’t need to be quantitative, but they cannot be
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] subjective.

[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Challenging but possible: Your goals need to be credible. If
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] people consider the outcome unimaginable, they’ll simply ignore
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] it and give up. Your goals should also stretch the team, inspiring
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] and challenging people. If you ask your team to stretch, you’ll
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] often discover that they find ways to deliver more than you
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] expect. A good rule of thumb is to shoot for about a 70 percent
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] success rate.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] User-oriented: Never organize team goals by function

[15.0|Unnamed-T3] flexibly organize their capabilities and creatively solve problems.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Don’t organize goals around the features you’re delivering—

[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Heuristics for testing your goals
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Assess your goals using these guidelines:
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Does your goal start with a verb (“launch,” “build,” “refactor,”
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] etc.)? Then you probably have an action, so reframe it to describe
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] the outcome you want. Often, this takes the form of translating
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] “X so that Y” into “Y via X” (and consider if you need X in there
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] at all). A helpful trick to figure out the proper framing is to read
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] the goal out, ask yourself why, answer that question, then do that
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] a couple of times until the true goal comes into focus. (See Table
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] 2 for an example.)
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Do you have “engineering goals” and “business goals,” or
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] something similar? Stop it.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Are your goals more than one page, more than three to five
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] objectives, or more than three to five KRs per objective? No one
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] will read them—let alone remember them.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] When you (or your team) look at your goals, do you wince and
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] think, “What about X? I was really hoping to get to that this
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] quarter”? If not, you probably haven’t focused enough, and your
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] goals are not adding value.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Could one team member think a goal is achieved and another one
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] completely disagree? Then your goal isn’t specific enough. (By

===== PDF PAGE 108 =====
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] contrast, if everyone feels it’s mostly successful but the
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] assessments range from 60–80 percent done, who cares?)
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Can you imagine a scenario where the goal is achieved but you’re
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] still dissatisfied with where you ended up? Then your goal isn’t
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] specific enough, or an aspect is missing.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Could you be successful without achieving the goal? Then your
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] goal is overly specific, and you should rethink how to define
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] success.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] FAQs
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] How do you manage against the goals?
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] If you set good goals and have real buy-in, then the whole team
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] should have a set of shared goals you can use as a foundation. As
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] you talk about team performance, meetings, sprint planning, and
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] progress, frame those conversations against the goals you set.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Organize your activities around the goals, talk about them
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] constantly, and frame the day-to-day in terms of how they will help
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] achieve your goals. Keep them top of mind for everyone, all the time.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] How do you score goals?
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] I usually do a lightweight mid-quarter and end-quarter review that
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] uses green, yellow, and red color-coded scoring (representing
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] success, mixed results, or failure). The mid-quarter review is a
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] helpful checkpoint to refocus everyone on goals we may have gotten
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] distracted from. The end-quarter review is mostly to help us
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] recalibrate how aggressive we were and highlight areas where we are
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] regularly failing to make progress. If the reviews require a lot of
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] effort or feel like they need to be done more often, then your team
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] probably hasn’t fully internalized the goals and isn’t using them to
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] guide day-to-day activities.

===== PDF PAGE 109 =====
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] What do you do if the goal changes before the time period ends?
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Sometimes strategies change or we learn things that change our
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] priorities. That’s fine. Start working on the new goal immediately.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Add a zero with an asterisk or something when you go to score the
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] old one. Who cares? But if this happens to you all the time, you may
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] be setting goals that are too specific or incorporating activity
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] planning into the goal-setting process.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] What about quantifiable measurability?
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Goals generally benefit from clearly defined, measurable metrics. For
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] example, “Median latency is under 200 milliseconds” is obviously a
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] much better definition of success than “Latency is reduced.”
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] However, sometimes people get hung up on enforcing
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] quantifiability and can lose track of what really matters. It’s fine for
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] some goals to have no numbers, as long as it’s clear what success
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] looks like and it’s not a subjective question. A goal like “MVP is up
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] and running on production hardware, and multiple external
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] companies have tested it and provided early feedback” has no
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] numbers, but it’s very concrete. The important thing is that the
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] whole team assesses success and failure the same way. As Andy
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Grove said about goal assessment, the important thing is that “at the
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] end, you can look without any argument and say, ‘Did I do that, or
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] did I not do that?’ Yes. No. Simple.”
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] What if I can’t measure the thing I want to optimize?
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Generally, I say use the metric in your goal anyway and try to assess
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] it by proxy. Better than setting the wrong goal entirely. As John
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Tukey said, “Far better an approximate answer to the right question,
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] which is often vague, than an exact answer to the wrong question,
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] which can always be made precise.” Plus, nothing motivates

===== PDF PAGE 110 =====
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] development of the right metric like using them to actually measure
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] success across the team!
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] What if I have a metric but I don’t know what the right target is?
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Quite often, you’ll agree on a good way to measure success, but you
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] won’t know exactly what a reasonable target is since you don’t yet
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] have a baseline. Generally, I say who cares? Take a guess at a
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] reasonable number, be honest that it’s a stab in the dark, work
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] toward it, and update your target along the way as you learn more.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Better to set the goal in the right direction and not be sure exactly
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] how far you should get than to risk going in the wrong direction.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] What if you can’t agree on what the goals should be?
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] There are any number of reasons this could be the case. Let’s
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] assume you’re dealing with a reasonable set of people working
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] together in good faith and trying to do what’s best for the team. (If
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] you aren’t, it’s not a goal-setting problem.) The first step is to stop
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] arguing and try to diagnose why you’re stuck. If you can figure that
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] out, you can almost always find a good resolution.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Here are some common issues people get hung up on—work
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] from the top down to determine where you’re falling out of
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] alignment, then work through that question while holding
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] everything else to the side before continuing down the list:
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Different assumptions about vision or strategy: If we aren’t
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] aligned on what we’re trying to do as a team, there’s no effective
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] way to figure out the milestones along the way.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Different assumptions about priorities: If you think that we
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] should do A first, then B, and I think we should do B first, then A,
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] we can’t align on goals.

===== PDF PAGE 111 =====
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Different interpretations: Tease this out by talking through
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] concrete scenarios and seeing if you agree on what success is and
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] what it isn’t.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Incomplete success statements: If your goals don’t capture
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] something important for success, you may see people trying to
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] pull things up a level into very broad statements. Try adding
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] additional, very specific statements to narrow down the range of
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] good outcomes until you’re on the same page, then simplify as
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] appropriate.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Different assumptions about feasibility: If someone feels the
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] goal is fundamentally not possible or cost-prohibitive, it’s good to
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] name this explicitly and dig into it.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Different assumptions about capacity: Try expanding the time
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] frame a bit and see if you all agree on the overall plan and are
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] simply disagreeing about how far you should get in a specific
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] time period.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Why “goals” and not “OKRs”?
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] OKRs are just goals with a particular structure. They separate the
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] thing we’re trying to do (the objective) from the concrete definition
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] of success (the key results). I like that structural distinction—I find
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] it helps enforce a clear definition of success—so I tend to use the
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] OKR framework myself. As Andy Grove, the forefather of “managing
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] by objective,” framed it in High Output Management, OKRs separate
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] out two key questions:27
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Where do I want to go? This answer provides the objective.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] How will I pace myself to see if I’m getting there? This answer
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] provides the milestones, or key results.

[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Here are a few other thoughts on how to effectively set and use
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] goals:
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Say what percentage of goals you expect people to hit up front
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Some companies and leaders like their goals to be as realistic as
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] possible, so it’s expected that 100 percent of the goals will be
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] achieved. Other companies prefer their goals to be more
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] aspirational, so it’s expected that some of the goals—around 20–30
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] percent—will not be met. You can do both, but take care to specify
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] which goals are aspirational (hit 70 percent) versus committed (hit
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] 100 percent). Either way, always consider the dependencies required
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] to achieve those goals.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Committed goals are a good idea if:
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] There’s an existential company threat, for example if a
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] competitor has developed a better version of one of your top
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] product features.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Another team working on a top-priority project is blocked.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] A customer has been told a product or project will be delivered
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] on a particular timeline.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] The argument for biasing toward more aspirational goals is a
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] simple one: Human and team behavior often lead us to anchor on
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] and solve for a defined outcome. A more challenging goal might lead
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] us to think differently about how to accomplish it, and thus generate
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] fresh ideas and energy. A team might also unconsciously take the
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] pressure off of their efforts once they hit an “achievable” goal,
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] forestalling the possibility of carrying the work further and
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] exceeding expectations, which might motivate the team to have
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] more confidence in their abilities in the coming quarters. The key
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] with more aspirational goals is to set expectations: Teams should
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] know that hitting 70–80 percent of the goal counts as success, but

===== PDF PAGE 113 =====
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] that there will be great recognition and reward for exceeding those
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] expectations. (See the chapter appendix on page 125 for guidance on
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] helping the team write good OKRs.)
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] An individual’s goals should include one or two personal goals
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] As a manager, remember that the work of the individual is both
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] company-focused (what they’ll do to contribute to the division) and
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] individual-focused (how their work will contribute to their broader
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] career narrative and development). Goals should reflect this. Every
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] quarter, an individual should have at least one or two personal
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] development goals in addition to the work output they expect to
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] complete. That way, the team member is doing the work that’s
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] needed today but also developing abilities that allow them to grow
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] their impact, contribute to the work the team might need to deliver
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] tomorrow, and advance their career.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Say someone on your team can only do basic analysis, but your
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] data is growing increasingly complex. It’ll be important for that
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] person to develop more advanced analytics skills, including how to
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] write SQL queries to access the data from your internal systems.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] They might have a goal to conduct up to 30 basic analyses that
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] quarter, as well as a goal to take a SQL class and demonstrate that
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] they can conduct a complex analytical project by the end of the
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] quarter. As people become more experienced, their goals might
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] become less skill-focused and more about capabilities. Instead of
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] “Write a detailed project plan,” for example, their goal might be
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] “Lead a complex project from plan to completion with measurable
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] positive results.”
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] As a manager, it’s tempting to have your team focus only on what
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] they need to get done in the quarter rather than working on more
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] developmental goals. But just as a company can’t rely on one
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] product for revenue growth indefinitely and must invest in more

===== PDF PAGE 114 =====
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] speculative work on future products or revenue streams, so must
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] teams and individuals invest in their future development. This is
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] especially true in high-growth environments. The skills and efforts
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] that got your work done in Q1 are not going to be the same ones
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] required 6 or 12 months later. You must build for future scale and
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] challenges, both in your team practices and among the individuals
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] on your team. What’s more, if part of your role is to coach and
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] develop your people, how will you demonstrate your commitment to
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] that aspect of your job if you don’t keep people focused on their
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] developmental goals? It’s easy for a manager to say, “I’m here to
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] coach you,” but it’s your actions that matter, not your words.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] (There’s more on coaching in Chapter 5.)
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] The “how” is just as important as the “what”
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] When looking back at a particular project, quarter, or year, don’t
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] limit your review to the goals someone accomplished. Pay attention
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] to how the person or team approached the work. Even if the team is
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] hitting their goals, they might have gotten there in a more painful or
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] less efficient way than they could have. Maybe someone needs to
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] focus on communication and collaboration with fellow team
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] members instead of on the quality of their output, or on how to
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] scope a problem instead of on the technical work of solving it. Make
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] sure you ascertain the “how” alongside the “what” and provide
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] feedback to individuals accordingly.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] You may be familiar with the concept of a Pyrrhic victory: a
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] victory won at such a great cost that it was not worth the battle. All
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] too often, I see the work equivalent of Pyrrhic victories. Yes, the
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] product launched on time, but the team and the relationships
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] therein barely survived, and people are unable to contribute quality
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] work in the month afterward because they’re exhausted and
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] communication is brittle. One early reader of this book told me,

[15.0|Unnamed-T3] “Performance = results × behaviors. It’s multiplicative. Hitting
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] targets while fomenting unrest and backstabbing should get you a 0,

[15.0|Unnamed-T3] make sure your team is defining goals and accomplishing them, but
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] not at the expense of their future ability to do so. When a company
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] is growing, it needs to have a consistent ability to get work done—
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] sustainably—or else growth will stall.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] This is very much a moment that calls for balancing management
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] with leadership. Soliciting feedback about how the work was

[15.0|Unnamed-T3] fix interpersonal or procedural issues, or might prevent them from
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] occurring or worsening. Remember to say the thing you think you
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] cannot say: Provide direct feedback to individuals or to the team and
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] acknowledge that a certain project was accomplished at too high a
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] cost. That includes acknowledging your own role in that reality.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Being honest and demonstrating an ability to reflect and improve
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] will encourage mutual self-awareness, and it will help you
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] demonstrate that leadership is not perfect, nor is it comfortable. If

[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Metrics that matter
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Your core company metrics are another structure that can—and
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] should—replicate down from the company level to divisions, teams,
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] and sometimes even to individuals. Like goals, metrics can be set on
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] both long-term and short-term horizons.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] In my experience, long-term metrics tend to be lagging
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] indicators. They represent the output of a ton of operational, short-

===== PDF PAGE 116 =====
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] term “input” metrics. At Stripe, we start each year with what we call
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] company targets. These are the metrics for the year that best reflect
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] our company priorities. Some are financial outcome metrics that
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] reflect the work we’ll do in the coming year, while others are
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] strategic input metrics, like the number of monthly active businesses
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] using our products. For example, we might track daily or weekly
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] user adoption for a set of newly launched products. These are inputs
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] to metrics that ultimately measure revenue and margin in our P & L.

[15.0|Unnamed-T3] leaders, divisions, and teams are accountable for the inputs that
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] drive those outcomes each quarter.

[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Metrics framework
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Using the following metrics framework—see Figure 7 above—can
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] help you manage your team effectively:
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Objectives
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Objectives are broad statements of prioritization, strategy, and
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] intent. An objective should answer the question “Where do I want to
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] go?”
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Creating metrics
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Metrics allow you to measure whether you’re achieving your
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] objectives. They answer the question “How will I know I’m getting
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] there?” One objective can have one or many metrics.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Long-term metrics: mission and vision
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Long-term metrics help define success against your team’s
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] mission and three-year vision. Mission metrics tend to be
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] lagging, so they’re not necessarily the metrics you monitor
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] regularly to know if you’re on track. While mission metrics move

===== PDF PAGE 119 =====
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] slowly over longer time horizons, you should always set
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] milestones for long-term metrics in your annual plans.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Ideally, a team uses the same metrics for a long period of time,
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] e.g., two to five years. Examples include payment volume,
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] revenue, total losses, and API reliability. A team should aim for
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] three to five charter metrics.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Short-term metrics: operating or input metrics
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Short-term metrics are real-time or leading indicators that
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] measure activities or intermediate outcomes. While they might
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] not determine the success of your mission or three-year vision,
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] they do inform whether you are on track for your desired
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] outcomes. Operating metrics are often linked directly to team
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] goals. A team should aim for three to five operating metrics.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Other metrics
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Measure other results the team wants to achieve in the half.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Ideally, these are continuous metrics. If you have a binary metric
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] (e.g., ship product X), that can be a useful starting point to think
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] of a continuous measure for that product (e.g., ship product X to
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] 50 new users).
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Once you’ve set your metrics, make sure you have a
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] dashboard to review your metrics regularly.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Using metrics
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Metrics can’t help you if you don’t use and review them regularly.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Only then can they help you answer the question “Am I making
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] progress? Why or why not?”
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Metrics are imperfect, and only by reviewing them regularly do
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] you learn how they can be improved or changed to be even more
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] useful. Some ideas for when to review metrics:

===== PDF PAGE 120 =====
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Have a weekly or biweekly metrics meeting.
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Discuss your metrics in a Monday morning standup or biweekly

[15.0|Unnamed-T3] We’ve invested heavily in writing for three reasons:

[15.0|Unnamed-T3] levels the playing field between employees independent of
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] location, seniority, or tenure because everyone has access to the

[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Second, at Stripe we believe longform writing leads to
[15.0|Unnamed-T3] higher-quality thought. For the writer, it’s much easier to spot

[15.0|Unnamed-T3] Is it complete? Is the information you’re making accessible the
