# S1 digest

## Part 1: what the old page holds that's worth keeping

### 0. Management basics checklist

- The standard checklist assumes: individual reports; weekly or biweekly 1:1s; quarterly goals tracked together; bidirectional feedback every three to six months; formal performance conversations weighing results and how; weekly team meetings; job descriptions and an interview rubric; job ladders and levels; a compensation philosophy; a manager above who gives context.
- At Sŏn this reads as a gap list, not a scorecard, because several assumptions don't hold by design (no manager above the founders; strands instead of levels). It applies twice: to the founders as a partnership with no manager above them, and to the two leads as the floor's management layer.
- Legal and counsel question, kept verbatim in substance: the founders' legal responsibilities as employers and managers in Texas, beyond the compensation question, are not addressed anywhere in the record and are flagged to counsel, outside this program. Compliance logging, HACCP, and traceability run through the stack (WP p.10, p.21); Texas/FLSA exposure tied to embedded compensation is a separate, already-parked legal question.
- 1:1s: no desk-native 1:1 exists. The shift brief and shift close (WP p.13 to 14) and a ninety-day onboarding plan with check-ins (WP p.18) are the closest equivalents. A founder-to-founder 1:1 equivalent does not exist.
- Quarterly goals: leading and cultural metrics exist (employee NPS, cultural labor score, customer recognition rate; WP p.22) with no targets attached and no goal-setting cycle at house or team level.
- Bidirectional feedback: reviews every three to six months, development-focused, feedback runs both directions including on management effectiveness, a no-surprises rule, and a structured feedback channel are all described (WP p.19, p.22), continuous rather than periodic, stronger than the standard checklist's version; the instrument itself is not built.
- Formal performance conversations: reviews are described as development-focused rather than verdict-rendering (WP p.19); whether "how" something was done is assessed separately from demonstrated mastery, and by what instrument, is open.
- Weekly team meetings: the shift brief functions as the team meeting (WP p.20); no meeting above the shift (a weekly floor meeting, a leads' sync, a founders' meeting) exists yet.
- Job descriptions and rubric: documented competency requirements and semi-structured interview questions exist (WP p.11, p.18); founder job descriptions and a built rubric instrument do not.
- Job ladders and levels: Sŏn's structure is strands and an inverted web, not levels (WP p.10 to 11); whether a single house needs levels at all, and what "clearly communicated" means for a web rather than a ladder, is an open question, not resolved here.
- Compensation: a structure exists (pay built into menu price, a point system weighted by days worked, calculated daily, culinary included, cross-strand fluency recognized; WP p.11, p.19 to 20); the philosophy behind the structure is not yet written.
- The founders have no manager above them, so "goals agreed with your manager" and "1:1s with your manager" do not apply as written; a founder-level substitute (a founder cadence) is named as the gap.
- Option: run the checklist once, as this gap list, and then retire it rather than re-running it as a recurring readiness test. Reasoning: several rows assume structures Sŏn has deliberately replaced, and scoring their absence as failure would reimport a headcount-scaling model.
- Sequencing: the gap list hands items forward rather than resolving them: a goals layer, a planning cycle, the founder cadence, the review instrument, the compensation philosophy, and the levels question all depend on later work.

### 1.1 Build self-awareness to build mutual awareness

- Research: standard founder-principle material is about building trust between a manager and reports, one conversation at a time. At Sŏn, trust is described as pre-extended by the structure (a server's judgment is pre-authorized before the first shift; WP p.13, p.26), so the question becomes how to hold a structure that already extends trust, not how to earn it.
- Six candidate founder principles, argued from the white paper rather than restated from the book:
  - Option: anything a founder knows that the house needs is a liability until it is written into the system, and moving it there is the founder's job. Reasoning: the founding claim is a company that does not depend on either founder's memory (WP p.05 to 06, p.13, p.37).
  - Option: a founder's own way of thinking must not become the unexamined shape of the system; every instrument a founder designs is checked against a user who is not the founder. Reasoning: "restaurant founders build systems for themselves without realizing it, then blame employees when those systems do not work for anyone else" (WP p.17).
  - Option: a founder states what is decided as decided and what is open as open, to investors, customers, the team, and each other, and says "I have not decided" rather than improvising a position. Reasoning: the white paper practices this on itself, naming open stack decisions and undecided daypart questions rather than presenting them as settled (WP p.15, p.22, p.36).
  - Option: when something fails, ask what allowed it before asking who, and apply this to the founders' own domains first, in writing, in a post-mortem. Reasoning: management is described as absorbing blame from staff, and universal problems are treated as design problems (WP p.17).
  - Option: authority lives in the domain, each founder owns their domain outright and is accountable to its outcomes, and neither founder decides inside another's domain (WP p.07 to 08, p.03, p.09). Open seam: which decisions are domain-owned versus joint, and how a deadlock among three equal partners resolves, is not addressed anywhere except one worked example (whether to add a service period is a three-partner decision; WP p.36).
  - Option: founders are subject to the same bandwidth-before-results sequence as everyone else, and a founder running at zero bandwidth is a system failure, not a sign of commitment (WP p.13, p.17, p.30). The team-level leading indicators (employee NPS, cultural labor score) have no founder-level equivalent; what a founder-level read should be is not addressed anywhere.
- Explicitly not answerable by extraction work, and named as a boundary the team held: each founder's personal values, work style, strengths and failure modes, and how each wants to be disagreed with. These must come from the founders and the chef partner directly, never authored on their behalf.
- Working-with-me document: a founder's written account of how they work, exchanged among the founders and later issued in a shorter form to the two leads. Functions as: a seam document between founders who rarely observe each other's work directly (unlike a manager and reports who see each other daily); the first document about the people, not the concept, that an incoming chef partner reads; and an inventory of what still lives only in a founder's head.
- What it must never be: a job description, a company-values statement, a performance instrument or scorecard, or binding on the other founders; it does not set the founder cadence, which is a joint decision.
- Spine: eleven prompts (seat and its edges; what lives in my head; how I work; how I decide; how I disagree and how to disagree with me; my strengths and what each costs; my three values at work; what I look like under load; how to reach me; what I need/what you can expect; what I don't know yet), each with guidance on what it's really asking, how to answer well, and what a weak answer looks like. Preceded by three self-exercises done alone: a values exercise (reduce ten values to three, each with an activity, an outcome, a trade-off), a work-style placement on two axes (introverted to extroverted; task- to people-oriented) taken three times (at work, at home, under load), and a strengths inventory (three or four strengths split into skills and capabilities, each marked innate or acquired, completed with "this is also a weakness when...").
- Seat preambles: Brandon's (concept, systems, experience, public voice) weights prompts toward what of the concept is still only in his head, where his domain ends, and what his public-voice availability does to the concept. Dominic's (capital, architecture, technology build) weights prompts toward what breaks in the model and stack if he's unreachable, how others get a financial read without a figure asserted outside his domain, and how he intends to learn the floor, since his domain didn't originate in hospitality. The chef partner's preamble is written to an unfilled seat and is a prompt, not a description; it flags that two source documents currently describe the chef's authority differently, reconciled by the person seated plus the two founders.
- Open question, not resolved here: whether the working-with-me exchange with an incoming chef partner happens before the partnership is signed, as diligence, or as an onboarding instrument written some weeks in. Brandon and Dominic can write theirs now regardless, since they are already partners.
- Instrument specs kept: the values exercise feeds only prompt G and is never aggregated into company values or used in a performance conversation. The work-style grid is shared vocabulary only, never used in hiring, floor role assignment, or progression; whether service pressure selects for particular work styles is open, not concluded here. The strengths inventory feeds prompt F and the knowledge-transfer register, and must never become a floor competency map or be used in compensation or review.
- Options on the document's design: one spine across all three founders with seat preambles, so the documents are comparable side by side (edges, decision drafts, dependencies) and gaps can be found; and keeping prompt B as a running inventory feeding one shared register, reviewed on a cadence, as the direct audit of whether the "runs without its founders" claim is true.

### 1.2 Say the thing you think you cannot say

- Option: every founder-level document carries an open-items section; a founder asked for a position they don't hold says "I have not decided" rather than improvising one; disagreement between founders is raised in the room where the decision is being made, not carried out of it. Reasoning: the risk named here is not a manager who filters too heavily, it's a founder fluent enough that an open question sounds settled, which is a subtler and more likely failure at Sŏn.
- Option: when something fails, ask what allowed it before asking who, applied to a founder's own domain before anyone else's, and stated in writing in a post-mortem. Reasoning: a lead should never learn in a review that a founder has been unhappy with a system for a month (the no-surprises rule, WP p.19, applied upward). The two disciplines, naming what's open before it fails and naming what allowed it after, are treated as one habit from two angles.
- Open, founder-gated: how the partners actually disagree with each other, and what a founder does when they believe another founder is wrong inside that founder's own domain, is not addressed by any source document; it has to be stated by each founder for themselves.
- Prompt E's content (how I disagree, and how to disagree with me) asks: how a founder raises a problem with another founder's work; how they want to hear they're wrong, in what form, what setting, from whom, how soon; what they do when angry and what the others should do about it; what "going meta" (talking about the conversation itself) looks like with them; and what they will and won't say in front of the team. A weak answer is "I welcome feedback" or "I'm direct" with no story and no account of what it looks like when it goes badly.

### 1.3 Distinguish between management and leadership

- Research: the standard split, leadership sets direction and handles adaptive (open-ended) problems, management executes and handles technical (solvable) problems, with experienced people needing mostly a leader and inexperienced people mostly a manager, doesn't map cleanly onto Sŏn's structure. Sŏn cuts management into two parallel co-lead roles, a strategic orientation and a coordinative orientation serving one shared vision, and each lead is described as carrying both modes rather than splitting them between two people (WP p.09 to 10).
- Option: adopt the leadership/management distinction as a triage question asked of a recurring problem, not as a split between people: does this have a fix (route it to process and the domain owner) or does it need a judgment (route it to the domain owner, or to the founders jointly when it crosses domains or touches the concept)? Reasoning: a lead who keeps applying process fixes to a judgment problem is building the wrong thing; a founder who keeps applying judgment to a fixable problem is holding the house in their head.
- Option: hold direction at the center as a written thing, the shared vision the two leads serve, rather than as a rank held by one person. Reasoning: overlaying "leader" and "manager" onto the two co-leads would either rank one above the other, which the parallel structure is designed to prevent, or split each lead into two people, reintroducing the manual-integration work the structure removed.
- Open, founder-gated: which founder carries the concept's voice to the team internally, and whether the two leads carry that voice when the founders are not in the building, is not addressed by any source document.
- Option: treat stability as a property of the system, not something a lead produces personally by holding a schedule in their head or standing in as the manual link between floor and kitchen. Reasoning: a lead doing that isn't managing well, they're compensating for a system gap, and the fix is restoring the system rather than praising the lead. Framing point: the standard model assumes management is inherently the stabilizer; that assumption does not transfer where stability is meant to be systemic.

### 1.4 Come back to your operating system

- Research: two different things get called "an operating system." The human sense is the system a team runs on: mission, goals, metrics, meetings, and hiring and development cadences. The other sense is a technology stack (a data layer, a connective layer, an AI layer, and a top surface layer; WP p.21). Conflating them causes two failure modes: concluding the human system exists because the software exists, and treating the software as a replacement for human cadence.
- Vocabulary convention proposed for every later session: "operating system" and "the cadence" always mean the human system; the software is always "the stack," never "the operating system" on its own.
- Relationship: the human cadence reads what the stack surfaces, and the stack records what the cadence does. A stack with no cadence reaches no one; a cadence with no stack forces a person to be the manual integration layer.
- What already exists of the human operating system, per the white paper: mission and intent language; leading and cultural metrics without targets; a floor cadence (a shift brief, a shift close, a pre-shift briefing weighted equal to signature work; WP p.13 to 14, p.20); a review cadence every three to six months (WP p.19); a feedback channel (WP p.22); a ninety-day onboarding cadence with check-ins (WP p.18); a hiring system and a development system (WP p.11, p.17 to 19); a brand-side cadence of daily, weekly, and monthly reads (WP p.31).
- Gaps: no goals layer (metrics exist without targets); no planning cycle for the year, the quarter, or a new service period's launch; no founder cadence for how the three partners meet, decide, and review each other's domains, though a floor cadence and a review cadence exist; no cadence for how the two co-leads sync with no one above them; no mechanism for carrying priorities to the team on an ongoing rhythm after onboarding ends.
- Metronome and tempo (closely tied to cadence): the standard advice is to set a pace faster than comfortable, since a fast-growing headcount sets that pace regardless. Sŏn has no growing headcount to force a pace, so the live question is which of Sŏn's tempos should be deliberately uncomfortable, and who sets each one.
  - Option: service tempo is set by the room and the moment, never accelerated by the founders; where a service period needs speed, the engineering happens upstream (front-loading labor in prep, moving closing labor to an overnight crew, a stack that absorbs integration work) rather than by pushing the people in the service moment itself.
  - Option: the build sequence (which service period opens when, whether a new one is added at all) is deliberately slow and gated on proof, run on its own for a full quarter before anything is added to it (WP p.36), while the build work itself (content, hiring decisions, feedback, the stack) moves fast through multiplication and system design rather than founder effort, sized explicitly to avoid burning the founders out (WP p.30 to 31). Reasoning: a build tempo that's uncomfortable because founders are working past their own bandwidth is the same bandwidth failure named elsewhere, not the metronome doing its job.
  - Risk to carry forward: the founders' build-speed habit is likely to leak into the service room in the first quarter of a new service period, when the build engine is idling and the founders are physically present on the floor.
  - Open, founder-gated: what sets a founder's own learning pace is not addressed anywhere; the development system described is built for the team, not the founders.

### Stripped

- The canon-based service-tempo architecture (a named step-back sequence, a named culinary standard, a named "master register" tone rule, and per-service-period canon citations) that supported the metronome section: stripped as resting on brand/canon material; the underlying tempo question and its white-paper-backed answer are kept.
- The canon's account of the chef seat's authority ("the brand owns the culinary identity, the chef executes within it"), used to argue that the record holds two conflicting descriptions of the chef's authority: stripped as canon-dependent; the fact that an open question exists about how the chef's authority is described is kept without quoting either source.
- Canon citations supporting tone and writing rules for the working-with-me document (a named brand voice register applied to the document's tone): stripped; the substantive writing rules (no performed conviction, no "guest," no em dashes, no daypart code names, no financial figures) are kept as house style, not as canon citations.
