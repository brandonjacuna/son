# Digest: coaching, hard feedback, and the informal feedback culture

## Part 1: What the old page holds that's worth keeping

### 5.1 Hypothesis-based coaching

**What the book asks (Book pp. 385-390, p.286).** A manager gathers data ("more than you realize, need less than you think," p.387), forms a hypothesis about a person's strengths and weaknesses from observation (including the manager's own added context: a person's "work style, preferences, strengths, and weaknesses," p.388), and tests it by saying it to the person as an observation about behavior and impact, "not a judgment about who they are as a person" (p.390). The manager may ask motivation questions: "is it new, episodic, or persistent? Could the person be experiencing something in their personal life?" (p.286). A manager may sit in on a person's other meetings "to get more insight" (p.390), may offer a promotion as a test of gaps (p.391), and files a hypothesis away to raise later. Self-perception "consistently misaligned with what you and others observe" is named as a driver of low performance (pp.390, 395).

**Sources research: what a manager can read vs. what stays a person's impression of a person.** Every source considered for building a hypothesis at Sŏn is something the person themselves can already read: their own prior check-in lines, an active plan's states and milestones, readiness-assessment rows and load reads, incident captures the person wrote for their own tables/stations, a count of whether and how they used their discretion range, dated fixes to their work domain, schedule facts (shifts worked, missed pairings, moved check-ins), and a record of skills/language connections demonstrated. The weakest possible source is a lead's own unwritten observation from a coverage position, because it is the one source the person cannot read.

Sources considered and refused as data about a person: a peer recognition (it is a peer's act about an act, never a read of a person); a channel item (a friction report, not a person-read); any diagnostic/organizational-health record (evidence lines, scorecards, reset records deliberately carry no person's name); a hiring interview scorecard (hiring assessments never carry forward into a person's ongoing record); a mentor's account (a mentor is explicitly barred from becoming a sponsor, coach, or channel for a lead); a peer's account of a peer (treated as an informal-network risk, the same kind of dependency on undocumented relationships that causes operational breakdowns); a work-style personality placement (never used as a read of anyone in a room); and a lead's own note written anywhere outside the one formal conversation.

**Option:** treat the lead's unwritten observation as a legitimate but weakest-tier source. **Reasoning:** it gives a lead a channel for what they notice without institutionalizing surveillance; because it can't be read back by the person, any hypothesis resting on it alone should wait for a record to corroborate it, or be offered explicitly as impression only.

**Option:** refuse peer accounts, mentor accounts, and personality/work-style placements as coaching data entirely. **Reasoning:** keeps the coaching system from re-creating an informal grapevine of reputation and gossip, which is a documented failure mode; costs the manager some of the "10-15 data points" richness the book recommends.

**What a hypothesis may be and may not be.** One shape only: a specific behavior in a specific service, and its impact on the work (book's own model example, p.390, is this shape: "I have a theory that you're not effectively getting important information across... when we came out of that business review, I sensed the CMO was unsure"). It may never be about who the person is (the book's own "bad communicator" is flagged, p.390, as the counter-example), never a verdict, never a placement on a skill-will grid, never a motivation question about the person's personal life, never a performance-dimension mark, never a "self-awareness gap" label.

**Where a hypothesis lives.** The book has the manager file it away and revisit later (p.386). The Sŏn design tested here keeps it unwritten anywhere: it exists only in the lead's head between the records that produced it and the one conversation at which it's tested, with no note, memo, or file. If a lead needs to hold it longer than the standard interval, the mechanism for shortening the wait (bringing a scheduled one-on-one forward) is the release valve, not writing it down.

**Testing a hypothesis (Book p.389, p.394, p.395, p.386).** Book: "the best way to test your hypothesis is to share it directly... 'I'm testing a hypothesis, let me know whether it feels right.'" Own it as perception, not as asking the person to confirm criticism (p.394); expect it can take "a few rounds before you arrive at a common understanding" (p.395); "sometimes you'll be wrong, which is in itself a data point" (p.386). Three outcomes considered: person agrees and adds their own read; person disagrees and the records are genuinely thin (the hypothesis is dropped or the lead reads more before raising it again); person disagrees but the records are not thin (the lead points to the specific dated records and asks what the person sees in them).

**Self-awareness gap, reframed.** The book's label for someone whose self-perception doesn't match what others see, said to correlate with becoming a low performer (pp.390, 395), was treated as a trait word resting on exactly the kind of "what others observe" data source ruled out above. The proposed replacement: track it as a pattern, not a label, the same hypothesis raised at consecutive check-ins, with the underlying records unchanged after any environmental fix, and no acknowledgment from the person. That pattern is what gets escalated to a later performance conversation, never the label itself.

**What a hypothesis may never become, regardless of outcome:** a verdict; a word written in the lead's own voice on any page about the person; a rating or evidence item at a formal review; an organizational-health/diagnostic finding; a task assignment; a pay decision or a factor in one; a reason cited in any change to a person's role; a listed cause on an incident report; or a citation in any decision about whether someone stays. The stated design principle: because nothing about a hypothesis is ever written down, nothing exists later for any other process to cite.

### 5.2 Giving hard feedback

**What the book asks (Book pp. 392-395; Workbook pp. 113-116).** "Be an explorer, not a lecturer" (p.392): open with a neutral, open-ended question, or an empathetic observation that is "supportive, objective, and specific instead of emotional and generic" (p.394), own it as the manager's perception, cycle between question and observation until the two people share an account of the problem, solutions only after that. The workbook provides a written recap letter template after a hard-feedback conversation structured as "the situation, the observable behaviors, and the impact they had" (p.113), plus three follow-on letters for progress update / back on track / insufficient progress (Workbook pp.114-116).

**The two openings, kept as a way of talking, not a form to fill out.** First opening: a neutral open-ended question about a specific, recent, real event ("how did the Saturday shift go for you," not "do you think you're bad at X," which the book itself flags as a leading question that signals a made-up mind, p.393). If the person's own answer already surfaces the issue, that is the strongest possible outcome, their own hypothesis about a development area (p.386), and nothing more needs to be said. Second opening, used only if the first doesn't surface it: situation-behavior-impact as a spoken observation ("on these two dates the record shows X; both times Y happened, and it produced Z"), never generic or trend-based ("you've gotten a lot worse" is the book's own flagged example of what not to say, p.394, because it's unreadable from any specific record). Cycling between the two, and a stated last resort of the observer giving their own flat impression with an example, are both kept intact from the book; the verdict that would naturally follow that last resort is the one thing refused.

**Option:** no written recap of a hard-feedback conversation exists in any form; the person's own line, written and read back at the one formal conversation, is the only record, and it can be as long as the person wants to make it. **Reasoning:** a manager's written summary is inherently the manager's voice about a person, which conflicts with keeping the person as the sole author of their own record; it also means there's no institutional document that could later calcify into a performance narrative outside the person's control.

**Option:** keep the three follow-on progress letters (Workbook pp.114-116) but design their actual form in a different, later piece of work, under the constraint that whatever that design is, it still writes only the person's own words at any regular check-in. **Reasoning:** avoids deciding now whether/how a documented improvement arc can exist at all without contradicting the "no one but the person writes about the person" principle; flags a real tension between wanting a paper trail for formal performance processes and wanting no manager-authored narrative.

**Failure typing and blame absorption, a mechanism, not a posture (WP p.13, p.17, p.14, p.26; profile concept of failure types: basic/complex/intelligent).** WP: "the first question is not who failed, it is what allowed this to happen. Systems are designed to prevent, catch, and recover from errors. Management absorbs blame from staff. Post-mortems are private and process-focused" (p.17); "the customer should never feel at fault, even when they are" (p.26); "before intervening at the people level, identify which failure mode is actually present" (p.14).

Three failure types considered, each with a different consequence: a **basic failure** (a known, documented process simply not followed) is met with a process placement fix, and only reaches the person as a hypothesis if the same failure recurs on the same role after the fix is already made. A **complex failure** (a combination of factors, no single step wrong) is met with a process fix only, there is deliberately no person in this category. An **intelligent failure** (a judgment call, taken within someone's authorized discretion, that didn't work out) is met with the system absorbing the lesson and the person not corrected at all, correcting a good-faith judgment call as though it were an error is treated as the exact failure this design exists to prevent.

**Option:** type every failure at a process-review step (never on a person's individual record), track counts by type at a periodic operational review, and never track failure counts by individual person. **Reasoning:** keeps the record's stated "no blame" philosophy mechanically true rather than aspirational; risk if not followed: a capture or incident report that names a person as the cause quietly becomes an individual performance record by the back door, defeating the whole design.

**Risk / open tension:** whether a hard correction can ever be said informally, in person, between formal one-on-ones, and only written up afterward, versus corrections happening only inside the one designated conversation format. Both positions exist in the older material and were never reconciled; the argument for allowing informal in-person correction is that forbidding it makes candor a fully scheduled, ambush-feeling event; the argument against is that any lag between saying something and recording it lets it live only in the manager's memory, unreadable by the person, which recreates exactly the "manager's private notebook" problem the whole design is trying to avoid. This is a live sequencing question for Sŏn to decide, not resolved by either side of the old material.

**Two kinds of live-service speech, and where correction of a person is never allowed.** Distinction drawn: an "operating instruction" has the object of the work as its subject and a present action as its verb, and is complete the moment the action happens ("fire the four-top," "hold the sequence," "re-set the table"); "feedback about a person" has the person as the subject and a pattern or quality as the predicate, and is never complete because it's asking for a change in the person, not the completion of an act. The first is treated as native to live service and kept entirely intact. The second is never said in front of other staff or customers, during service, at any point, it always waits for a private, one-on-one conversation. A live instruction that has to be repeated at the same person on the same task in one shift is read as a process or training-readiness problem to fix, not a cue to correct the person in the moment.

**Risk / sequencing:** a correction repeated live to the same person during one shift, if treated as a personal failing rather than a process signal, risks becoming exactly the public correction the design is built to prevent; the recommended sequencing is: the instruction/fix happens live and in the moment; the incident is logged that night; the person's own account of the incident is recorded before any manager impression forms into a hypothesis; the actual conversation about it, if warranted, happens at the next private one-on-one, which can be moved earlier if needed but is never held on the floor.

**Legal/counsel-adjacent question (kept verbatim in substance from a related item, though its main subject sits outside this chunk):** a separate item on departure conversations flags that "what may be said and retained" in a person's record after a departure needs counsel's answer before any retention design is finalized, noted here because it bears on how far back a coaching or hard-feedback record could ever be referenced.

### 5.3 Creating a culture of informal feedback

**What the book asks (Book pp.395-398).** Informal feedback is "the one-off pieces of advice or observations people offer to help colleagues... in their day-to-day work" (p.395); the book's own diagnosis of a comparison company is that it's good at feedback on work product but weaker on "bidirectional, informal feedback" where "people frequently speak up and say something constructive without necessarily being asked" (p.396). The chapter's four "asking for feedback" pointers: ask on different occasions, through different forums; normalize the practice by mentioning received feedback at team meetings; let feedback sit before reacting; follow up on it afterward (pp.397-398). "Praise publicly and criticize privately" (p.396). Team feedback, unlike individual feedback, "should be data-driven and can be presented as an honest assessment of how things are going" publicly (pp.396-397). A cited example: "to be brutally honest you can't have a culture that's brutal" (p.396).

**Directions candor can move in, and what surface/record each uses:**
- Lead to person: the private one-on-one, a version of it moved earlier when something can't wait, and live operating instructions during service. Never a public room, a written message, a note, or a designation (a temporary duty assignment) used as reward or punishment.
- Person to lead: a standing upward question at every one-on-one, in the person's own words, written only if they choose; and a structured feedback channel for concerns about the house, a tool, a process, or a space, with a named owner and a resolution window. **Risk / open gap:** there is no clear destination yet for a concern specifically about the person's own manager, flagged explicitly as an unresolved hole rather than something the coaching system fixes.
- Peer to peer: positive recognition, written as a peer's own words about a specific act, read aloud at a team gathering. Corrective feedback between peers is handled two ways: during service, only as an operating instruction inside someone's actual task authority (e.g., a food-runner correcting another runner about a table, because it's their job in that moment); between shifts, informal spoken peer-to-peer correction is neither required, built a surface for, nor recorded by the house, it is simply left as something colleagues may or may not say to each other, and is refused only in three cases: said where other staff can overhear it, written down anywhere, or turned into an expected norm the house asks people to perform.
- To/from a person holding a temporary designation/duty: feedback about how they performed in that duty is delivered at their normal one-on-one, citing the specific incident, and the duty is never withheld as a punishment or granted as a reward.

**Option:** build no formal mechanism at all for informal between-shift peer correction, no surface, no requirement, no record, treating it as ordinary colleague conversation the house has no business managing. **Reasoning:** the book's own diagnosis names *not asking* as the failure to fix, but every constructive thing a person could say already has a designated non-peer destination (an incident report for a failure, the feedback channel for a friction, a joint working document for an interface disagreement); a peer choosing to say something informally to another peer instead is treated as their choice to make, and the house declines to build a norm pressuring anyone to do that peer-correction work.

**Recognition decline, handled without becoming a correction of the peer.** When a public recognition submission actually functions as a disguised correction, a state-of-the-person judgment, or a comparison between people, the person who would read it aloud instead declines to read it, and separately tells the peer who wrote it, in person and privately, one sentence about which platform rule it didn't meet and one sentence about what would have met it, never asking why they wrote it, never relitigating its content, never doing this in front of others. Nothing about the peer is written down; the recognition stays on record as simply "unread." A repeated pattern of declines from one person is treated first as a question about whether the recognition prompt itself is inviting the wrong kind of writing, before it's treated as a question about that person.

**Option:** treat a repeat pattern of declined recognitions as a signal to review the recognition system's own prompt/design first. **Reasoning:** avoids quietly turning a "declined recognition" count into a backdoor performance measure of the person who keeps submitting them.

**Her four "asking for feedback" pointers, translated:** "ask on different occasions/forums" becomes the single standing upward question asked at every regular one-on-one (so asking is never a manager's occasional initiative); "normalize by mentioning feedback at team meetings" is refused as a practice, the reasoning given is that any mention of an individual in that setting risks becoming exactly the kind of public exposure the design elsewhere refuses, and a general "here's what we learned" framing at a team gathering does the normalizing work instead; "let it sit" is handled by having the recipient repeat back what they heard without immediately explaining or defending; "follow up" is handled structurally by the feedback channel's resolution window and reporting the outcome back at the next one-on-one.

**"Praise publicly, criticize privately" and public team feedback, as existing mechanisms rather than new policy:** public praise is simply the existing peer-recognition mechanism (never a manager's own public praise, treated as a separate class to avoid a manager-endorsement dynamic); private criticism is the one-on-one; public team-level feedback is a short, evidence-based read on whether a team's stated goal was met, delivered as one sentence with the reasoning, never as a manager's personal narrative-style assessment of a team's performance.

**The culture stated as a property of mechanisms, not a disposition:** the working thesis carried through this material is that candor should not be asked of anyone as a value or an attitude; it should be a predictable property of a system where every hard thing has a specific, private, recorded destination, nothing difficult is ever said in a group setting, and no page anywhere holds a sentence about a person that the person themselves didn't write. Whether that's achievable, versus needing some explicit cultural expectation-setting, is itself framed as an open question rather than a settled fact.

**Cross-cutting risk, all three chunks:** the record contains three genuinely different uses of the word "feedback", feedback about tools/systems from use data, feedback upward about management, feedback about the house's mechanisms and processes, and feedback to a person about their own work, with no distinction drawn between them, which risks a hard performance conversation being accidentally routed onto a channel meant for process complaints, or a process complaint being mistakenly treated as a read of a person. Recommend the language be disambiguated by surface (which destination) rather than relying on the single word "feedback."

**Additional unresolved question surfaced (not a legal question but treated with similar weight):** what counts as the kind of severe misconduct that must never be handled as ordinary coaching or feedback (referred to as "the non-negotiable breach") is a name without a definition anywhere in the source material; the boundary between hard feedback and that category is currently undrawn and was flagged as needing an explicit definition before it can be relied on operationally.
