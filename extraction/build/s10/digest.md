# S10 digest: creating the team environment (book pp. 304-330)

Session task and reasoning previously lived on the S10 Sŏn Operating System doc page; that ledger is retired. This digest keeps what's worth carrying into the 4.5 chunk.

## The book's model vs. the operating structure already built

- Book's premise: a manager builds team culture by convening people, through offsites (2-3 days/year senior teams, at least 1/year junior teams, planned against Tuckman's four stages) and meetings (groundwork, roles, norms, PAL, decision methods, decision logs) (Book pp. 304-328).
- Option: treat culture as built by structure rather than by convening, i.e. every condition the book's offsites/meetings exist to create gets assigned to a standing mechanism that runs without a manager calling a room together. Reasoning: prior sessions (S3-S9) already built a brief, a close, a check-in, a leads' review, a partners' review, a house review, a mechanism reset, a decision-rights register, and per-service designations that do most of this work without anyone convening a meeting for it.
- WP citations grounding this: "great nights feel like distributed intelligence rather than coordinated direction... not a management achievement... the structure functioning correctly" (WP p.8); order is "Bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results" (WP p.12); day's rhythm "Beginning of shift, prepare for the customer. During service, serve them. End of shift, reflect and strengthen the relationship" (WP p.26); errors are the process's, "Management absorbs blame from staff. Post-mortems are private and process-focused" (WP p.17); feedback "runs both directions" (WP p.19); recognition is peer-to-peer "in the moment" (WP p.19); a real employee area "is not a perk. It is basic dignity" (WP pp.19-20); generosity pre-authorized, "their judgment is trusted" (WP p.26); the culture measure named is "engagement results across psychological safety and values alignment" (WP p.23).
- Open question carried forward: whether "conditions for agency" for employees (see below) is fully built out as mechanisms or remains partly aspirational; several sub-questions below name exactly where it's incomplete.

## What "conditions for agency" means for a person on the floor (ten conditions, decomposed)

This decomposition is useful as a checklist independent of its origin; the six-question canon-test procedure it was run through is stripped (see Stripped).

1. Legible rules: person can read, before acting, the rules of seat/service/house.
2. A stated range: authority to act without asking, limits and escalation destinations written in advance.
3. Judgment built, not procedure only: training reaches independent judgment and something reads whether it did.
4. Safety to err: an error is the process's first, captured, never a verdict on the person.
5. Safety to speak: a question, dissent, failure report, and concern about a lead each have a destination, and something reads whether they arrive.
6. Being known: the house holds the person in their own words/language and knows what it must supply for them.
7. A path: the next step is visible before asked for; advancement doesn't wait on a manager.
8. Time that is not taken: paid pre-service window, check-in on the clock, close, and slack exist and are tracked.
9. A say in the rules: what a person reports changes the process; what the floor reports reaches the rules.
10. A place of their own: physical space, described in WP as "a precondition for presence" (WP p.20).

- Note: "belonging among the team" is treated as an outcome these conditions produce, not a condition itself; retention sits third in WP's order, after bandwidth and joy (WP p.12).
- Note: "vulnerability" (one of the book's four psychological-safety risk acts) is treated as something the house does not solicit, distinct from the conditions above.

### Status of each condition, per-condition detail

For each, keeping: what mechanism already exists (from S3-S9), what's missing, what would complete it, and who'd need to decide it. Verdict labels ("mechanism/partial/slogan") themselves are retired framing but the underlying gaps are real and worth carrying.

- **Legible rules.** Built via a seat sheet, a range card, non-negotiables delivered before operational training ("why comes before how," WP p.18), a team-facing reference surface. Gap: the onboarding "why block" is empty until the founding charter is ratified, so the rules a person reads first are, today, unwritten. Read of absence: "employees do not know what advancement requires because the competencies are not documented or accessible" (WP p.14).
- **A stated range.** Built via a decision-rights rule (authority sits with closest competent owner; range stated in advance; triggers named before the event), floor authority ("server owns the table"), a generosity range ("every server carries a bottle to gift as they see fit," WP p.26). Gap: numeric dollar thresholds and "known limits" are unset; a pre-opening cohort would train on a range whose money edge is a sentence rather than a number.
- **Judgment built, not procedure only.** Built via a training sequence (modules, live session, observed/paired services, readiness read, solo) and a self-paced skill-unlock system. Gap: nothing reads whether judgment keeps growing after the readiness form is passed, except by inference from how often a person's range gets used.
- **Safety to err.** The most complete condition: "blame the process," a capture logged at close (faster than a thank-you note), private process-focused post-mortems off the floor, no verdict on a person's file, no discipline via the check-in.
- **Safety to speak.** Four speech acts, each needing a destination: a question (mentor, check-in); a dissent (a written joint hold, no unilateral case heard); a failure report (the capture); a concern about the house/lead (check-in, a feedback channel with 7-day acknowledge/30-day close windows, a monthly anonymous pulse). Biggest gap: a concern about the lead who holds someone's own check-in currently has no destination other than that lead; the pulse is one anonymous question with no content. Depends on a still-open decision about who holds a lead's own check-in.
- **Being known.** Built via a person page in the person's own words, read back at check-ins; interviews/briefs in the person's own language; a mentor matched for language/overlap; a one-time career conversation. Gap: thin among peers, since the roster changes every service — a person is mostly "known" through the mentor and peer recognition, not ongoing peer contact.
- **A path.** WP: "the path is explicit before they start it... employees do not need to ask what it takes to advance" (WP p.11); a self-paced skill-unlock system "so no one's advancement waits on a manager's attention or judgment" (WP p.11); no levels/ladders. Gap: can't run until a learning platform and first module set actually exist.
- **Time that is not taken.** Built via the paid pre-service window ("the highest-leverage time a restaurant has"), a check-in as the first 15 minutes of a shift, a protected close window, a time-cost ledger arguing minimal standing meeting load. Gap: the close window's actual length is unstated, a "cultural labor score" doesn't exist yet, and labor-model slack isn't independently verifiable — a lean schedule and a slack-having one "look identical... on a calm night."
- **A say in the rules.** Built on the process side: nightly captures update the process ("the next server inherits a system that has already learned from it," WP p.13), a feedback channel syncing to tool changes ("continuous feedback loops so the tools evolve based on real usage," WP p.17). Gap on the rules side: the annual reset/revision is run by leadership, not the floor directly; floor input is read from records rather than solicited live. A quarterly open question to the whole staff (if a gathering exists) would be the one direct channel.
- **A place of their own.** WP: a real break space with secure lockers "is not a perk. It is basic dignity, and dignity is a precondition for presence" (WP pp.19-20); "If it matters, Sŏn pays for it" (WP p.19); a locker assigned before arrival (WP p.18). Gap: the least built of the ten — a real-estate item with no priced build plan and no line currently reading whether it exists.

## Designed autonomy against drift, both directions

- Risk direction 1 (rules tightening until the result is foreclosed): three failure modes to watch for on a real floor. (a) Scripted service under rush pressure, because scripts are reproducible by a green crew — guarded against by training that reaches judgment rather than a script, and by reading unused ranges at the reset as a red flag rather than a well-run section. (b) Procedure-only training that measures compliance and stops, rather than reaching judgment — guarded by a module standard requiring a judgment-tested row and by skill unlocks running on a schedule rather than a manager's sign-off. (c) A lead's personal standard quietly becoming an unwritten rule (e.g. "here's how I want it done tonight" at a brief) — this is flagged as the drift the current design is *least* defended against, since there's no single owner or versioned document for "the emotional standard of the room," only a role (Maitre d) that holds it informally. Guard in place: a change that isn't on a versioned register/sheet/module isn't treated as a rule, and check-in lines naming "the lead's way" get aggregated as a friction signal reviewed weekly.
- Risk direction 2 (openness loosening until the space stops feeling safe): an act with no stated limit (unset dollar/numeric thresholds are a real current gap); a shift with no one clearly holding the room; a topic with no named destination; a failure with no capture; and behavior that breaches a hard, non-negotiable line (e.g. any form of exploitation, toxicity, treating people as disposable) rather than being read as a process failure. Where the record is silent: what happens when someone uses their range badly (not over the limit, but inside it and wrong) has no answer here — flagged as a dependency for whatever chunk handles coaching/feedback and performance management.
- During the first operating quarter (freeze period), the working assumption is that rules should not move at all: the "loose edge" is guarded by rules staying fixed, and the "tight edge" by there being exactly one moment (the first reset) where they can be revisited on evidence rather than by anyone's assertion mid-quarter.

## How a service team actually forms, storms, norms, performs

- Book's Tuckman model (forming/storming/norming/performing, Book pp. 306-308) assumes one fixed group of people meeting repeatedly. Sŏn's "team" is actually three different objects, and the stages land differently for each:
  - **The nightly service roster** (whoever's working tonight, across all stations): dissolves at close, so it can't storm or norm as a group — it has to "perform" on its first service. Forming happens at the brief (naming who's holding which role for the night); conflict resolution happens through pre-set rules (who covers what if a station goes down) rather than the group "working through" a storming phase; a genuinely unanticipated conflict becomes a capture at close, not a group process.
  - **The house** (the whole staff as one standing team): forms once, pre-opening, through onboarding cohorts and training services; "storms" (if that word still applies) during the first operating quarter, which is exactly why rules are frozen during that quarter; "norms" at the first quarterly mechanism reset, where every mechanism gets kept, changed, or ended based on the quarter's actual record.
  - **The onboarding cohort**: forms through the training sequence itself (modules, live session, observed/paired services, a "why" briefing on non-negotiables before operational training, a readiness read) — this already matches the book's forming-stage guidance (be directive, provide extra support and modeling, build psychological safety through supportive norms) without needing translation.
  - **A domain** (e.g. all of front-of-house, or kitchen): doesn't form as a team at all in Tuckman's sense; people in a domain relate to their lead one-on-one and to each other only via the shared roster.
- Book's Table 8 (task-focused vs. team-focused offsite activities, pp. 308-309): task-focused items (metrics, goals, retrospectives, long-term strategy) already have a fixed home in existing weekly/quarterly reviews. Team-focused items: "defining team norms" as a group exercise is not adopted (norms are written once, versioned, not agreed per-group); "discussing roles and accountability" happens nightly via one-line role call-outs at the brief; a "team building" activity as such is not adopted; **a staff meal is the one team-focused idea on this list that the white paper and other source material are silent on** (see below — this is a real open item).

## The staff meal: a genuine silence worth flagging

- Fact: neither the white paper nor other Sŏn source material mentions a pre-shift staff meal, even though it's the one team-building mechanism the restaurant industry already runs daily, at essentially no incremental "meeting" cost, inside the paid pre-service window.
- The pre-service window itself is described as time to "prepare for the customer" (WP p.26), with no stated length.
- This should not be resolved by inventing an answer from nothing. The practical constraint worth naming for whoever decides: a meal that fits inside the kitchen's existing prep window without displacing other pre-service work is a real possibility; a meal that would displace the pre-shift briefing, or add meaningful kitchen labor/cost on a busy night, is a different and costlier decision. Timing matters: this is best decided before the pre-service window's shape gets locked in during training, since adding a meal after the fact would displace something else already scheduled there.
- This is a joint operational/kitchen decision (needs kitchen leadership's read on cost and feasibility, not just a floor-side call).

## Psychological safety: what the environment can build without any coaching program yet existing

- Book's definition (from Project Aristotle, via the book): "the ability to take interpersonal risks like asking questions, offering a dissenting opinion, discussing a failure, or expressing vulnerability without fear of negative repercussions" (Book p.306), which the book treats as necessary for a team to progress between developmental stages.
- What the environment (structure, not a coaching conversation) can do for each of the book's four risk acts, before any feedback/coaching system exists:
  - **Asking a question**: routed to a mentor or the check-in's "what's in the way" prompt or a feedback channel; none of these three creates a written record about the person asking.
  - **Offering a dissent**: routed to a written joint hold document (both sides state the case in writing; no one side gets heard alone) or the check-in's upward question; a dissent becomes a record with a destination, not a verbal confrontation in a room.
  - **Discussing a failure**: routed to the close-of-shift capture, under "blame the process" — post-mortems are private and process-focused (WP p.17), and management is described as absorbing blame from staff (WP p.17).
  - **Expressing vulnerability**: treated as something the house does not solicit or build a mechanism for; whatever a person volunteers at a check-in is recorded only in their own words if and when they choose to say it. What the house offers instead is physical dignity (a real break space, secure lockers), described in the white paper as "a precondition for presence" (WP p.20), not an emotional prompt.
- A seventh mechanism worth naming as a safety-builder: a pre-authorized range of action is itself a safety mechanism, because "pre-authorizing it signals trust to staff" (WP p.26) — the size of a person's authorized range is effectively the size of what they can do without fear of being second-guessed.
- Explicit dependency/gap: what actually happens when someone raises a failure — is it met with a straightforward process fix, or does severity get triaged differently for a simple mistake vs. a complex one vs. a repeat pattern — is not decided here; it belongs with whatever chunk covers coaching, feedback, and performance response. Similarly, what happens when a hard non-negotiable line is actually crossed by a person (not a process failure) is not decided here; only that such a report should reach senior leadership same-day and never be aired in the group brief or close.
- Finding worth flagging: the white paper's own stated measure of culture, "engagement results across psychological safety and values alignment" (WP p.23), is survey-shaped, but survey-based instruments have generally been avoided elsewhere in this program as an information source. Substitutes discussed: the capture rate, the rate of process updates following a capture, and a simple recurring one-question pulse — none of which is fully equivalent to a real psychological-safety survey, and all of which would need a baseline established from an actual first operating quarter.
- On the "no topics are undiscussable" idea (Book pp. 317-318): the practical inversion worth carrying is that every topic should have a *named destination* (who/where it goes), and the real risk is a topic with no destination at all, not a topic no one wants to raise. Concretely, a concern about a lead which that same lead would otherwise be the one to receive is exactly this kind of undestined topic, tied to the open decision about who holds a lead's own check-in.
- On correcting behavior inside a meeting-like setting (Book pp. 327-328): the book's own example of naming someone's "work style" out loud in a room (e.g. calling someone "acting a bit red") is a useful negative example — the working assumption here is that no one should be corrected or characterized in front of the group; a chair/room-owner can decline an off-topic item and end on time, but any actual correction of an individual belongs in a private one-on-one conversation, not in a group setting.

## Offsites: whether Sŏn holds any at all

- Book's stated purposes for an offsite: evolve a group into a team; evaluate progress and set near-term priorities; build long-term strategic thinking (Book p.305).
- Option A — no offsite for the broad staff (the "house"): reasoning is that a whole-staff offsite costs a closed day of service, which is already a real cost decision on its own, and the onboarding cohort plus training services already do the team-forming work; ongoing team-state is read from operational evidence (capture rates, feedback patterns, etc.) rather than from a periodic offsite.
- Option B — no separate offsite for department leads: reasoning is that leads already meet weekly with a hard stop and meet founders monthly; an annual "reset" style session (see below) already covers the offsite's real content for that group.
- Option C — one closed-hours block per year for founders/ownership only, combining a "mechanism reset" (reviewing what's working, what should change) and a "principles revision" (checking stated principles against what actually happened over the year), optionally off-site by choice, at no cost to service, carrying no role in reading team morale. This is essentially the book's offsite reduced to its core decision content, without the interpersonal-ritual scaffolding (openers, icebreakers, a facilitator role, a feedback form).
- The one existing thing that already resembles an offsite, without being labeled one: a single onboarding orientation block per cohort wave, done with a founder present, delivering the "why" before operational training begins — this already functions as the book's "forming" work.
- Specific book-offsite elements and their fate (options, not verdicts): a personal check-in-style opener → not adopted, treated as an unwanted vulnerability prompt; icebreakers → not adopted; the agenda structure (purpose/agenda/limit/notes) → already how every existing review is run; a rotating notetaker role → not adopted as a separate role, since each review already has its own written record; a spoken closing round → not adopted as spoken, but the same function (reflection) exists in writing, added to the record and read aloud at the next review's opening; a shared pre-read planning document → already how monthly and quarterly reviews work; a written feedback form after the offsite → not adopted, treated as a survey instrument.

## Meetings generally: the book's meeting material against existing structures

- The book's premise "when you think of teams, you think of meetings" (Book p.311) doesn't match how the floor actually operates: there's no meeting during live service, only a brief (opening) and a close (ending), both addressed to a crew about to work or having just finished.
- Book's roles: a "directly responsible individual" (DRI) → not adopted as a separate role; every existing mechanism instead has an owner with a defined range of authority, which the book's DRI concept doesn't include. A facilitator role → folded into whoever chairs a given review (e.g. an alternating chair for the leads' weekly review) rather than a separate, fixed facilitator. A notetaker role → not a separate role; each mechanism's own record serves this function.
- Book's meeting norms (Book pp. 316-319), and how each maps: fixed meeting times and a hard stop before whatever comes next (already the case); pre-reads distributed roughly a day ahead with dedicated reading time before a review (already the case); "no undiscussable topics" → addressed via the "every topic needs a destination" principle above; "disagree and commit" → the disagreement itself gets written down rather than assumed resolved, and support for a final decision is checked later against what actually happened, not assumed as buy-in; a "parking lot" for off-topic items → not adopted as a device, since off-agenda items already have a defined channel to go to instead; periodic reconfiguration of meeting norms (book suggests every 3-6 months) → handled by the same annual/quarterly reset process rather than a separate meeting-norms review.
- Book's cost-of-meetings argument (Book p.312, "it's a good exercise to calculate how much time... meetings cost") → this program's counterpart is a time-cost ledger tracking the total paid-time cost of every standing mechanism per person/lead/founder, used to argue the total time commitment for an hourly team member stays low (brief + close + a 15-minute check-in + one pulse question + occasional recognition + periodic reviews).
- Decision-making methods (book offers autocratic, consensus, democratic, consultative, delegated, Book p.324): Option — fix decision rights in advance via a standing register (who owns which decision, what range they can act in without asking, what triggers an escalation and to where) rather than choosing a method live in a room. This effectively makes "delegated" the default form and treats "consultative" as a rule (certain decision classes require consulting affected parties before deciding, not just informing them after). A founders'-level decision rule for cases requiring joint agreement between exactly two founding partners (versus more than two) is flagged as still open and not decided by this material.
- Book's decision-making frameworks (RAPID, SPADE, etc., Book p.326) and personality-based decision exercises (Book p.325) → not adopted as separate frameworks; the standing decision-rights register is treated as the one framework taught to the team.
- Decision logs (Book pp. 326-327) → the book's caution that a decision log is "most effective when... rigorously kept current" is used as the reasoning for embedding decision records directly inside each mechanism's own existing record (e.g. a goal page's change history, a founders' page) rather than creating one separate spreadsheet-style log that could go stale.

## Leadership team meetings (book pp. 328-330)

- Book's "first team" concept (leaders should prioritize the leadership team's success over their own department's, per Lencioni, Book p.329): Option — reject this as a strict priority ordering; department leads are described as parallel/co-equal rather than in a hierarchy with each other, so a lead prioritizing a shared leadership meeting over their own department could itself create a management layer that's meant to be avoided. What the book's concern is actually protecting against (a lead hiding their department's real problems from the leadership group) is instead treated as a signal-integrity problem: a department issue that never surfaces in the cross-department leads' review is itself the failure to catch.
- Stripe's Monday-morning meeting cadence (three hours: written snippets circulated the night before, then metrics, calendar, hiring, and open topics, Book p.329): the analogous existing structure is a shorter, fixed-agenda weekly leads' review with a hard stop, where written updates (a nightly close-of-shift note, a lead's weekly written note) substitute for the book's "snippets" concept.
- The book's supplemental/expanded leadership-team idea, once a company outgrows a small core group (Book p.330), and its caution against "20 or 30 people... debating key company decisions" (Book p.330): Option — no permanent supplemental leadership tier; new leadership lines get added only when a new service period's staffing plan explicitly requires one, decided at that period's own planning gate, not by growing a standing leadership meeting's invite list.

## The work style/personality grid: does it get any team-wide use

- The grid (a work-style/personality typing tool referenced earlier in the program) is already placed as a personal self-awareness document for founders and leads, not used in scheduling, structure, or placement decisions.
- The open question carried into this chunk: should it become shared team vocabulary people use about each other, or stay founder/lead-only.
- Option A (reject broader team use): reasoning — the book's own example of using the grid in a meeting (describing a colleague as "acting a bit red") is exactly a labeling of another person in a group setting; a floor with a fast turnover and no fixed roster is more likely to misuse a typology as an instant, superficial label ("we need a [type] tonight") than to use it thoughtfully; hourly staff aren't asked to produce the kind of self-reflective document (e.g. a "working with me" doc) that leads and founders write, so there's no natural place for a floor person to record a self-placement in the first place; and there's already a separate, non-typological vocabulary in use for describing differences in work (distinguishing kinds of work rather than kinds of person).
- Option B (one narrow, one-directional use): a lead's own self-placement, written in their own "working with me" document, could be visible to people in that lead's department and referenced upward (e.g. at a check-in, or via a feedback channel) — but never used to describe someone else, never downward, and never appearing on any operational record (schedule, person file, review notes).
- A practical safeguard worth carrying: since misuse (the grid's typology appearing on a schedule, a person's file, a brief, or review notes) is a real risk, some kind of periodic check/search across those records for grid terminology outside its approved contexts is worth doing.

## Community and mentoring after onboarding; designations as rotation

- Onboarding is designed to create "a sense of community with other new hires" through the cohort experience itself, which is thought to meet this need before opening. Open question: does the steady state (after opening, when new hires arrive one at a time into an already-running team) need any additional community-building mechanism.
- Option (no new mechanism, acknowledging a real gap): rely on the existing mentor-matching system (same work area, matched for language/overlap), paired shifts with a mentor described as "the teaching body," a brief that names a new hire's first solo shift as a small recognized fact, and peer recognition; acknowledge candidly that this may build a weaker sense of team belonging than what's built for customers, since customer-facing belonging gets much more design attention in the white paper than staff belonging does. One track record note: hiring a small batch of new people on the same start date (so later hires arrive as a small group rather than solo) is a scheduling option worth considering, not a fully designed mechanism, and has a real trade-off (an open position stays open longer while waiting to batch hires).
- Mentor relationship after a person's formal onboarding/training plan closes: whether the relationship continues is the person's own choice, expressed once (e.g. at a later check-in). Option: the house does not "score" or formally track a continuing mentor relationship, and doesn't require it or assign a new role to it (e.g. not "sponsor," "coach," or a private channel to leadership); if a person wants to keep working near their old mentor, this is treated as an ordinary scheduling preference, honored when possible; a former mentee typically becomes eligible to mentor others themselves once their own onboarding plan is complete, which is how the mentor pool is meant to replenish itself.
- Rotating designations (temporary per-shift role assignments like "who's running the pass tonight," not permanent titles): Option — build in an explicit rotation requirement so that no one eligible person holds a given rotating role on literally every shift they work in a quarter while another eligible person never gets it; this should be tracked as a scheduling/fairness metric, not read as a judgment on any individual's performance, and a designation should never carry a pay differential, a title, or a note on someone's personnel file. Risk to guard against: a lead informally always tapping "the person who's good at it" for a rotating role, which quietly turns a rotation into a permanent unofficial position.

## Bandwidth as the primary read of team environment health

- Reasoning: the white paper frames organizational bandwidth as "the organizational metabolic rate" and warns that "an organization running on building logic... has no way to measure bandwidth. It measures output" (WP p.12) — so a team-environment mechanism should be read against whether it costs bandwidth, not just whether it produced its intended output.
- Two new environment-specific things worth tracking as bandwidth signals: how often a shift brief's short team-content slot gets dropped entirely (a sign the pre-service window is too compressed), and how often a shift's closing note/incident capture gets cut short or skipped in favor of the other (a sign the close window is too short).
- Principle worth carrying: a goal that gets hit at the cost of team bandwidth (e.g. burning out a section to make a number) should be scored as a kind of failure at the time it happens, not just noted afterward; the same logic should apply to a team-environment mechanism itself — a brief slot or a gathering that technically "ran" but only by cutting into needed rest/prep time should be read as a failed mechanism, not a working one.
- Explicit known gaps: there's no defined "cultural labor score" yet, no stated length for the close window, and no independent way for this program to verify real slack/slack-lessness in the labor model — three real, unresolved measurement gaps.

## The founder side and the chef partner's side of "conditions for agency"

- The ten employee conditions above were also read loosely against founders and a chef/culinary partner, with the intent to be honest about how one-sided the design work has been so far:
  - Rules, decision range, and having a say in the rules are strong for founders, for the obvious reason that founders wrote the rules.
  - Time not taken and having a visible development path are notably *weaker* for founders than for staff — the existing design was written by founders about the team, and doesn't really turn the same lens back on the founders' own workload or growth trajectory. This is flagged as a real gap, not resolved here.
  - Founders' personal interiority, values, and working style are explicitly out of scope for this kind of document and route elsewhere.
  - The chef/culinary partner's conditions are only partially covered (their formal role, scope of authority during service and in menu direction, and a three-way founder vote on at least one topic — lunch service — are the pieces that exist); most of the chef partner's own working conditions are treated as the chef partner's own to define, not something to be decided for them here.

## Selected findings (internal tensions / silences worth someone resolving)

- The stated measure of team culture in the white paper ("engagement results across psychological safety and values alignment," WP p.23) is a survey-style measure, while this program has generally avoided survey instruments elsewhere; there's a real gap between what's promised as a measurement and what's actually instrumented.
- Belonging/dignity is described in much more developed, concrete detail for customers (a multi-visit arc building recognition and familiarity) than for staff, where the equivalent commitment is mainly physical (a break space, lockers) rather than a relational system; worth naming honestly rather than assuming parity exists.
- "Management absorbs blame from staff" is stated as a one-directional principle, but there's no stated process for what happens when a failure genuinely is a person's fault (e.g. an actual serious violation) rather than the process's — this is a real gap in the record, not just an omission in this synthesis.
- The pre-service window's actual length is never stated numerically anywhere in the source material, which matters because a check-in, a brief, and (if adopted) a staff meal would all need to fit inside it.
- The idea that "employees are the users" of the systems built for them (WP p.17, explicitly noting the founders' own past pattern of building systems for themselves and then blaming employees when they don't generalize) is itself is in tension with the fact that every mechanism proposed in this program so far was designed by founders/this process before any actual employee used it — worth remembering that the real test only comes after the first live quarter of actual use and feedback.
