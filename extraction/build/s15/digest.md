# S15 digest: managing managers, and managing out/firing/layoffs

## Part 1: What the old page holds that's worth keeping

### Managing managers (book pp. 449-453)

Research and how it lands in a restaurant:
- The old work's core problem: her material assumes a manager of managers who holds 1:1s with managers and does skip levels, and a company that can fire fast after a documented case. It also assumes an HR function and employment lawyers who run separations, a severance matrix and exit packages, and a funded company's layoff as restructuring. None of those functions exist as designed.
- Two lead seats (Maitre d, Operations Lead) sit parallel with no one above them; a "manager of managers" layer would recreate the single point of failure the two-lead split exists to avoid.
- The 1:1 becomes a monthly lead-and-partner conversation on the clock, at a closed hour, on the lead's own page, not a report.
- A founder spending time on a team's daily work (her "engineerication") lands as ordinary floor presence: a founder in a seat is bound by that seat's rules like anyone, and what a founder observes is a capture at the close, never a judgment about a lead.
- Skip levels, the mutual 1:1 document with action items, introducing a report to leaders, and loaning standing are all judged incompatible with a flat, two-lead structure and are candidates to leave out entirely.

Risks, failure modes, sequencing, dependencies:
- Risk: a lead's check-in holder (paired partner) unintentionally becoming a "manager who assigns and reviews" by informal drift, an ongoing discipline question, not a one-time decision.
- Sequencing: who holds a lead's check-in must be settled before leads are in the building; every downstream mechanism (review, career conversation, window, flag read, departure) depends on that holder.
- Failure mode named in the old work: a lead's personal standard becoming an unwritten "rule" because no versioned document holds the room's standard beyond a service model. Risk of "node overload wearing a taste", a standard that lives only in one person's head.
- Failure mode: a permanent, informal "room holder" pattern (always assigning the same person to a role) becoming an unrecognized layer.
- A repeated one-sided hearing by a lead (acting on one party's account without both sides) is flagged as a pattern to watch and correct at the mechanism level before treating it as a performance issue.
- Two founders reading two leads' domains without either lead having two readers is achievable only if reading happens through records (a review record, a domain page) and not through direct oversight of people.

One item of adjacent substance: whether a founder holding a lead's check-in changes anything about the founders' employment classification (see counsel question 24 below) informs but doesn't gate this chunk.

Options the old team argued (no verdict):

- Option: the word "manager" is retired from the business's vocabulary entirely (for any seat, on any document or customer-facing surface), and "lead" is used wherever the business names an act of leading. Reasoning: a manager, by definition, assigns and reviews from above; if no seat is meant to sit above another, keeping the word invites the structure it names. "Management" would survive only as a description of a function (information flow, coordination) never as a job title.

- Option: a lead's monthly check-in with a partner is held by exactly one partner per lead, never by both partners together, never by the other lead, never by nobody, and never on a rotating basis. Reasoning against each alternative: two leads holding each other's check-ins turns each into the other's coach/evaluator, which contradicts a flat structure; having no one hold it leaves a lead's concerns without any destination; both partners holding it together makes it a review "from above" by headcount; rotating it breaks the continuity a monthly relationship needs. Reasoning for one dedicated partner: it mirrors how the two founders already review each other's domains, in writing, one relationship at a time, one level down.

- Option: a lead's development (their growth, coaching, and correction) runs entirely through mechanisms the business already has for everyone, training modules, mentoring from a peer lead, a personal "working with me" document, structured check-ins and reviews, with no separate leadership program, 360-degree assessment, or founder-led mentoring of a lead's character. Reasoning: adding a bespoke leadership-development track for leads reintroduces a hierarchy in substance even if not in title; using the same tools as everyone else keeps the structure genuinely flat.

- Option: when a lead's work isn't going well, the business reads its own systems first (whether the lead was given a workable range of responsibility, whether needed support existed, whether the check-in cadence was actually followed) before looking at the lead. Reasoning: a lead who is holding a team together "by force of personality" is treated as a sign the system under them is under-built, not as a personal failing, consistent with a general operating stance that unowned failures are traced to missing structure first, personal responsibility second.

- Option: a written record of concern about a lead's performance is never kept by anyone standing above the lead (no file, no case notes); instead a repeated pattern (e.g., repeated one-sided decisions, a personal standard substituting for a written one) is tallied as a count and raised once, directly, at the lead's own check-in. Reasoning: avoids creating a "personnel file" dynamic that implies a manager-subordinate relationship and a paper trail built toward eventual termination.

### Managing out, firing, and layoffs (book pp. 454-466)

Research and how it lands in a restaurant:
- The core reframe: nearly all of her "managing out" content assumes a manager delivering a termination decision they arrived at unilaterally or through discussion with the employee. The old work's alternative model treats a decision to end someone's employment as something only the founders (partners) decide, never a lead, and never mid-conversation with the employee.
- A restaurant's "layoff" equivalent isn't a company-wide restructuring; it's seasonal or slow-period scheduling. A slow month typically reduces someone's hours before a whole role disappears. The scheduling system is described as the first place any reduction shows up, before it ever becomes a question of someone's continued employment.
- Distinction drawn: a reduction in scheduled hours is not automatically an end of employment. It only becomes a departure question if a specific seat/role is being eliminated permanently and the person holding it has no other position to move into.
- Every account of an employee leaving in the existing white paper describes the employee's own choice to leave; none describes the business ending someone's employment. This gap is treated as something the old work needed to design from scratch, since no precedent exists in the founding materials.
- A person's own account, in their own words, is treated as something that belongs to them, not something the business writes on their behalf or repeats to others.
- The exit interview is conceived as build-good-will and continuous-improvement oriented (asking what could have been better about the workplace), never used as input to any decision about the person, and never something the person is required to do.
- A "grow beyond the building" idea (staying in touch with former employees to see how their careers progressed) is proposed as an opt-in, once-a-year check-in, not an ongoing relationship, and only with the person's explicit ongoing consent (which they could revoke any time).

Risks, failure modes, sequencing, dependencies:
- Risk (named repeatedly, treated as the central hazard of this whole area): a schedule change used quietly to reduce someone's hours to nothing without a formal, honest, documented conversation about why, a "soft" way of pushing someone out that avoids the harder, more honest process. Guards proposed: any rule for who loses shifts when hours are cut must be written down in advance and applied by a fixed formula (not picked person-by-person); a role can't be eliminated and then quietly recreated with a different person soon after; if a role is eliminated while someone is actively going through a performance-related process, the performance process has to finish first, not be short-circuited by suddenly calling it a "reduction."
- Sequencing/dependency: a decision to end someone's employment should only reach the level of "founders decide" after (a) a documented period of the person's performance not meeting expectations despite support, or (b) a serious one-time violation of a clear standard of conduct. It should never be based on a single person's opinion, a customer complaint alone, a performance score, or anything not already on a written record the person has seen.
- Dependency: this whole area depends on getting basic employment law questions answered first (see counsel questions below), for example, what "at will" employment means in Texas, what must legally be paid and by when, whether the tip/service-charge pool counts as wages for final-pay purposes, and what can legally be told to a departing person or to the person's future prospective employer.
- Sequencing: a temporary paid suspension (someone scheduled off with pay while a serious allegation is being sorted out) is proposed to run only until the next case-by-case decision point, not indefinitely, and specifically only when the concern is about how someone treated another person (not, e.g., a policy violation with no other person harmed).
- Risk: treating any legitimate personal hardship (illness, family emergency, etc.) as something a manager should "observe," "track," or discuss with others is explicitly identified as the wrong instinct from her material; the safer path is a purely administrative "not currently scheduled" status with no reason recorded, changed simply because the person asked.

Options the old team argued (no verdict):

- Option: employment can end in exactly three ways, the person's own choice to leave, a decision made jointly by the two founders (with legal counsel present) based only on a closed, pre-defined set of records, or a schedule/role reduction that only becomes an actual departure if no other position is available. Reasoning: narrowing the possible "causes" of a departure to a short, closed list (and explicitly ruling out anything else, a supervisor's opinion, a single complaint, a performance score, a customer's comment) is meant to prevent an ad hoc or informal firing dressed up after the fact.

- Option: no one below the two founders can ever decide, announce, or even imply that someone's employment is ending. Reasoning: keeping firing authority exclusively with the founders (rather than delegating it to a team lead) is treated as the load-bearing choice that prevents the whole area from recreating a traditional manager-fires-employee dynamic; a lead who announced such a decision "would have become the manager who fires."

- Option: the founders' decision to end someone's employment is treated as a "reserved" decision requiring both founders (not just one) plus legal counsel present, defaulting to "the person stays" unless both founders affirmatively agree otherwise. Reasoning: requiring unanimity and defaulting to the status quo is meant to make ending someone's employment procedurally hard to do lightly or unilaterally, consistent with a broader pattern in the founding materials of important calls requiring both founders' agreement.

- Option: before the founders even look at the specifics of a person's situation, they first review whether anything on the business's own side (a role's defined scope, a resource that was promised but not delivered, an interval between check-ins that had drifted) might explain the shortfall, and if something is found lacking, the decision-making process stops there and the item goes back to be fixed rather than proceeding to a decision about the person. Reasoning: mirrors a general principle elsewhere in the plan that failures are attributed first to unclear or missing systems, and only afterward, if nothing systemic explains it, to the individual.

- Option: severance pay, if the business offers it at all, is either a fixed rule applied identically to every departure of a given kind, or not offered at all, never something negotiated case by case. Reasoning: a pay system with "no raise to withhold, no bonus to deny, no rating to lower" (i.e., no ordinary managerial pay levers) is read as structurally unable to support negotiated severance either, and negotiating severance case-by-case was seen as reopening exactly the kind of individually-negotiated, opaque outcome the compensation design elsewhere was built to avoid.

- Option: if a legally serious allegation surfaces (an accusation that someone toxic or exploitative toward another team member or a customer), the accused person is placed on a paid suspension (their regular base pay, with those scheduled days still counting toward eligibility for the shared tip pool) until the founders make a decision, rather than being asked to keep working or being sent home unpaid. Reasoning: paying the person during the review period was viewed as the practical way to honor a "no disposable people, no premature punishment" value while the specific complaint is being sorted out; the alternative (having them keep working during the review) was considered and set aside as worse when the complaint involves potential harm to another person in the building.

- Option: any written explanation given to someone about why their employment is ending states only the citation to the closed record that was reviewed (a date, a case reference) and nothing more, no elaboration, no comparison to other people, no scripted apology, no negotiation. Reasoning: the old work treated a spoken justification "in the moment" as functionally equivalent to keeping an informal, undocumented case file on the person, which runs against the written-record-only approach; if the reasoning isn't already on a record the person has seen, it shouldn't be said aloud for the first time during the conversation ending their employment.

- Option: seasonal or slow-period reductions in scheduled hours are described in writing in advance (as an ordinary, expected feature of the business's operating rhythm, stated on hiring documents and in a public-facing internal reference) rather than announced as a surprise event when they happen. Reasoning: framing seasonality as a known, disclosed fact from the start (rather than a crisis response) was viewed as more honest and as removing the temptation to use a "reduction" as a disguised way of pushing out a specific person.

- Option: exit interviews are always optional, always about the workplace and never about the departing person, and their content is never usable in any future decision (about that person or anyone else specifically), only aggregated anonymously over time. Reasoning: keeping the exit interview strictly forward-looking (what can the business learn) and never backward-looking (what does this say about the person) was seen as necessary to get honest answers and to avoid it becoming, in practice, a tool used against people.
