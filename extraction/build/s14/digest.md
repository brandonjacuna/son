# S14 digest: high performers, the steady middle, low performers (Book pp. 419 to 449; Workbook pp. 114 to 123)

## Part 1: what the old page holds that's worth keeping

### Topic: high performers (Book pp. 419 to 430)

Research and facts:
- Every proposal in this material assumes a manager who assigns work, rates people, and retains the best through pay, promotion, and visibility. The white paper removes the manager from that chain: "No one's advancement waits on a manager's attention or judgment. The system is the advocate" (WP p.11).
- WP p.7: the people carrying the most load in a high-performing room weren't the ones at the top of the org chart; they carried the most information.
- WP p.8 to p.9: "every position matters because the sequence is the mechanism"; no single element bears the load; distributed anchors beat centralized ones; "no top, no bottom"; growth is outward, not upward.
- WP p.11: advancement is self-paced and unlocks automatically; a system that reads documented competencies "favors the people who want it most"; cross-strand fluency (holding two kinds of work at once) is called "the real development marker"; the goal is that "the building rarely hires a manager from outside at all."
- WP p.16: "the best people have options... Sŏn has to be worth choosing," in a labor market described as tight.
- WP p.17: "nobody asks whether it works for a 22-year-old server six months into the industry"; burnout treated as a system failure, not a character flaw, is named as the industry's own error; universal problems are design problems.
- WP p.18: a mentor is picked as "a proven cultural steward rather than simply the strongest performer", the record never defines what a "strongest performer" is or how one would be measured.
- WP p.19: reviews focus on development and "the path forward rather than a verdict," governed by "no surprises"; the best ideas and earliest warnings "come from the people doing the work"; teaching and training-module authorship are paid; a server who also learns the kitchen "becomes more valuable everywhere."
- WP p.22: customer recognition rate is a leading indicator; cash, payroll, HR, compliance, and vendor coordination are described as moved into software, with no seat named as the human owner of any of it.
- WP p.23: retention and internal promotion rate are named as "direct evidence the development commitments are real rather than stated"; exit interviews should be "read for what could have been better rather than filed and forgotten"; "how people grow beyond the building" is called the longest measure of whether the environment did its job.
- WP p.25: if a server moves to another property in the same group, their regulars may follow them.

Risks, failure modes, and sequencing:
- A tier, label, list, or ranking that lives on a person functions as a rating; prior work already closed off pay, formal reviews, the person's file, and any group setting as places such a rating could live, so "three tiers" cannot survive as literal tiers on people, it has to become something read off existing records instead.
- A mentor relationship must not slide into becoming a sponsor; nothing gives a manager a sponsor's leverage over someone's advancement.
- A manager handing someone "stretch" work specifically to develop them is flagged as reintroducing exactly the kind of manager judgment the self-paced advancement design is meant to remove.
- A goal met at the cost of overworking the person or their teammates is read as a system/workload failure, not a result to reward, meaning no tier or bonus mechanism should ever exist that could reward it.
- Retention levers (counter-offers, bonuses) can't be added without colliding with a pay system that is deliberately limited to four fixed inputs.
- A special "top talent" list for pre-exit interviews, or interviewing a departing person's "close friends or colleagues," creates an uneven information channel and relies on a source (a peer's account of a peer) treated elsewhere as unreliable.

Legal and counsel questions (verbatim substance):
- At-will employment status, documentation requirements, termination process, protected classes, leave, accommodation, and discipline law are all named explicitly as counsel's domain; nothing here asserts a legal fact about any of them.
- No seat has been given clear, lawful ownership of employment recordkeeping; the back-office/HR function was described as moved into software, but the actual human or legal owner of records tied to a person's employment was never named.

Options (no verdict):
- Option: treat "three tiers" as fully refused and replace it with three situations read from existing records, a person moving past a skill level, a person holding steady at their current level, and a seat whose current level is genuinely in question, with no field, list, or count anywhere that sorts people into these as a status. Reasoning: any surface that could hold such a sort already functions as the tier the design is trying to avoid.
- Option: reweight the material so the steady middle gets the most space and attention, inverting the book's own emphasis (roughly a third on top performers, half on low performers, one page on the middle). Reasoning: a restaurant's product is consistency across ordinary shifts, not a small number of standout individuals; the operating model has no concept of one "load-bearing" role; a full night is carried by people simply holding their stations; and the tight-labor-market argument in the white paper is written for the ordinary worker, not the top of the market.
- Option: drop pre-exit interviews of a "top" group and colleague retrospectives on departure entirely, relying instead on the same upward-feedback channel for everyone. Reasoning: singling out a group for extra listening creates unequal treatment, and a peer's secondhand account isn't treated as a reliable source anywhere else in the design.
- Option: no counter-offers, no retention bonuses, no "top talent" list. Reasoning: pay has a fixed, narrow set of inputs, so there's no figure a manager could offer or promise even if one wanted to.
- Option: when someone wants more than what's structurally available, tell them plainly what growth paths do exist (other seats, other departments, an open leadership role filled from within) and treat their eventual departure to grow elsewhere as a normal, even positive, outcome rather than a retention failure.
- Option: when someone isn't ready for a promotion, whoever evaluates readiness says so plainly, against an objective read of the role's stated expectations, without the manager's personal opinion or softening added on top.

### Topic: the steady middle (Book p.431)

Research and facts:
- Book, cited only to test against: "given enough time you'll see the entire normal curve"; treat the team as "a portfolio of talent... assessed and either supported, coached, or moved"; some teams need people who deliver "similar work, consistently well," and it should be fine to reach a "terminal" level; medium performers should get development plans and have their feedback sought; celebrate them with recognition and special assignments rather than compensation.
- WP p.4: the record's own argument for its kitchen technique is that "the tenth cup is not the first", the value of the method is raising the consistency floor and holding it.
- WP p.8, p.9, p.11, p.20: "no hierarchy of importance"; distributed anchors, not a concentrated one; advancement doesn't reward tenure, "a person who has been in the system longer does not move toward the center."
- WP p.16 to p.17: tight labor market; design built for the ordinary worker, not only the top of the market.
- WP p.19: peer recognition "lands differently... because it comes from the people who understand the work"; the best ideas and earliest warnings come from the people doing the work.
- WP p.22 to p.23: employee NPS, a cultural-labor measure, customer recognition rate, and retention/internal-promotion rate are all read as leading indicators at the team or house level, never at the level of a single person.
- WP p.25: customer recognition at a later visit is produced by the same server having been there for the earlier ones.
- Research tension worth naming: the pay/advancement architecture gives tenure in a seat zero weight ("a person who has been in the system longer does not move toward the center," WP p.11), yet the customer-recognition and regulars argument (WP p.25) depends entirely on the same person holding the same seat over time. The design and the stated value of steadiness pull in different directions and this isn't reconciled anywhere.

Risks and sequencing:
- The rest of the progression material (skill unlocks, an internal promotion pool, succession planning) only ever describes people who are advancing. There's no language anywhere for the person who holds a seat and doesn't want to move up, even though that's structurally who carries most of the nightly load.
- If "internal promotion rate" gets treated as a target to push up, the risk is a manager pressuring unlocks onto people who never asked for them, undermining the idea that staying in a seat by choice is a legitimate, valued outcome.

Options (no verdict):
- Option: define "the steady middle" as "a person holding a seat's current skill level," which describes nearly everyone on nearly every shift, rather than as a performance category. Reasoning: it maps onto data the system already records (skill level held, since when) without inventing a new judgment field.
- Option: what the operation owes this group, full pay weight for the seat they hold (no partial credit for not advancing further), workload protection ahead of anyone chasing something, a predictable schedule, the same check-in cadence as anyone else, access to mentoring opportunities, and rotation-based access to special one-shift designations rather than favoritism. Reasoning: converts the book's vague "verbal recognition" into things that already exist rather than a new manager discretion.
- Option: recognition for this group runs through peer recognition, the person's own record read back to them at reviews, the pay arithmetic itself, and customer recognition metrics, never a manager's individually chosen "special assignment." Reasoning: keeps recognition tied to something observable rather than a manager's personal favor.
- Option: don't create a formal "terminal level" designation. Reasoning: the next skill level stays available on the person's own timeline indefinitely; someone who never takes it still gets full credit for the seat they hold.

### Topic: low performers / work that is not holding (Book pp. 432 to 449; Workbook pp. 114 to 123)

Research and facts:
- Book, tested rather than adopted: "a low performer isn't always a low performer, it's all about the context" (p.432); notice when isolated feedback "has become a pattern"; involve HR and stay aware of legal protections; give "no surprises"; document every conversation to the person and to HR; be direct as an expression of compassion; move deliberately within a stated number of months; form a hypothesis about the outcome before starting; align on the pattern within one to three conversations; agree next steps whether or not the person agrees; write a plan naming skills to change, an end date, and a measure; end in improvement, a move, or the person leaving.
- WP p.14: "before intervening at the people level, identify which failure mode is actually present"; "no single element bears the load... other nodes are compensating without visibility" describes what an underperforming seat actually looks like structurally.
- WP p.17: "management absorbs blame from staff"; "universal problems are not people problems, they are design problems"; burnout is a system failure, not a character flaw; post-mortems are private and process-focused.
- WP p.26, noted as a tension rather than adopted: "the customer should never feel at fault, even when they are", the record's only explicit "someone is at fault" language is about customers, leaving an open question about where individual staff accountability begins.
- WP p.36: "never assert the unproven", cited as a reason not to promise a staff member a future role or path that doesn't concretely exist yet.
- The required order of operations before any conversation ever touches the individual: capture the incident in the observer's own words, naming a process and not a person; classify the failure type (a straightforward, fixable process gap; a complex failure with no individual driver; or an "intelligent" failure that is never corrected against a person); look at the broader pattern across the seat, shift, or department; make and verify department-level fixes; check whether the role/seat definition itself is realistic; check what the operation actually supplied against what the person said they needed; only then hear the person's own account; and only after all of that, if a problem still recurs after a fix, form even a tentative view of an individual behavior pattern.
- The book's own performance improvement plan template requires: a signed standalone document, stated role expectations, dated examples, goals/deliverables, milestones, weekly progress checks, an evaluation end date, a clause letting the manager end the plan early, an at-will clause, a warning that failure "may result in termination," a "must maintain this level" clause, and two signatures. Three companion letters exist: a progress update, a "getting back on track" letter, and an "insufficient progress" letter.
- Book's "consistently late" example calls for a manager to give "feedback quickly and strongly"; flagged as a case where hearing the person's own account should come first, before any strong or quick manager response.

Risks, failure modes, and sequencing:
- Two specific scenarios explicitly pause any person-level process because the failure is the organization's, not the individual's: (1) someone was hired despite a documented hiring-stage concern, on the promise of extra support, and that support was never actually delivered; (2) a performance plan was extended because of a promised operational fix, and that fix was never actually made.
- A pattern worth flagging on its own: if the same seat or role repeatedly triggers this process across different people, that's read as a signal the role or seat design itself may be wrong, checked before assuming it's about the person again.
- Zero-tolerance categories (safety-critical events, a specific named protocol breach, a known regular customer going unrecognized, candidates being "ghosted," and feedback items left open past a deadline) are explicitly not treated as automatic proof of individual fault; each is read first as a process failure and only escalates to a person-level conversation on the same recurrence path as any other fixable problem, no faster.
- Genuine conduct violations (serious misconduct, harassment-type behavior, exploitation) are explicitly kept on a separate, higher-severity track from ordinary performance handling; treating one as the other in either direction is flagged as a mistake to avoid.

Legal and counsel questions (verbatim substance):
- Whether a single serious conduct incident is by itself enough to end someone's employment, or whether it always requires something more, is left open, pending both a values decision about what counts as this kind of serious violation and legal review.
- How long a documented serious-incident record should stay "active" and limit someone's eligibility for future roles is left open, with one option floated (kept on file permanently for transparency, but stops actively limiting eligibility after a set number of review cycles) against the alternative of a record that limits eligibility permanently, presented as options, no verdict.
- Everything about lawful discipline documentation, what a termination process must legally include, protected-class, leave, and accommodation law, and what an incident record may legally contain, is named as requiring counsel's review before anything here is built.
- No seat has formal ownership of the "HR"/employment-recordkeeping function the book assumes exists; the load is currently split, informally, across whoever holds someone's regular check-ins, a general operations role, and outside counsel, flagged as a gap needing an explicit decision.

Options (no verdict):
- Option: reject the book's performance improvement plan document, its three letters, and its scripted "sample conversation" entirely, as a one-sided, manager-authored case file about a person. Reasoning: a document written only in the manager's voice, with a preset end date, a clause letting the manager end it early, and a built-in termination warning, functions as a verdict reached before the process even starts, and conflicts with a broader principle that no document about a person should be one-sided and that "no surprises" should be structural, not just a stated value.
- Option: replace the book's multi-month timeline and its "operational / skills-fit / behavioral" categories with a single bounded review window measured in a countable number of shifts (not calendar months), closed by a hands-on, in-context skills check performed by someone from a separate qualified pool rather than by the direct manager. Reasoning: a countable, observable close is harder to dispute than a manager's subjective month-based judgment, and keeps the manager from being both coach and judge.
- Option: replace "peer feedback from a set number of people" or another manager's assessment as the way progress is measured, with a single qualified assessor's hands-on evaluation instead. Reasoning: a colleague's secondhand account of someone's conduct is treated as unreliable everywhere else, so it shouldn't be introduced here.
- Option: when an issue is genuinely not resolved, the only available next steps are moving to a different seat, department, or schedule that's actually open, or a decision only senior leadership (never a single manager) can make about continued employment, never a formal "demotion" (there's no lower rung to demote into), and never a manager-offered "less demanding role" for personal hardship (treated instead as an ordinary scheduling request the employee can raise themselves, not a judgment call about their life).
- Option: don't create any new HR-titled, floor-manager, or assistant-manager role to hold this workload. Reasoning: the design principle of removing back-office administrative roles into shared functions/software means this workload should stay with whoever already holds the person's regular check-ins, splitting off only what's legally necessary (an incident record, a second reviewer, outside counsel).
- Option: when a new hire simply doesn't work out, read it only as a signal to review the hiring process itself (was the role description accurate, was the hands-on hiring assessment calibrated correctly, was promised onboarding support actually delivered), never as a mark on the individual. Reasoning: mirrors the process-before-person principle and closes a previously flagged gap where a bad hire had no formal feedback loop back into hiring practices.

### Stripped

- Canon 1.0 and Deck 3.0 citations underpinning the "Playground" agency test, the tier ranking of the company's non-negotiable values against that test, the Jeong/Nunchi development philosophy, the "Mahk" consistency commitment, and the "Jaeyeonmi" maintenance-philosophy language, all used to argue how a conduct violation should be weighed and whether it outranks other values.
- The "David protocol" breach category, a Canon-sourced named service protocol cited as one of the zero-tolerance categories.
- The extended argument over whether the deck's tier placement (ranking company non-negotiables above the "Playground" agency test) should govern how a serious conduct violation is handled, since Canon 1.0 itself does not rank them the same way.
