# S8 digest: Team structures

## Part 1: What the old page holds that's worth keeping

### Her chapter, translated (book pp. 261 to 281)

- Team, project, working group definitions (book pp. 265 to 268, Table 6): a team has a shared long-term mission lasting at least a year and lasting structures; a project is a shorter-term subgroup inside a team; a working group is short-term, cross-functional, has a directly responsible individual, and spins down.
- Her introduction's real distinction (book p. 262): most "teams" are just individuals parallel-processing; the milestone is working as an integrated entity.
- Option: Sŏn has exactly one team in her sense, the whole house, since it has a shared mission, persistent jobs, and lasting structures (this operating system), and the record's description of a great night, "distributed intelligence... every element in relationship with every other" (WP p.8), matches her integrated-entity test. Reasoning: nothing smaller than the house meets her definition; a domain is a set of seats with one owner, not a team with its own norms/culture; a strand is an intelligence and output, not a team; a service period has no people, no lead, and one shared goal set of its own (S3 P7.1), so it fails her test on three counts.
- Her word "team" would then translate three ways: the house (the collective), the domain (a manager's reports, "domain lead" in the record's sense), and the service roster (who's on tonight, dissolves at close). The record's "floor team"/"culinary team" read as domains.
- Whether service periods are one team or several: book's carryover thesis is that dayparts compound rather than reset. Record support: "one restaurant and one brand... met at different times of day" (WP p.33); a bolted-on period "becomes a worse version of dinner... because the restaurant underneath was built to do one thing at one hour" (WP p.2).
- Option: a period counts as "one restaurant" (not a separate one) exactly when its charter's dependent-interfaces field is non-empty and its provided interface is consumed by another period or the connective layer; a charter that fails this test isn't opened. Reasoning: this operationalizes "periods compound, don't reset" as a testable rule rather than an assertion, and mirrors the record's own diagnosis of a peer operator's failed dayparts (WP pp. 33 to 34).
- Consequence for scheduling/cross-training/identity: one schedule from one labor pool; cross-training becomes a fluency marker (not a team transfer); one uniform arc and one register that "loosens" rather than changes.
- Solid/dotted lines (book pp. 265, 275): her dual reporting has a primary and secondary manager, both in HR systems, both doing goal-setting/reviews, separate 1:1s. Option: refuse solid/dotted lines outright; every seat has exactly one domain lead who holds the check-in, opens the seat, decides the hire; where a second lead's judgment must reach the work (e.g., the Head of Beverage's program output read by the Operations Lead), that second lead reads a record, never holds a check-in or a decision about the person. Reasoning: any second line with review/assignment power reintroduces a person standing above another, which the structure's foundational departure (recorded upstream) refuses.
- Functional vs business-line, vertical/horizontal (book pp. 270 to 272): her business unit "has a unique product... and can operate independently." Option: domains (floor, beverage, kitchen, operating architecture) are the functional axis; service periods are the record's business-line axis ("different first customers at different times," WP p.35); but the structure is neither functional nor business-line because the intersection has no manager. The business unit concept is refused outright: a period that "can operate independently" is exactly the separate-restaurant failure mode the interface test exists to catch.
- Hybrid/hinge/business leads (book pp. 272 to 278): her hinge is "the pivot point where you turn the focus from the product toward the customer," usually product marketing; business leads run forums, set dotted-line goals, calibrate performance, are evaluated on results, have no ladder. Research point: the record already has a hinge concept, but in a room, not a team: "the bar is the hinge of the dinner-to-late-night transition." Option: refuse the hinge team and business leads structurally; the pivot toward the customer is the outward-facing strand in every domain, not a team between domains. Business leads' surviving functions map to the periods' three-lead holders (goals set jointly by the Maitre d, Operations Lead, and Chef Partner, per S3 P7.1); forums are the brief and the close; headcount/dollar accounting for business leads touches financial figures, which are out of scope for this work, and is named as a gap for Dominic's domain.
- Keeping structure flexible / yearly reexamination (book p. 265): her rule is to preserve optionality and reexamine "at least once a year," neither too often nor too rarely. Option: the structure itself is fixed (changes only by a versioned entry) and gets its flexibility from per-service designations (who holds the room/door/sequence changes nightly) rather than from restructuring; reexamination happens at every gate (period opening) and periodic mechanism reset, which is more frequent than yearly while periods are opening and yearly after.
- Spans of control (book pp. 280 to 281): her concern is neither too few nor too many direct reports, "adequately flat, but with appropriate bandwidth." Research point: the record has no reports to count, since nothing reports to anything; the team read spans instead as check-in load per lead, timed (fifteen minutes per check-in at a set interval), and flagged the Maitre d's rotation as the one load that can exceed its budget. Risk/gap: the count of check-ins per lead is unknown until period charters set seat counts; read this at the first mechanism reset.
- Layering (book p. 280): a new layer of authority introduced under a leader as reports grow. Option: refuse layering outright (growth is "reach," not "altitude"); bandwidth relief comes from the stack absorbing integration work, from per-service designations, and from lengthening the check-in interval after training; developing people for more responsibility comes from designations held more often and the succession pool's ordering by connections, not a new layer. A new leadership line is added only when a formal disturbance-class test at a gate shows a class resolving to nothing or defaulting to a founder; it sits beside existing lines, never above them.
- Code Yellow (book pp. 268 to 270): her special operating mode for existential risk has shielding from other obligations, daily standups, a dedicated space, power to convene leadership, longer hours/weekends, an all-hands update, a ten-week ceiling. Option: refuse the special-powers apparatus; keep the discipline only, a named risk from an existential-risk register gets a founder owner, a written closing condition, and a daily state, tracked on a page and read first at the weekly leadership review until closed. Reasoning against the powers: no decision-rights entry changes because a risk is live; pulling a lead onto a risk full-time is itself the failure mode the structure works to avoid (a lead absorbing load that belongs elsewhere); longer hours/weekends conflict with the record's labor commitments; there's no regular all-hands mechanism to update, and a live risk doesn't cross into service hours.
- Teams/working groups/projects vs Sŏn's temporary structures (book pp. 266 to 268): her rule that survives whole is "the worst thing you can do is let a temporary structure persist for too long." Option: her project becomes a process change with a single named owner and a date, closed when versioned; her working group becomes the charter-drafting group for a new service period (the leads plus partners, dissolved when the charter is ruled). What's refused: any standing "team" smaller than the whole house, a working group that outlives its purpose, and a project that quietly becomes a permanent seat.
- Work style demand by period (research point, paraphrased by time of day): early morning demands repetition/precision/low interaction density/high consequence for small errors; a fast lunch demands throughput under a clock; dinner is the full range; late night is energy and presence. Risk flagged: under service pressure, staffing naturally selects for only two of these profiles, leaving no one measuring the others. Option: never build a work-style-by-period grid as a selection, placement, or scheduling tool; instead, the demand lives in each period's charter (what the period asks of the seat) and each person self-selects their periods, with the schedule drawn from availability and passed training, never a personality match. The grid may still be useful informally, inside a lead's own working-with-me document.

### The record's structural model (white paper and prior sessions, not the book)

- Center-and-edge model, no altitude: "a web with a center and an outside edge, neither superior, growth outward not upward" (WP p.9); "new people always enter at the outside edge" (WP p.9); roughly eight leadership lines run the day (WP p.22).
- Two parallel co-leads: the Operations Lead (accountable to labor efficiency, cost targets, compliance, vendor performance, facility integrity) and the Maitre d (owns "the how" of the experience, accountable to experience quality and team health); "neither reports to the other" (WP p.10).
- Below the leads, functional designations "mark accountability in the moment... not permanent elevations above anyone" (WP p.10).
- Strands, not levels: outward-facing (relational, felt by the customer) and inward-facing (architectural/timing, customer never feels it directly but feels the result), different intelligences (WP pp. 10 to 11).
- Kitchen shape in outline: Station Leads as domain owners, Chef on the line and Chef on prep as execution ("neither a promotion from the other"), Steward holding kitchen-sequence continuity (WP p.11).
- Head of Beverage owns the beverage program and its execution across the same two strands (WP p.11).
- Progression is connections, not altitude (WP p.11); an "invisible advocate" function schedules advancement rather than a manager's judgment (WP p.11).
- Leadership delta claim: roughly eight lines across the day versus fourteen positions around one dinner service in a comparable peer, and a kitchen of two title tiers instead of four (WP p.22).
- Open items the white paper itself names: role naming, the compensation architecture for cross-strand fluency, and the web rendered as a designed visual artifact (WP p.15).

### A founder ruling on the Maitre d, and its consequence

- Brandon ruled (2026-09-13) that the floor/service manager and the Maitre d are one position: the Maitre d is "freed from the host stand entirely to hold the floor and the team" (WP p.10), combining what most operations split between a service director and a maître d, "two positions with so much inherent crossover that splitting them is the structural error." This closed the one-role-or-two question and the naming difference between the record's halves for the operating system (not for any customer/candidate-facing wording, which stays with the record's separate naming owner).
- Consequence: the Maitre d becomes domain lead for the whole floor (both strands, runners and hosts included), not just the outward-facing side. Argued options for this sub-question were: the Operations Lead (matches the inward strand's "architectural/timing" language), the Maitre d (matches the domain-lead definition as a people function: check-ins, opening seats, deciding hires), or a separate strand-lead seat. Team's reading landed on the Maitre d, reasoning: the floor is one domain with two strands, not two domains; the Operations Lead's own outcomes (labor efficiency, compliance, facility) contain no people-development function; the ruling already made the Maitre d "master of the house"; and a separate strand lead would just reintroduce a floor-manager-equivalent seat the leadership count has no room for (WP p.22).
- Load test on the seat (risk/failure-mode framing, from the record's own "node overload" reference: "one domain owner absorbing load that belongs to adjacent domains... the fix is restoring the separation," WP p.14): three findings, each resolved by adding a per-service designation rather than a second seat:
  1. The room is unheld during the Maitre d's absent hours unless a "room" designation names who holds it for that service (coverage, escalation destination, transition trigger, emergency authority only, nothing else).
  2. Walking every table between the threshold and the seat contradicts the record's picture of the maître d at the threshold; a "door" designation (built on a host seat) carries that function per service, with the Maitre d there personally only when free. This reading of the threshold as a register rather than a fixed post is a divergence from the record's literal wording and needs the record's owner to reconcile.
  3. A kitchen failure spanning multiple sections would otherwise reach the room owner with nothing between the kitchen pass and the floor; a "sequence" designation on the inward-facing strand (a runner seat, named per service) owns the floor's side of the pass and is the first responder.
  4. (Separately, a stack/system finding, not a seat finding): a stack outage mid-service has no fallback in the record and would land on the Maitre d's memory by default; the fix proposed is a written degraded-mode service procedure, run once in training with the stack off.
- Decision-rights entries the ruling touches, restated in owner/range/triggers form (values still open where noted): the server's escalation destination (server handles inside known limits, Maitre d handles above; financial-recovery threshold value still needs Dominic's input, not this program's to set); the pre-service/dinner-to-late-night transition trigger (owned by whoever holds the room, person-triggered not clock-triggered, never announced); emergency authority during a service (Maitre d for the room, Chef Partner for the kitchen, each may close their own side without asking; closing for a whole service period needs the Operations Lead; closing longer than one period needs a founder); and the live cross-domain call when a failure spans both the floor and kitchen mid-service (each side's owner calls their own side; when one call must cover both, the room's owner decides because "dinner is what everything protects," WP p.33, and the kitchen owner is told immediately; the Operations Lead isn't called mid-service).

### Levels, ladders, entry point, salaried seats

- The book assumes job levels/ladders; the open question was whether a single house needs them at all. Research point cited from the record itself: "progression develops connections, not altitude... further along is visible in the number of connections a node has developed... not in where they sit vertically" (WP p.11). Option: no levels, no ladders. The five functions a ladder normally serves are instead carried by: a visible two-horizon path on each seat's own sheet; connections recorded on a person's own page; a pay progression tied to unlocks (no ladder rung, priced separately); a per-service designation as the only form of "elevation," lasting one shift; and an explicit "advancement is outward, not upward" answer built into the onboarding materials.
- What a lead "enters at," given "everyone enters at the outside edge" with no stated exception for leads who sit "at the center": option reached is that the record actually describes two different webs, the house's web, where a seat's position (edge vs. center) is a property of the seat itself, not the person; and each person's own inverted web, where every person (leads included) starts at the center of their own map with zero connections and grows outward. Under this reading, a lead enters at the seat's own entry point (whatever that seat's onboarding horizon is) while personally starting at zero on their own development map, so "further along" for a lead is measured the same way as for anyone: connections, fluency, and complexity held, never a rank. This reading needs founder ratification since the record's sentence about the outside edge is written without an exception.
- Which seats are salaried: the option reached is a structural test, a seat is salaried if it owns a domain or a program across every open period (not scheduled shift by shift). By that test: the Operations Lead, the Maitre d, the chef seat, and the Head of Beverage qualify; Station Leads are chef-gated; no edge seat qualifies, and no per-service designation ever makes a seat salaried on its own. Pricing is out of scope here (financial figures excluded).

### Cross-strand and cross-period fluency

- The record commits to recognizing "cross-strand fluency" (the ability to hold both the outward-facing and inward-facing work) in pay as "meaningful" (WP p.11) but never defines it in a demonstrable way, and separately lists pricing it as an open item. Option reached: define it in three parts, certified on both strands (a passed readiness assessment on a seat in each strand of one domain, scored independently by each strand's own assessor), exercised on both (an actual seat held on each strand within a recent window, so the skill stays current), and "held at once" evidenced (not tested) by real shift-close records of a coordination failure absorbed without visible disruption. Recorded only as dated connections on the person's page, never as a score, rank, or title.
- Cross-period fluency (the parallel marker for working more than one service period, e.g., morning and dinner) is placed the same way: a passed readiness form for a second period plus an actual seat held in each period within a window, recorded as period connections; flagged as the leading indicator for who's nearest when a new period's seats open (a succession signal), and explicitly never a tool for placing someone across periods by a lead's judgment.

### Advancement's two accounts, and who signs off

- The record contains two different pictures of how someone advances, in one place the system schedules a practical assessment automatically with no manager involved, in another the person "schedules a competency conversation" with a lead who "signs off," which contradicts the record's own separation-of-assessor-from-decider rule used elsewhere in hiring. Option reached: split it into three roles: a system schedules the assessment automatically once a training window elapses; a trained assessor from a pool (never the person's own mentor) scores the practical; the domain lead simply records the resulting form at the person's next regular check-in and may not override a "ready" or pass a "not yet" reading either way. This is stated as the resolution "until founders rule which of the record's two wordings should stand."
- For advancement across domains (e.g., a server training toward a kitchen seat), the same logic places the sign-off with the receiving domain's own lead (since that domain has the information about whether the skill is actually held there), scored by that domain's own assessor pool, with the person's original lead simply reading the outcome on the person's page. The kitchen side of this is chef-gated.

### Temporary structures, and the kitchen

- The chef partner's own domain, everything below a shared "spine," is explicitly chef-gated; nothing on this page should be read as pre-deciding it. The shared spine that holds regardless of who the chef is: the kitchen is one domain with one lead; two title tiers only (Station Leads as owners, Chef on the line/Chef on prep as execution, neither a promotion from the other); the Steward is its own seat with its own onboarding materials; every kitchen seat needs a completed sheet before it opens; the kitchen has its own emergency authority in service; a kitchen pass owner is named every service; and the kitchen's goals are part of one shared goal set with the floor, not separate.
- Fourteen items are explicitly left for the chef to decide once hired (collected for that person's onboarding packet): the station list; whether a Station Lead is a standing seat or a per-service designation (and whether it's salaried); the pass owner's seam with the floor's own sequence designation; the line/prep split; the Steward's count and place; the kitchen's own assessor/mentor pools and readiness benchmarks; kitchen shift shapes per period; the kitchen's part of each period's operating rules; how a safety flag (an at-risk-customer alert) is handled in the kitchen; the chef seat's own check-in holder; kitchen metrics; a "complexity budget" per dish and per period; how many supervised run-throughs happen before someone serves alone; and the kitchen's part of the org visualization.

### Structure-change process

- The book's method for a structural change is one-on-one messages framed around growth, a monitoring plan, then broader communication. Option reached: every structure change (creating/retiring a seat, changing a leadership line, adding a period's seats, changing a designation's rule, changing a pool/salaried mark) is a single versioned entry with its reason stated first, in the record's own terms (a specific tension lost, a disturbance class resolving to nothing, a period gate, a ratified charter), proposed through the leads' regular review and finalized by the partners. It reaches people through four channels already in use rather than an announcement: the affected person's own regular check-in, a new version of the seat's own onboarding sheet, the next verbal shift brief, and the shared team reference where the org structure lives. No lead makes a unilateral announcement and there's no all-hands mechanism.
- A separate disagreement-resolution process from the book's workbook (writing up a disagreement and escalating up two reporting chains to a shared manager) is refused as an escalation mechanism, since there's no manager standing above two domain leads, but its useful form (writing the problem, options, and trade-offs on half a page before escalating, refusing to hear one side's case until both have written jointly, and a time window for resolution) survives as the format for the existing cross-domain dispute path.

### Open findings and unresolved conflicts (flagged, not resolved here)

- The record is inconsistent on whether the maître d role is freed from the entrance/threshold or fixed to it; resolving this needs whoever owns the customer-facing/brand wording.
- The record names a sonic/audio-governance responsibility split across "management" and "floor management" tiers that assumed two layers of floor management; under the one-seat ruling this needs re-reading as one tier, routed to the same reconciliation owner.
- The Head of Beverage is described three different ways (a nightly designation, a program owner, a collapsed managerial seat); resolved above as a seat and line, pending founder ratification.
- "Roughly eight leadership lines run the full span of the day" (WP p.22) doesn't square with any single seat covering every open hour by itself; resolved by adding per-service designations that carry a domain's live functions when its lead isn't present.
- The record describes managers "plural" pouring tableside and reviewing incidents (WP pp. 13, 17, 26) even though only one floor seat exists under the ruling; resolved by reading those functions as carried by whoever holds the room-designation that service.
- The record's own failure-mode reference (node overload) doesn't clearly say whether live floor coordination itself counts as the "operational work" that shouldn't fall on a lead, or whether it's the "how" the Maitre d is supposed to own; the team's reading treats live coordination as the lead's proper "how" work and schedule/labor/stack management as the Operations Lead's separate "operational work," so the ruling doesn't blur the intended separation.
- Whether a Station Lead is a standing seat or a per-service designation is unresolved, chef-gated.
- The record's own language describes the structure in terms of "levels" ("one level below," "below the management layer") even while insisting there are no levels; the team's reading treats "below" as "at the edge" and "level" as "kind," not altitude, throughout.
- An events team is dropped from the leadership-line arithmetic (WP p.22) even though hosting events in the room is named elsewhere as a way of building customer relationships (WP pp. 28, 31); no seat, designation, or charter currently owns an event. Team's reading: an event is simply a service under an existing period's charter with its own brief, held by that service's normal room-holder, with no new seat created, pending a founder ruling.
- An overnight cleaning crew is described as contracted labor "at the same cost" to redirect server labor toward relationship-building (WP p.26), but has no seat, no range of what it may touch, and no domain lead, even though kitchen continuity and facility integrity are both explicitly owned elsewhere; flagged as a gap for the Operations Lead and founders to place (seat class, vendor with a written range, or otherwise).
- Every mid-service escalation path in the record eventually terminates at a founder, even though the record separately says the company is "built to run without" its founders and one readiness check tests exactly that; the team's reading is that every founder-facing escalation destination should be understood as reached only after service, never during it.
