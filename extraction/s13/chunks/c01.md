## 1. Her ask against the record, and how much is already decided

### 1.1 What she asks

Her formal review process asks for five things a company must have once it has a few dozen people: a talent strategy, which "could be as simple as 'pay for performance'"; rubrics tied to job levels and ladders; agreement on how to assess results against them; "a program to capture performance feedback and promotion nominations via written and numeric assessments"; a calibration process "to equalize treatment of people at the same level"; and "a compensation philosophy and a means for the performance and promotion processes to feed into any compensation outcomes" (Book pp. 399 to 400). The review itself is a written document the manager drafts after reading three to five peers' feedback and the person's self-assessment, carrying a promotion nomination and a performance designation on a five-point or three-point scale, submitted into calibration, adjusted there, formalized in an HR tool, and delivered in a conversation that spends little time on the past and most on the next half (Book pp. 402 to 407). Calibration is a roll-up of managers by level, facilitated by a division leader, checking outliers, distributions, and bias, and ending in a spreadsheet of changed designations and promotions checked against compensation budgets (Book pp. 407 to 413). Compensation is a philosophy, a fixed and variable split, job levels, market data from an outside source, salary bands and equity targets, and an annual refresh; the compensation conversation is a manager explaining the system, sharing a raise or a bonus or the absence of one, handling comparisons and disappointment, and pointing a disappointed person at the job ladder (Book pp. 413 to 419). Her closing sentence is the one this page answers: employees are "assessed and rewarded fairly, meaning appropriately for their contributions at their level, within the system you've built" (Book p.419, context line).

The workbook adds two instruments: a Performance Review Template (name, current level, proposed designation, proposed promotion, why not higher, why not lower, the one-sentence takeaway, three impact areas with metrics, two strengths and two development areas each with an approximate designation, and a promotion proposal with its timing) and a Compensation Conversations Preparation and Guide (the timeline and resources, preparation at the individual level including the size of the last increase, owning the message, and an outline that reminds the person of their current pay, shares base salary and target bonus for the coming year, and opens for discussion).

Every piece assumes a salaried knowledge worker, a manager who assigns, reviews, and rates from above, an annual or semiannual cycle with a review season, a calibration committee, levels and a ladder, salary bands and an equity refresh, a raise or a bonus as the reward the review produces, and a written narrative about the person in the manager's voice. None of that is Sŏn, and the session task says so. The task's own premise is tested in 1.5.

### 1.2 What the record already holds

"Reviews happen every three to six months and focus on development, growth, and the path forward rather than a verdict. The governing rule is no surprises: continuous communication means a formal review never contains new information. If something is wrong, the person knows before the review. If something is excellent, they know before it too. Feedback runs both directions. Team members weigh in on management effectiveness, operational improvements, and culture, because the best ideas and the earliest warnings both come from the people doing the work" (WP p.19). ### 1.5 The session task's premise, tested, and the task text's figure

The task says almost none of her compensation machinery transfers, and that what transfers is the principle: compensation must be legible, defensible, and connected to a review people trust. Tested piece by piece, the premise is right about the form and wrong about one of its three principles.

What transfers as function once the form is removed, and is built here: a published account of how pay works that every person can explain in their own words (her philosophy, section 13); a stated mechanism from which a person can compute their own pay (her bands and market data have no equivalent, but her "instill trust in your systems" does: the pay page, section 13); a calendar (her review cycle becomes the person's own clock, section 2); a conversation guide (her compensation conversation becomes the offer conversation, the mechanics module, and the destinations of a pay question, section 14); and a rule for what a lead may and may not say about pay (section 14). What the record already decided and this page does not re-derive: built into menu price, never a service charge and never presented as one, no tips, the whole team including the culinary team, a point system weighted by days, calculated daily, paid on a reasonable rhythm, benefits from the start.

The third principle does not transfer. At Sŏn compensation is not "connected to a review process people trust." It is disconnected from the review by design, and the disconnection is what makes the review trustworthy and the pay legible (section 12). Her pay is connected to her review because her review produces a rating and her rating produces a raise; the record's review produces "development, growth, and the path forward rather than a verdict," the check-in refuses pay, and no feedback act touches pay (`17tn048qcjg`). What the task's principle is reaching for, that a person trusts the system that pays them, is carried at Sŏn by a system with no judgment in it: the seat, the horizon, the markers, and the days, each on a record the person can read.

The task text also carries a specific percentage and names a data source that is on the program's exclusion list. The record itself says the built-in compensation is "set at a defined percentage that is still being finalized" (WP p.20) and that "the remaining open piece is the final percentage" (WP p.22). The task text and the record therefore disagree about whether the figure exists, and the team did not use the figure, reason from it, or let it shape a sentence; financials are not a context source for this program. That is a finding about the task text, not a position, and it is stated once.

**P1 (Recommended).** Her review and compensation material lands on a record that settled the pool's principle and form and the review's focus, interval, and governing rule, and on eleven sessions that built everything a review would read and fenced everything it may not. What this page adds is the review as a designed mechanism (when, where, who, on what, writing what, refusing what) and the compensation architecture as a mechanism with no figure in it. Her forms are refused wherever they assume a rating, a level, a manager's narrative, or a raise; her functions survive as the pay page, the mechanics page, the person's own clock, and the rules for what a lead may say.

**P2 (Recommended).** The session task's premise is answered in two parts: her compensation machinery does not transfer as form, and two of its three underlying principles transfer as function (legible, defensible), while the third (connected to a review) is refused by design, because at Sŏn the review's trustworthiness and the pay's legibility both depend on their separation. The task text's figure and source were not used, and the record's own statement that the percentage is unfinalized is the one this page follows.

## 2. The review, placed: interval, calendar, clock, room, holder, language

### 2.1 The interval, inside "three to six months"

The record states a range and no owner (S4 finding, line 41). Session 4 argued the check-in to monthly as "the longest interval at which the review every three to six months can be true to its own rule" (S4 4.5). This page picks the review's interval inside the range from the other side: the longest interval at which the review still does work the check-in does not, and the shortest the Maitre d's windows can carry beside every floor check-in.

Six months. Three arguments. First, the check-in already carries the running conversation monthly, so a review at three months is the fourth check-in wearing a longer form: it would re-read three lines the lead has already read and the path line the career conversation just wrote. At six months the review reads five check-in lines, a quarter's captures twice over, at least one unlock window on most seats, and a fluency window's currency, which is enough record for the dimensions to say something the last check-in did not. Second, the Realist's arithmetic (S4 4.5) governs: the Maitre d holds every floor check-in monthly, and a review is a longer block on the same windows; at six months the review adds one block per person twice a year, at three months it adds four, and Session 12 has already added placed and extended check-ins to what the seat spends (S12 P37). Third, the record's word is "every three to six months," and the six-month end is the one that keeps the review formal: a review that runs as often as the check-in stops being the moment the record wrote it as. The interval is a reset parameter with a floor: the reset may shorten it toward three months and never below, on one read only, whether reviews changed a path line or a supplied line the check-ins had not touched, which would mean the check-ins are not carrying the path. Reviews that contained something new are not a reason to shorten the interval; they are a candor failure on the lead's entry (section 4).
