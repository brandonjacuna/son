21.2	Operating cadence
15.0	We’ve covered the constituent parts of a successful operating
15.0	system. The operating cadence is how you, well, operationalize it. I
15.0	once worked on a consulting project for DirecTV called
15.0	“Operationalizing CRM,” and I remember that, as I wrote those

[p.136]
15.0	words at the top of every presentation, I found the word
15.0	“operationalizing” awkward and uninspiring. Yet here I am, extolling
15.0	the virtues of operationalizing your operating system!
15.0	Because humans are fundamentally creatures of habit, you’ll
15.0	want to create an operating cadence—the schedule or rhythm with
15.0	which your company, divisions, or teams provide progress reports
15.0	and make decisions—that they can follow naturally. People should
15.0	be able to break with the framework if there’s an emergency or
15.0	another good reason to do so, but most of the time you’ll want
15.0	stability and predictability. If different teams set goals at different
15.0	times and measure the results with different definitions of the data,
15.0	chaos will reign over execution and morale will suffer.
15.0	Cadences will vary by manager, team, and individual, but they
15.0	should be agreed upon up front by all of the relevant parties. They
15.0	will very likely mirror the company cadence, which is often driven
15.0	by milestones in the calendar year. There’s no secret to designing
15.0	your operating cadence—it depends on the type of team you’re
15.0	running and, often, on the type of business and company you’re
15.0	operating within. The key is to have a cadence but not get stuck in it.
15.0	Often, these rhythms will require iteration, and they should be
15.0	regularly revisited to keep them relevant and fresh.
15.0	Examples of operating cadences include:
15.0	Annual planning processes
15.0	Quarterly business reviews
15.0	Monthly all-hands meetings
15.0	Biweekly 1:1s
15.0	Weekly snippets and team meetings (with metrics reviews)
15.0	Daily standups

[p.137]
15.0	If you don’t have a set operating cadence, observe what types of
15.0	interactions and reviews are happening organically. For instance,
15.0	maybe a few teams are already using a given process or
15.0	communication mechanism. Figure out why they’re happening and
15.0	what’s useful and not useful about these approaches. Once you’ve
15.0	learned more, try testing the approach with a broader swath of the
15.0	company or with the whole company for a month or a quarter, then
15.0	solicit feedback. Be ready to iterate.
15.0	Within each cadence, teams and individuals will produce various
15.0	kinds of reports, including written updates, automated reports, and
15.0	other measures of progress. A support team, for example, might
15.0	start each week by reviewing customer satisfaction, feedback, and
15.0	response rates, then create a game plan to improve the numbers in
15.0	the coming week. A product leadership team might review the
15.0	launch calendar and share status updates on each upcoming launch.
15.0	The key is to provide predictable, common, and consistent
15.0	structures that serve as touchstones for every person in the division
15.0	or team. Like the smaller unit of a weekly team meeting, your
15.0	collective operating cadence should help people develop a shared
15.0	understanding of the importance of their work, how they’re going to
15.0	accomplish it, what success looks like, and how to measure it. (See
15.0	Figure 8 on the next page for an example of a company-wide
15.0	operating cadence.)

[p.138]

15.0	Be sure to solicit frequent feedback on the operating system and
15.0	cadence. You’ll want to know:
15.0	Does everyone know what the mission and the objectives are for
15.0	the company, the division, and the team, as well as the work
15.0	they’re individually expected to do?
15.0	Is there a clear timeline for planning and execution, and are there
15.0	established measures of progress against targets?
15.0	Are projects with dependencies aligned and agreed on across the
15.0	relevant teams and leaders so ownership and priorities are clear?
15.0	Does everyone know how you’ll measure success and who is
15.0	accountable for the constituent parts of the work? Do they know
15.0	when plans or results should be delivered?
15.0	Are there mechanisms in place to monitor progress, make
15.0	decisions, and unblock teams?

[p.139]
15.0	Are there communication structures in place to provide updates
15.0	on goals and notify relevant teams of any changes?
15.0	Do you recognize and celebrate success? Do you share and learn
15.0	from mistakes? Do your internal communications reinforce the
15.0	company values and operating approach?
15.0	I recommend designing and delegating your operating cadence to
15.0	suit the preferences and competencies of the leadership team. Only
15.0	spin up these processes once you’re certain that leaders will make a
15.0	dedicated effort to implement them. Ideally, there will be one owner
15.0	for each key structure in the operating cadence, and all of the other
15.0	leaders will agree to honor the structure.
15.0	A colleague once observed that I managed to be “switched on”
15.0	for every review meeting, no matter how many times we’d
15.0	conducted it. I think it’s generally true that strong operators are
15.0	dedicated and consistent in how they honor run-the-company
15.0	structures, and that great operations are often a matter of repetition
15.0	and consistent improvement over time. Young companies tend to be
15.0	filled with people who love inventing and building new things, and
15.0	therefore need to balance these tendencies by hiring or elevating
15.0	leaders who can recognize and invest in repeatable and repeated
15.0	processes. You want a mix of leaders and processes that marry
15.0	creativity and invention with operational rigor. Be clear on which
15.0	leader owns which company structures, and take care to match
15.0	those structures with each leader’s strengths and abilities. The
15.0	example they set will imbue the structure with cultural and
15.0	executional importance. Conversely, lack of ownership—or, worse,
15.0	leadership not adhering to company operating approaches—will
15.0	undermine your ability to build a stable architecture that grounds
15.0	the day-to-day, week-to-week, month-to-month, and year-to-year
15.0	accomplishment of your mission and long-term goals.

[p.140]
15.0	A brief note on process
15.0	Process has become something of a pariah in modern business
15.0	environments. It’s known as the thing that slows people down and
15.0	sucks their souls. In fact, I called this chapter “Foundations and
15.0	Planning” because calling it “Your Process” might have sent readers
15.0	screaming for the hills.
15.0	I often hear from founders who worry about their companies
15.0	becoming “bloated” with process, meetings, check-ins, and updates.
15.0	Understandably, many managers are wary of introducing too much
15.0	process, and excessive meetings can become a giant drag on
15.0	productivity. But having a process that guides how you run things
15.0	isn’t inherently bad. Bad processes cause bloat, but good processes
15.0	help provide clarity, which leads to faster execution.
15.0	A few thoughts on process:
15.0	Watch out for defensive processes
15.0	In environments without clear owners and decision-makers,
15.0	something starts to happen that I call defensive—or, more bluntly,
15.0	cover-your-ass—process. These types of processes tend to crop up
15.0	when something has gone wrong and, instead of coming up with a
15.0	clear owner to avoid the mistake in the future, someone creates a
15.0	process.
15.0	I remember a time at Google when the “bit flip”—the approval
15.0	mechanism—to launch a new feature or product swelled from
15.0	something like 3 people to over 20. It started with legal’s insistence
15.0	on a review, and took on more and more bloat as multiple functions
15.0	wanted official notice of an impending launch. Eventually, the
15.0	product managers rightly complained, and the powers that be
15.0	reduced the bit flips back to a handful of people by making
15.0	ownership and accountability clear. For example, the product

[p.141]
15.0	manager was tasked with consulting legal and other impacted
15.0	functions, and then it was their job to make a decision on readiness
15.0	to launch based on the advice they’d received. It was no longer
15.0	legal’s job to defensively block launches that should have been more
15.0	thoroughly vetted.
15.0	Good processes should create lightweight checks that solidify
15.0	alignment and achieve a combination of speed and adherence to
15.0	best practices, so that participants no longer have to divine the best
15.0	way to do something. Defensive processes, on the other hand, exist
15.0	because people are not aligned on who owns a particular decision,
15.0	and they inevitably slow things down.
15.0	Beware of stale processes
15.0	We’ve all seen it happen. People ignore deadlines for requested
15.0	materials. Participants start opening their laptops in meetings and
15.0	don’t submit updates on their work. Folks get disruptive and fall into
15.0	roles: the “What about the bigger picture?” person, or the “Do we
15.0	have an action item owner?” person. Just like meetings, processes
15.0	can get stale and become rote. It doesn’t mean the process was bad,
15.0	but it may mean that the process has outgrown its purpose and it’s
15.0	time to revisit it. Or maybe it was a defensive process, and now that
15.0	you’ve rooted out the source of confusion that made everyone play
15.0	defense, the process can conclude.
15.0	You also don’t want to change processes too quickly. You’re
15.0	trying to achieve the tricky balance of having a process in place for
15.0	long enough to give it a real shot but not so long that bad processes
15.0	will stick around and damage team productivity. Revisit your
15.0	processes every six months or so. More often than that can be
15.0	destabilizing, but less often will cause people to switch to autopilot
15.0	and stop engaging.

[p.142]
15.0	Allow for experimentation
15.0	People tend to be afraid to experiment with processes because they
15.0	worry that it makes them look unprofessional or indecisive. But you
15.0	may not know what works best for your team until you’ve tried it.
15.0	I’m a big fan of experimenting with systems and processes. If you do
15.0	experiment with your processes, make sure to:
15.0	State how long you’re going to try the process for.
15.0	Determine when you’ll check back in to see how things are going.
15.0	Set your evaluation criteria for whether to continue or revisit the
15.0	process.
15.0	Process is not synonymous with meetings
15.0	Meetings are useful tools for doing a lot of the things you need to do
15.0	to run your teams well: sharing information, having discussions,
15.0	making decisions, bonding as a team. But there are many ways to get
15.0	things done without meetings, depending on your team’s
15.0	preferences and what you’re trying to achieve. One way to do this is
15.0	to ask yourself: Could this meeting’s objective have been achieved
15.0	through a written update or by having everyone contribute to a
15.0	project tracker? A good process should subtract from the number of
15.0	meetings, not add more.
15.0	How to tell if something is wrong with your operating system
15.0	or cadence
15.0	Pay attention to the following warning signs—they could indicate
15.0	that you need to revisit your operating system or cadence:
15.0	Goals, timelines, or accountable owners for work are unclear. In
15.0	other words, you don’t know which team is doing what, when.
15.0	You feel like everything is moving too slowly.
15.0	People are flagging problems but aren’t able to suggest solutions.

[p.143]
15.0	You can’t find simple information on the status of the work.
15.0	No one can tell you who the decision-maker is for a project or
15.0	what the priorities are for the company or their team.
15.0	People stop participating or showing up when asked to
15.0	contribute key information or to participate in meetings.
15.0	It’s not clear which updates or meetings matter, and you hate
15.0	updating the tracker or attending your own meetings.
15.0	A sound operating system running on an efficient cadence is
15.0	essential to execution and lays the groundwork for great
15.0	management. It’s the foundation for accomplishing your work as a
15.0	company builder and manager. Once it’s in place, it guides and
15.0	shapes all the other aspects of your company, including:
15.0	Hiring: Bringing people into the organization who will carry the
15.0	highest-priority work forward and set a positive company
15.0	culture.
15.0	Team development: Maximizing impact and efficiency through
15.0	processes that help people communicate, make decisions, and
15.0	collaborate, as well as measuring the impact of the team’s work.
15.0	Feedback and improvement: Developing and coaching
15.0	employees as you collectively identify the skills necessary for
15.0	organizational success, while also helping employees clarify their
15.0	own development goals and career aspirations.
15.0	We’ll take these elements one by one in the following chapters,
15.0	starting with how to get the right people on board as your company
15.0	grows.

30.0	Writing Good OKRs

[p.153]
11.2	This guide was written by Stripes on our finance and tech enablement teams in
11.2	collaboration with our CTO, David Singleton.
16.9	The basics
11.2	Write: Note the top-level 3–5 objectives, with 1–5 key results per objective, that
11.2	you’re committed to accomplishing in the quarter. Identify which are must-hit
11.2	goals. (See the next section for guidance.)
11.2	Collaborate: If you partner closely with other functions or teams, get feedback
11.2	and make sure you agree on the OKRs you’ve set.
11.2	Publish: When you’re done, change “Draft” to “On track” in the document title to
11.2	signal that your OKRs are published (on the company intranet or other
11.2	document-sharing method) and in progress.
16.9	Guidance on ambition
11.2	Most goals should be ambitious yet attainable. This means we expect to
11.2	achieve the vast majority of them over time, while also recognizing that some
11.2	will be a stretch. Overall, we should expect to score in the 70–80 percent range
11.2	over the quarter.
11.2	A small number of our goals (20–30 percent for any given team in a quarter)
11.2	might be hard commitments. These should be marked as “must-hit,” and we
11.2	expect to reach 95 percent attainment or more. If these goals are trending
11.2	behind, we expect to sacrifice other goals in service of hitting them.
16.9	Good OKRs start with good objectives
15.0	Best practices for good objectives
11.2	Objectives are clearly defined goals that answer the question “Where do I
11.2	want to go?”
11.2	Objectives should be an outcome—a description of the state of the world at
11.2	the end of the quarter. They should focus on the user problem you’re
11.2	solving.
11.2	Objectives should be focused on the most important work. There should be
11.2	no more than 3–5 of them.

[p.154]
11.2	Objectives chart intent and direction—they chart outcomes.
11.2	Objectives are long-term and often span several quarters, even years.
11.2	Objectives nest up and down to some degree in an organization.
11.2	Objectives should be ambitious and can be open to (some) interpretation
11.2	and debate.
11.2	Objectives can be aspirational (70 percent) or committed (100 percent).
11.2	Identify which are which!
11.2	All objectives should matter. The best objectives are inspirational.
11.2	Objectives can be shared across teams.
16.9	Define key results
15.0	Best practices for good key results
11.2	Key results are concrete definitions of success. Each one determines
11.2	whether the objective has been achieved or not and answers the question
11.2	“How will I pace myself to see if I’m on track?”
11.2	Key results should list outcomes, not activities. There should be 1–5 key
11.2	results per objective.
11.2	Key results are measurable and binary: Either they did or didn’t happen.
11.2	They’re not open to interpretation.
11.2	Key results flow from a theory of a plausible causal mechanism that
11.2	connects the key result with the objective and with your own work.
11.2	Key results build a shared model of reality across teams and organizations.
11.2	Key results should compound over time, if possible.
16.9	Common pitfalls when formulating OKRs
11.2	Using an activity or action as an outcome.
11.2	Having a key result that can’t be measured in some way.

[p.155]
11.2	Turning a roadmap directly into objectives and key results.
11.2	Writing more than 3–5 objectives, or 1–5 key results per objective.
30.0	Iterating and Scoring
16.9	Mid-quarter scoring
11.2	At some point in the quarter, the team should get together to discuss progress
11.2	to date and give it an approximate score. The easiest way to do this is a simple
11.2	green, yellow, and red rubric accompanied by an assessment of on track, off
11.2	track, or at risk. (If your team prefers to use percentage-based scoring, do
11.2	what works best.) Items that are off track or at risk should be discussed further
11.2	so that the team can find ways to redirect their energy and build in time to
11.2	recover.
16.9	How to handle changes in information and
16.9	priorities
11.2	If new information arises that changes an objective or key result, there should
11.2	be some common practices to track and handle the changes. This could mean
11.2	scoring an OKR lower or swapping it out. Do whatever makes sense for the
11.2	team, but be consistent and clear. Most importantly, communicate changes to
11.2	any impacted teams, and clearly document what’s changed and why.
11.2	Here’s some general guidance:
11.2	If you add or drop a key result, document the decision and notify impacted
11.2	teams.
11.2	If you add an objective (e.g., due to an urgent shift in the world), document
11.2	whether it resulted in deprioritizing existing work and, if so, what work was
11.2	deprioritized.
11.2	Dropping an objective should be rare. This may happen due to a change in
11.2	constraints or because it truly isn’t providing value to users. In these cases,

[p.156]
11.2	it’s best to stop working on it and keep it in the OKRs for scoring and
11.2	discussion.

11.2	Focus on results: We already had a plan, we took a specific action, and we

11.2	Be candid: The core audience is leadership. They want a candid

11.2	Prepare in advance: Share the memo at least 24 hours in advance. We will
11.2	also allow reading time in the meeting—the meeting will be a discussion
