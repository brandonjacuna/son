What Sŏn uses instead is one connection type and two kinds of reading. Every seat has one domain lead, named by seat on the sheet's field 3 (S5 P33), who holds the check-in, opens the seat, and decides the hire. That is the only lead a seat has, and it is not a reporting line, because the lead does not assign or review. Where a seat's work is read by a second lead, the Head of Beverage's program outputs by the Operations Lead, a runner's timing by the kitchen's pass owner, the second lead reads a record, the stack's outputs or the shift close, and holds no check-in and no decision about the person. Her dotted line's function, that a second leader's judgment reaches the work, is carried by the record being read and by Class B consultation before a decision that crosses domains (S3 4.3). Her solid line's function, that someone develops the person, is carried by the domain lead's check-in. No seat has two leads; no lead holds a "separate 1:1"; nothing about a person is written by a second lead.

**P15 (Recommended).** Sŏn has no solid or dotted lines. A seat has one domain lead, and where a second lead's judgment must reach the work it reaches the record, not the person. Dual reporting, the secondary manager, the second 1:1, and any HR-system field for a second manager are refused.

### 6.2 Functional, business-line, vertical, horizontal

Her macro choice is functional or product-line, and within it teams orient vertically (a region, a product) or horizontally (a support function across all verticals) (Book pp. 270 to 271, Figures 15 and 16). Her business unit "has a unique product that is priced for and sold to a specific customer segment, and can operate independently" (Book p.272).

At Sŏn the axes exist and they are not org-chart axes. The domains are functional: the floor, beverage, the kitchen, the operating architecture. The service periods are the record's business lines in her sense of "different first customers at different times" (WP p.35), each with its own reason to exist and its own operating logic (WP p.34). A period could be drawn as her vertical and a domain as her horizontal running across all periods, and the drawing would be accurate and useless, because at Sŏn the intersection has no manager. The domains own the seats and the check-ins; the periods own the charters, the goals, and the interfaces (section 7); and a person sits in one domain and in whichever periods their sheet's field 4 names. Her business unit is refused outright: a period that "can operate independently" is the separate restaurant the interface test refuses (section 3.3), and the record's whole thesis is that the periods share "one layer underneath that holds all of them" (WP p.33).

**P16 (Recommended).** The domains are the functional axis and the periods are the business-line axis, and the structure is neither functional nor business-line because the intersection has no manager: domains own seats and check-ins, periods own charters, goals, and interfaces. The business unit is refused as the separate restaurant the interface test exists to prevent.

### 6.3 The hybrid, the hinge, and business leads

Her hybrid gives a business lead solid-line ownership of product and engineering, dotted lines into the customer-facing functions, and a hinge, "the pivot point where you turn the focus from the product toward the customer," commonly product marketing (Book pp. 272 to 273, Figure 17). Her business lead runs forums, sets goals for dotted-line reports, calibrates their performance, is evaluated on the business's results, and "there is currently no BL ladder" (Book pp. 274 to 278).

The hinge is the one idea in her structures section that names something the record also names, and the record puts it in a room rather than a team. "The bar is the hinge of the dinner-to-late-night transition" (Canon 1.0, section 06). The pivot from one register to another at Sŏn is a place and a person-triggered moment, not a function embedded between two organizations. And the pivot from the operation toward the customer, which is what her product marketing hinge does, is the whole outward-facing strand: "the customer feels this strand" (WP p.11). Sŏn has no hinge team because the customer-facing pivot is a strand in every domain, not a team between domains.

Her business lead's functions map to the period's holders in section 7, and every one of the lead's powers that assumes altitude is refused: a period has no lead who calibrates the people who work it, no forums of its own beyond the brief and the close, and no ladder question because no seat has one. Her forums rule, "create the fewest, simplest, and most lightweight structures that work" (Book p.277), is the ledger's four-part entry test. Her headcount accounting, dollar budgets and "ring-fenced" heads reported at QBRs (Book pp. 276 to 277), touches figures: financials are not a context source for this program, the house has no headcount to ring-fence because one pool staffs every period, and the seat inventory carries a count per charter and no cost (S5 I1). The gap is stated: whatever labor accounting the financial model runs per period is Dominic's domain and is not on this page. Her performance management and calibration route to Sessions 12 to 15; her goal setting is held by Sessions 3 and 4 (S3 P7.1, S4 section 7).

**P17 (Recommended).** The hinge is refused as a team and held as the record holds it: the bar as the room's hinge between dinner and late night, and the outward-facing strand as the customer-facing pivot in every domain. Business leads, their forums, calibration, and headcount accounting are refused; the period's holders in section 7 carry what survives, and labor accounting per period is a stated gap in Dominic's domain.

### 6.4 Keeping the structure flexible, and reexamining it yearly

Her rule: preserve optionality, remind people the structure will change, reexamine "at least once a year," and neither too often nor too rarely (Book p.265). **P18 (Recommended).** The structure is reexamined at every gate review and at the mechanism reset, changes only by versioned entry, and gets its flexibility from per-service designations rather than from restructuring.

## 7. Who holds a service period

The ledger binds this page to say who holds a service period's goals, measures, and charter without making the period a team in her sense. Session 4 gave the charter its drafting and ruling: the partners with the two leads draft, the partners rule, the chef's seat is in every operating logic field (S4 P49, P50). This section says what "held by" holds.

A service period is held by three things and no person. Its charter is held by the partners' page as a versioned document, ruled by the partners, with the chef partner's vote on lunch the record's own rule (WP p.36). Its goals and measures are held by the three leads together, as Session 3 set, with no one of them owning the period: a period metric that crosses the seam is split at the seam (S3 P9.3), and a period metric that cannot be split has as its owner the charter's "held by" entry, which names one of the three leads per metric, never the period. Its interfaces are held by the leads' review's weekly interface read (S4 11.2) once two periods are open. Its live functions on a given service are held by the designations named at that service's brief. A person who asks "who runs the morning" is answered: the same leads who run dinner, through the morning's charter, its designations, and its interface read, and a room designation from the morning's seats on every morning service.

**P19 (Recommended).** A service period is held by its charter on the partners' page, by the three leads together for its goals and measures with every metric owned by one of them, by the leads' review's interface read for its interfaces, and by the brief's designations for its live functions. Its operating logic field is where authority and pace differ by period without a leadership line. No period has a lead, a team, or a roster of its own.

**P20 (Chef-gated).** The kitchen's part of every period's operating logic field, and the kitchen's designations per period.

## 8. The inward-facing floor strand, runners, hosts, and the Lead Host question

### 8.1 What the record holds

The record names the inward-facing strand's output and intelligence (WP p.10 to 11) and names no owner for it on the floor, where it names Station Leads for the kitchen and the Head of Beverage for beverage (WP p.11; S5 15.8). ### 8.2 The inward-facing strand's domain lead

Three candidates, argued. The Operations Lead, because the strand's output is "the operational sequence" and its intelligence is "architectural and coordinated and timing-precise" (WP p.11), which reads like the Operations Lead's orientation "toward managing the architecture of the business." The Maitre d, because the domain lead's functions are people functions, holding the check-in, opening the seat, deciding the hire (S5 P33), and the Maitre d is "oriented toward the people in the building, team and customers" where the Operations Lead is oriented toward "reading the systems that run it" (WP p.10). A strand lead, a third floor seat below the Maitre d owning runners the way a Station Lead owns a station.

The team's position is the Maitre d, for both floor strands, and the reasoning is structural, not a preference. First, the floor is one domain with two strands, not two domains; the record says "the web is composed of strands, not levels" and then names strands as the composition of a domain (WP p.10 to 11), and a strand with its own lead is a domain by another name. Second, the Operations Lead's outcomes are "labor efficiency, cost targets, compliance, vendor performance, and facility integrity" (WP p.10), and none of them is a runner's development or a runner's hire; giving the Operations Lead a domain of people would make that seat hold check-ins and decide hires, which is the manual people work the record removed from it. Third, the ruling makes the Maitre d "the master of the house," holding the floor team, and the runners are the floor team. Fourth, a strand lead seat is the record's floor manager returning at one remove, and the record's leadership count has no room for it (WP p.22). What the strand needs beneath the Maitre d is not a lead but a designation, because its disturbance class, kitchen-side timing at the pass, is live and per service (section 5).

The Operations Lead reads the strand's outputs through the stack, ticket times and the close's timing incidents, as a record, and consults under Class B when the schedule and the floor's coverage cross (S3 4.3). That is the second-lead reading of section 6.1, and it is not a second lead.

**P21 (Recommended).** The Maitre d is the domain lead for the inward-facing floor strand, and for runners and hosts, as for the outward-facing strand: holding their check-ins, opening their seats, deciding their hires, and holding no advancement. The floor is one domain with two strands. The Operations Lead reads the strand's outputs as a record and holds no floor seat's check-in. Every decision-rights entry that read "domain lead" as incomplete for runner and host seats (S5 P64, S7 16.10, `86akh7r10`) now reads the Maitre d, and the runner and host sheets' field 3 can be filled.

### 8.3 The sequence designation

The record's device for accountability in the moment is the functional designation (WP p.10). On the floor it names one, the Lead Host for the door. The inward-facing strand needs one for the same reason the kitchen needs a pass owner: the Realist's rule that "the pass is command-and-control" and that unowned it "buries the line." The floor's side of the pass, where plates leave the kitchen's ownership and enter the sequence to the table, has no owner in the record. The team proposes a per-service designation on the inward-facing strand, held by a runner seat named at the brief, that owns the floor's side of the pass for that service: the sequence to the table, the call to hold or fire the floor's timing when the kitchen's owner calls a station down, and the first answer to a kitchen failure before it is a room failure. **P22 (Recommended).** A per-service sequence designation on the inward-facing floor strand, held by a runner seat named at the brief, owning the floor's side of the pass for that service and the first answer to a kitchen-side timing disturbance. ### 8.4 Hosts, and whether the Lead Host seat exists

Finding 18.9 asked whether the Lead Host seat exists at all. The answer is that "Lead Host" is a designation and "host" is a seat, and the record says both once it is read with its own vocabulary. **P24 (Recommended).** The Lead Host is a per-service designation, not a seat. ## 10. Levels and ladders, what a lead enters at, and which seats are salaried

### 10.1 Whether a single house needs them

Carryover `86ajgnj4e` asks whether a single house needs levels and ladders at all, and says not to build them because the book assumes them. Session 6 worked what hiring needs from them and found it needs three things, an entry bar per seat, a fixed unlock period per seat, and an entry pay rule from Session 13, and no placement decision, because "everyone enters at the outside edge" (S6 P62, P63). This page answers the structural half.

Sŏn has no levels. The record says so as structure: "Progression develops connections, not altitude. A person who has been in the system longer does not move toward the center. Their node develops more threads. Further along is visible in the number of connections a node has developed, in cross-domain fluency, skill depth, and the ability to hold complexity, not in where they sit vertically" (WP p.11). The case against levels in the carryover, that "levels in a company that does not grow are a promise the company cannot keep," is the record's own: a level is altitude, and the web has none to offer. The case for, that "without levels there is no legible answer to 'what is my path,'" is answered by what carries a ladder's functions without its shape.

### 10.2 What carries what a ladder carries elsewhere

A ladder does five things in her company, and each has a carrier at Sŏn that is not a rung.
