## 1. Bar design principles

### The bar is the engine, not a counter
- `[ellis-public]` A bar is the machine that drives the whole business, as important as the kitchen, not a counter with equipment behind it. Beverage carries higher margin, lower labor, faster ticket times, and repeat rounds, which food does not. [2]
- `[ellis-public]` Ellis frames the bar as a front-of-house factory that is also a showroom. Owners care how the showroom looks and rarely about the factory's efficiency, which is the part that makes or breaks the business. [4] (Book topic: "Theatrical Factories")

### The Empty Square and inside-out design
- `[ellis-public]` The common failure: the bar stays an empty square in the plan set while every other piece of furniture, fixtures, and equipment (FF&E) is carefully placed, and someone else is left to make it work later. [2]
- `[ellis-public]` Inside-out design: design the working machinery first (purpose, objectives, function), then the aesthetic wrap. His analogy: you do not design a car body and hope the engine fits. [2] (Book topic: "Inside-Out 360º Design")
- `[ellis-public]` Aesthetic and operational teams must work in lockstep, with at least one person who translates between the two worlds. [2]

### Who should design your bar
- `[ellis-public]` People who specify and buy bar equipment are almost never the people who use it. Contractors optimize for on-time and on-budget, and they place equipment wherever the drains already are. [4]
- `[ellis-public]` His golf club analogy: a great club comes from a golfer who knows what the club must do working with an engineer. Same for bars. [3]

### Timing: the floor-pour rule
- `[ellis-public]` The first question Ellis asks a client is whether the floors are poured. Once slab, drains, and MEP rough-ins are in, equipment locations are fixed, and no one will core-drill a new build to move a station a few feet. [4]
- `[derived]` For Sŏn this is the strongest argument for doing bar concept work now, pre-lease: the bar layout must drive floor sinks, drains, and electrical rough-in, not the other way around.

### Zero-Step
- `[ellis-public]` If bartenders walk behind the bar, that is non-value time. Chefs stay in their station; bartenders should too. [3]
- `[ellis-equipment]` Every piece of the Perlick Signature Series is built around zero-step bartending: everything the bartender needs within reach without taking a step. [12]

### Neutral Spine and Neutral Reach
- `[ellis-public]` The goal is movement without constant bending, reaching, spinning, or searching for bottles. Ellis calls a bar that achieves this "the dance." [3]
- `[ellis-equipment]` Design moves in the cockpit that serve this: a narrow front speed rail the bartender stands into; curved rails that hug the body and widen at the sides for larger bottles; a shallower ice bin so the bartender stands closer to the drink and the customer; raised work height to cut reaching and bending. [7][9][10]
- `needs-book` The actual neutral reach envelope and spine angles Ellis designs to. See "Ergonomic Dimensions."

### Flow State
- `[ellis-public]` Ellis's framing: hospitality happens in the gaps between service steps, like music between notes. Fewer steps give the bartender time to connect with the customer. No amount of training fixes a bartender who walks 20 feet for every drink. [4]
- `needs-book` The full "Flow State" chapter.

### Peak Volume
- `[ellis-public]` Operators make their money in a few peak hours, a couple of nights a week. The bar must be designed to capture that peak, not the average. [3]
- `[perlick-training]` Count stations for the busiest rush with every seat full. A station can be closed on a slow night; one cannot be added later. [13]

### Shared equipment, adjacencies, zone vs. brigade
- `[perlick-training]` Every station should have its own equipment, since sharing slows service. The one shared item Perlick names is the hand sink, placed at the start of the bar where servers can reach it. [13]
- `needs-book` Ellis's own rules for "Shared Equipment," "Equipment Adjacencies," and "Zone vs. Brigade Design." These are core to Sŏn's layout decisions.

### Design Foundation, Optimizing the UX
- `needs-book` Both chapters.

## 2. Bar design practices

### Identical Cockpits; Repeat, Don't Mirror
- `[perlick-training]` Duplicate stations rather than mirroring them. Mirroring reverses the setup, which puts expensive bottles in the left hand and runs the flow from dirty to clean. Duplicated stations mean no one fights over the "good" station. [13]
- `[perlick-training]` Default flow left to right, clean to dirty: clean glassware, then the well and ingredients, then trash, dump sink, and dirty glass. The spirits go to the dominant (right) hand; mixers, soda gun, and garnish go to the left. Most-used base spirits sit in the front row. [13]
- `[book]` One cockpit design per outlet, repeated identically; fix fit problems by changing anything but the cockpit; across several bars keep one design. Reason: procedural memory, so bartenders work heads-up. (pp. 47 to 48; book-notes/identical-cockpits.md)
- `needs-book` Ellis's exact cockpit sequence and how he handles left-handed staff.

### How many stations
- `[perlick-training]` Perlick publishes a cocktail station calculator from seat count, drinks per hour per bartender, and ice volume. [13]
- `[book]` One bartender per station, no sharing except a deliberate brigade design; count complete, identical stations. Ellis ranks four ways to get the count: available space (a test fit), head-count ratios (misleading outside catering), floor plan plus concept, and, most reliable, reverse-engineering the pro forma (beverage revenue to drinks per hour to bartenders). Design to fit the business, not the drawing, and make the case early. (pp. 51 to 55; book-notes/how-many-stations.md)

### The Pickup, The Point, and The Well
- `[ellis-public]` On construction walkthroughs Ellis asks where the pickup is, where the support section goes, and where the pass is. [3]
- `[book]` The server pickup sits directly across the counter from the service well, never offset; it is a full service area (at least two POS printers, trash and recycling, storage, room for two to six staff), isolated from customer traffic. (pp. 57 to 58; book-notes/the-pickup-and-the-well.md)
- `needs-book` "The Point and The Well".

### Back-of-house service bars; stadium design by POS
- `needs-book` Both. Relevant if Sŏn's dining room drinks come from a service bar rather than the main bar.

### Death to Double Speedrails
- `[perlick-training]` Keep the distance from the bartender's hips (against the speed rail) to the drink rail to about 10 to 11 inches at most. A double speed rail pushes that out and forces a lean over. Use one rail plus tiered liquor steps. [13]
- `[ellis-public]` Real bartending means running up to the rail and hitting it with the knees. Rails must take that impact without bottles falling out, which is why he redesigned them rounded. [4][9]

### Engine Parts and Bar Geometry
- `[ellis-equipment]` The cockpit components are sold separately, so a small operator can buy only the pieces that fit (for example, just the sinks). [4]
- `[book]` Arrive at a shape from requirements, never by preference. Three foundational footprints: shotgun (cheapest, simplest, smallest), peninsula (hybrid; service and socialization, at a cost in flow and build), island (aesthetic and social; misses customers out of view, strands staff, 2 to 3 times the cost when radiused). Peninsula or island needs about twice a shotgun's room width. Avoid acute angles and tight radii. (pp. 71 to 80; book-notes/bar-geometry.md)
- `needs-book` "Engine Parts".

### Construction: die walls, counters, floor sinks, stick-built vs. ModBar, ADA counters, scuppers
- `[ellis-public]` A scupper (also called a drink rail or peanut rail) is the drained, perforated steel strip along the bartender's edge of the bar top where tins and glasses sit during building. [4]
- `[ellis-public]` Putting glass rinsers in a scupper rail is common and, per Ellis, a health code violation everywhere he has worked: food prep and waste areas cannot combine without physical separation. [4]
- `[perlick-training]` A modular bar (ModBar style) carries longer lead time and must be installed first, since it bolts to the subfloor. [13]
- `[book]` Floor sinks are placed after the equipment engine is final, under each station's ice bin, set back 8 to 10 in from the equipment edge. (pp. 101 to 102; book-notes/floor-sinks.md)
- `[book]` Accessible bar counter: waterfall it (lowered customer side, full-height bartender side); never use it as the pickup, never put stools or decor on it, no lift gates or flip-up counters, proper approach space. TAS governs in Texas. (pp. 107 to 110; book-notes/ada-bar-counters.md)
- `needs-book` Die wall and counter construction, the stick-built vs. ModBar decision, scupper details.

### Back bar, draft, wine
- `[perlick-training]` Back bar refrigerators are 24 in deep, not the 31 in kitchen depth. Leave about 1 in each side so a unit can be pulled for cleaning. [13]
- `needs-book` Back bar designs, draft dispensing, wine storage and service.

### Health code compliance
- `[ellis-public]` Much bartender-improvised equipment ("MacGyvering") violates health, building, or egress codes. Ellis treats compliance as non-negotiable. Local rules vary: New Jersey wants an audible and visual alarm on high-temp glasswashers; Los Angeles County has required a three-compartment sink even with a glasswasher. [4]
- `[ellis-public]` Custom fabrication runs roughly three to eight times the cost of catalog equipment. Custom work must come from an NSF-approved shop. [4]
- `[derived]` Check every Sŏn bar idea against Austin Public Health plan review and `codes/register.yaml` before falling in love with it.

### Ice
- `[ellis-public]` If an expensive machine makes perfect clear cubes, storing them in a room-temperature bin wastes them. Specialty ice belongs in a freezer drawer. [4]
- `[ellis-equipment]` The Perlick Ice Vault, designed with Ellis, is marketed as the only NSF-certified product to store and prepare craft ice. [12]
- `[perlick-training]` Specialty ice needs its own freezer. Deeper and bigger ice chests are not better: they are harder to reach into and to empty. Use dividers for multiple ice types. [13]

## 4. Aesthetics, etc.
- `[ellis-public]` Acoustics: Ellis reads a room by finding the first reflecting surface for sound and by watching where staff paths and customer traffic pinch. [5]
- `[ellis-public]` Non-alcoholic drinks are one of the fastest-growing categories. Customers want to feel part of the full experience whatever they drink, so the bar must serve zero-proof with the same care. [3]
- `[ellis-public]` The most important bartender quality, per Ellis, is empathy; the barback is the backbone of the bar. [3]
- `[derived]` Implication: design real space and paths for the barback (restock routes, support section), not just for bartenders.
- `needs-book` Lighting, climate, finishing materials, "The Forgotten," the design process chapters, and "10 Truths About Bar Design."
