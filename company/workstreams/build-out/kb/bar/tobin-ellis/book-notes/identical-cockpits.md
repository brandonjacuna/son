# Identical Cockpits

## The idea in Brandon's words
(Paraphrase from the book pages Brandon sent; no copied passages.)

Speed behind the bar buys everything else: the less time a drink takes, the more time the bartender has to read the room, talk with customers, taste, restock, and give real hospitality. Speed comes from procedural memory: the body learns reaches when every object stays in the same place, the same principle as mise en place. Ellis compares a station to a keyboard: if the keys move, typing slows and errors climb.

So every station is identical. A station that is a little bigger, smaller, or differently equipped stops bartenders from automating; their eyes drop to hunt for things, they cannot listen to customers while their brain re-maps the station, and their hands slow. Ellis's sequence: evaluate the requirements, design one cockpit for that outlet, then repeat it exactly as many times as needed. If the space does not fit, change something else, never the cockpit. Across a building with several bars, keep cockpits as close to one design as possible so bartenders and breakers (staff who float between bars to cover breaks) are equally fast anywhere.

A bartender in an unfamiliar or varied setup spends much of the shift adapting, which costs revenue, customer wait time, and heads-up hospitality. For the designer, one cockpit block repeated is also faster to draw and to revise: a spec change in the block updates every station.

## Rules and numbers
- One cockpit design per outlet, repeated identically for every station (p. 48).
- Fit problems are solved by changing anything except the cockpit (p. 48).
- Multiple bars in one building: stay as close to one cockpit design as possible (p. 48).
- No dimensions in these pages.

## What Ellis says to avoid
- Stations that differ in size, features, or layout, even slightly (p. 47).
- Fitting cockpits to the space one by one (p. 48).
