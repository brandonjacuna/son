# A.D.A. Bar Counters

## The idea in Brandon's words
(Paraphrase from the book pages Brandon sent; no copied passages.)

A share of bar seating must be wheelchair accessible: a lowered counter section with knee clearance, approachable from the front or side, giving the wheelchair user the same experience as everyone else. Some architects read the rule as requiring the whole depth of the counter to drop to the lower height, which leaves too little room underneath for bar equipment, almost all of which is taller than that space.

Ellis's preferred solution is "waterfalling": a step in the middle of the die wall that drops from the standard counter height on the bartender's side to the accessible height on the customer side, extended slightly to give knee clearance. Bar equipment installs normally behind it. He reports that an ADA compliance specialist he interviewed judged waterfalled counters compliant and had never heard of a wheelchair user complaining about them. Eye level in a wheelchair already sits at or above a standard counter, so lowering the whole counter adds nothing but a view of the bartender's floor. Bartenders can still serve it: lowering the arm a few inches costs a few inches of reach. A second accessible counter built under the main counter is another acceptable answer.

What invites litigation: using the accessible counter as the server pickup; putting stools, chairs, vases, or decor on or under it; anything that makes the wheelchair user ask for the space. Also avoid: a lift-up gate on a hinge that doubles as the staff entrance and the accessible counter (it blocks staff when in use); counters that flip up or lift mechanically; accessible counters tucked into back corners without proper approach and turning space. A licensed local architect who knows the accessibility rules is critical; Ellis names corada.com as a resource.

## Rules and numbers
- 5 percent of bar seating wheelchair accessible (p. 107).
- Accessible section about 3 to 5 ft long, at most 34 in above finished floor, with 19 in knee clearance (p. 107).
- A fully lowered counter leaves about 30 to 32 in of space under it; bar equipment with backsplash runs at least 34 to 36 in tall, and 29 to 31 in without (p. 107).
- Standard bar counter 42 in; wheelchair eye level about 43 to 51 in (p. 108).
- Lowered arm reach loss about 3 to 6 in at that distance (p. 108).
- 60 in is the standard wheelchair turning diameter; required approach space depends on the standard's specifics (p. 110).
- Example caption: about 60 in (two seats) of a counter reserved for wheelchair users (p. 107).

## What Ellis says to avoid
- The accessible counter used as the server pickup, or with stools or decor on or under it.
- A lift-gate entrance that doubles as the accessible counter.
- Flip-up or mechanically lifted accessible counters.
- Accessible counters in corners without approach and turning space.

## Sŏn application
- Open until a footprint exists. At about 8 bar seats, the accessible section still needs its own length and approach space; place it in the first test fit, not after.
- Texas: the Texas Accessibility Standards govern (the bar seat's 05-tas card: TAS 902 dining surfaces 28 to 34 in; 904.4 counters; 306 knee and toe clearance). Ellis cites the federal ADA; every number here is unverified for Sŏn until `codes/register.yaml` carries the TAS item and a licensed architect confirms it.

## Conflicts with other sources
- Ellis's "at most 34 in" agrees with TAS 902.3's 28 to 34 in range; TAS also sets a minimum. Ellis's "5 percent" and section length are his reading of the ADA; confirm TAS scoping (Chapter 2, not yet read).
