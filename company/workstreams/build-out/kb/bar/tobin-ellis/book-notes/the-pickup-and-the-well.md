# The Pickup and The Well

## The idea in Brandon's words
(Paraphrase from the book pages Brandon sent; no copied passages.)

The server pickup (where servers collect drinks from the customer side of the bar) belongs directly across the bar counter from the service well. Ellis treats this as one of the few absolutes, and splitting them as one of the most common bar design errors. Across from each other, the service bartender pours, sets the drink down, and moves to the next ticket, and the bartender and servers talk constantly. Offset, even close by, the bartender hand-carries drinks a couple at a time to the pickup, which wastes motion with only a few servers, and communication slows, with effects across the whole restaurant. A split pickup signals a designer who does not know bar operations. Any arrangement that keeps the pickup on the opposite side of the well beats splitting them.

The second common miss is sizing the pickup as a small counter. The pickup is a service area: servers pour their own sodas, print checks, enter orders, and talk to the bar staff there. It needs trash and recycling, POS terminals with printers, and storage for check presenters, silverware and roll-ups, menus, condiments, bus tubs, and glassware. Two to six staff may use it at once. Shrinking it for looks or for more customer space backfires: it creates flow problems and hurts the customer experience. A larger pickup, isolated from customer areas, speeds service and the room, the same way the kitchen pass does.

## Rules and numbers
- Pickup directly opposite the service well, across the counter; never offset (p. 57).
- POS terminals with printers at the pickup: at least two (p. 58).
- Room for at least two and up to about six staff at once (p. 58).
- Isolated from customer traffic (p. 58).

## What Ellis says to avoid
- Splitting the pickup from the well, even by a short distance (p. 57).
- A small counter as the pickup; shrinking it for aesthetics or seats (p. 58).
