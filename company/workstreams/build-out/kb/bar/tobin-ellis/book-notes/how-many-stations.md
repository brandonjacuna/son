# How Many Stations?

## The idea in Brandon's words
(Paraphrase from the book pages Brandon sent; no copied passages.)

A station is one bartender's complete, identical working set, and the count of those stations is the metric for any bar that is not a stadium. One bartender per station, always: two people sharing one station are no faster than one, the same way two designers sharing one desk are not. The only exception is a bar deliberately designed, with its playbook, as a brigade, and that is harder than it looks and not to be tried casually.

Ellis gives four ways to arrive at the count, from weakest to strongest:
1. **Available space.** Design one good cockpit, then fit as many identical copies as the footprint allows, separated by compliance, storage, and warewashing. Fast, risky, and mainly useful as a test fit: it shows early whether the footprint can support the business.
2. **Head count.** Catering ratios (bartenders per customer) work for banquets because the head count, timing, and drink list are known. A brick-and-mortar bar knows almost none of that, so the ratio misleads. Concept, location, menu, batching versus a la minute, jigger versus free pour, menu touring, and crowd all change how many people one bartender can serve. Ellis calls the missing-information problem the "complexity brake": designers lack the beverage program because operators are hired after the design is set.
3. **Reverse-engineer the pro forma.** The most reliable method. Start from the budgeted annual beverage revenue in the business plan, divide down to revenue per operating hour, then by average drink price, to get drinks per hour. Then ask how many bartenders it takes to sell, make, serve, and tender that many drinks, given the service model, program, systems, and staff. Compare that to what the test fit holds, and use the gap as the data-backed case for changing the bar.
4. **Floor plan plus concept.** When the inputs are missing, read the first front-of-house plan (seats, bar footprint, adjacencies) and the concept. A full-service room needs a dedicated service well regardless. Bar seats are worked with fewer customers per bartender in a full-service setting because the bar service progression is labor heavy.

When the count does not fit the drawn footprint, the designer's job is to take the numbers back to the design team and make the case early: architects accommodate data-backed changes made early and resist late ones. Always prepare a fallback count, but know that designing the bar to fit the drawing rather than the business caps revenue for the life of the bar. Build for peak success, not for getting by.

## Rules and numbers
- One bartender per station; no shared stations unless a full brigade design and playbook exist (p. 51).
- Station footprint: roughly 6 to 9 ft of front-facing underbar equipment, plus another 6 to 9 ft of back bar, per station (p. 51). Sharing on the back bar is far less harmful than sharing on the front bar or underbar (p. 51).
- Smallest complete underbar station from catalog equipment: about 6 ft 8 in, including POS and trash (p. 55). A steakhouse station runs closer to 8 to 9 ft (p. 55).
- Bar compliance and support (warewashing, hand sinks, service items: roll-ups and silver, linen, bus tubs, condiments, menus) takes length beyond the stations themselves (p. 55).
- Pro forma method, worked example structure (p. 53): annual beverage revenue / 52 weeks = weekly; weekly / operating hours per week = hourly; hourly / average drink price = drinks per hour. His example is illustrative, not a Sŏn figure.
- Bartenders for a given drinks-per-hour figure vary widely (his answer for his example: two to four), because selling, serving, and tendering cost more time than making, except at a service well (p. 53).
- Questions he asks to narrow it (p. 54): the beverage menu, how many cocktails, veteran versus trained-up staff, a la minute versus pre-batched versus fully batched, food-to-beverage mix, liquor-beer-wine mix, and service level.
- Floor plan example (p. 54): a full-service room's bar seats work at about eight seated customers per bartender; the dining room is served by one service-well bartender. Illustrative of the reasoning, not a rule for Sŏn.

## What Ellis says to avoid
- Two bartenders on one station.
- Sizing from catering head-count ratios.
- Designing the bar to fit the drawing instead of the business; "making it fit" by shrinking the most profitable area of the restaurant.
- Asking for bar changes late, after other trades have submitted drawings.

## Sŏn application
- Method (Brandon, 2026-10-09): size Sŏn's stations by the pro forma method from the Investor Review workbook's beverage revenue and operating hours (pulled by the session from Box; never estimated here), then check the result against the seat count (about 8 bar seats, two wells).
- The two-well split stays open. Ellis's reasoning (a full-service room needs a dedicated service well) is one input to that decision, not the decision.
- Every Sŏn station keeps its own complete cockpit; the shared hand sink and back bar are the places sharing costs least.
- A test fit of two identical stations plus compliance and support is the first concept deliverable once the footprint is known.
