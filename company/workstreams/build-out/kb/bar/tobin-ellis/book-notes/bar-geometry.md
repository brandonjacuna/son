# Bar Geometry

## The idea in Brandon's words
(Paraphrase from the book pages Brandon sent; no copied passages.)

The bar's shape and size are strategic decisions, usually made by preference, which Ellis calls the most dangerous way to make them. Shape sets the relationships between the point and the well, the well and the pickup, the pickup and the kitchen, and how customer and staff traffic compete at peak. Arrive at a shape from the requirements (room volume, room shape, budget, business), working inside out; do not fall for one. The more complex the shape for looks alone, the harder and costlier it is to design and build. A straight bar against a wall is the fastest, easiest, and cheapest.

Ellis names three foundational footprints rather than counting sides or letters (one-sided, U, L), because side-and-letter names limit options:
1. **Shotgun:** any bar running almost entirely parallel to a major wall, up to three sides. Most common, cheapest, simplest, smallest equipment and counter footprint; the structural wall saves building a back bar wall and simplifies MEP. Liabilities he scores: socialization and aesthetics.
2. **Peninsula:** attached to a wall but projecting into the room. A hybrid that takes strengths from shotgun and island. Strengths: service and socialization; liabilities: flow and build cost. (Most of his peninsula text is on p. 74, not sent.)
3. **Island:** the counter and everything behind it stand free in the room, often with an island back bar. Strengths: aesthetics and socialization; liabilities: service and build cost. Radiused islands waste space fitting rectangular equipment into curves and lose function at corners; curved fabrication costs two to three times as much at minimum. Operationally, people see roughly 190 to 200 degrees, so about half an island bar is out of the bartender's view at any moment and customers get missed; staff are stranded, crossing the crowd to restock; and the back bar is much smaller than the front, so many stations share little back bar.

Variants: **serpentine** (a curving shotgun with several radiused articulations: social and dramatic, hard and costly to design, and can bottleneck room traffic where it bulges out); **catwalk** (a long, narrow peninsula or island with no back bar: everything lives under the counter, bartenders walk sideways to reach it; works for very small bars, gets worse as it grows; usually chosen because the room is too narrow for a real back bar); **horseshoe** (a symmetrical, fully curved peninsula; no design advantage over the peninsula it belongs to). Any other shape (guitar, kidney) still falls into the three footprints.

Room flow: Ellis favors "broken lines of sight" (progressive disclosure): customers see some of the room, not all of it. Back bar islands, partitions, and screens around a three- or four-sided bar help flow, privacy, and bartender workflow; loops spread traffic, dead ends pile it up, and perceived density, not actual density, makes a room feel crowded.

Dangerous projections: avoid acute angles and very tight radii. His case: an acute-angle peninsula corner that staff could not serve, needed costly custom steel, cost the owners three bar managers in 18 months, and the restaurant closed.

## Rules and numbers
- Shotgun is a single line of equipment; peninsula and island are double lines and take at least twice the room width (p. 79).
- Shotgun cross-section, back wall to the far side of the aisle behind the stools (pp. 79 to 80): back bar equipment 24 to 28 in deep; clearance from back bar to underbar 36 in (Ellis calls it code-required; unverified for Sŏn); underbar equipment 24 to 28 in; die wall 5 to 7 in; customer-side counter overhang 9 to 12 in; stool space 24 to 36 in; aisle behind stools 36 to 60 in. Total 13 ft 2 in to 17 ft 3 in for a shotgun.
- Peninsula or island needs about twice that room width, 26 to 35 ft; an island needs it in both directions (p. 80).
- A 25 by 100 ft room (the classic city commercial lot) rules out an island bar, or forces a tight one with no seating around it (p. 80).
- Radiused or tight island bars: at least two to three times the cost, with fabrication and fit issues that can delay the project (p. 75).
- Bartender field of view roughly 190 to 200 degrees (p. 75).

## What Ellis says to avoid
- Choosing a shape by preference or by a story about the experience it will create.
- Acute angles and very tight radii.
- A catwalk where the room is too narrow for a real back bar, unless the bar is very small.
- Islands in rooms too small for them.
