## 1. What she asks for

Her framework has three parts, and she gives them a house: founding documents are the foundation, the operating system is the structural beams, the operating cadence is the mechanical components. Founding documents "detail the plans for the entire enterprise, including the company's long-term goals and principles and your company philosophy: why and how you exist and operate." Their job is to make sure "the operating systems you put in place share a common purpose, flow from your objectives, and are guided by a clear company ethos from top to bottom." A secondary job: when values are explicit, "expectations are known to all," which "makes it easier to give feedback when someone isn't meeting expectations."

Four documents:

- **Mission.** Why the company exists. "Descriptive and aspirational." Her test for descriptive: "another organization should not be able to have the same mission." Her test for aspirational: "it's unlikely a mission will ever be fully achieved." Every layer, team, division, company, has one, and each ladders up.
- **Long-term goals.** "The bigger-picture ambitions we hoped to achieve or improve over a period of years." Stripe's fit on two pages and had not changed in eight years. Quarterly and annual goals ladder up to them, so they are "a necessary precursor" to the goal-setting the rest of her chapter builds.
- **Principles.** The values that "establish the culture that enables you to work toward those goals." Her tests: "relevant, believable, enduring, and deliverable." Her warning: "too many companies make their principles so idealistic that they don't resonate with employees because they have no connection to how the company actually operates." Her drafting method: write down notable moments in the company's history and ask what belief prevailed. Revisit every year or two. What matters most is "ensuring that they are truly expressed in how we hire, reward, lead, and conduct ourselves."
- **Team charters.** A page per team that states why the team exists, its long-term goals, "what others can expect from the team," key metrics, major risks and dependencies, and how to work with it. Her template (workbook pp. 15 to 22): mission, vision, customers, metrics, strategic importance, major risks, provided interfaces, dependent interfaces.

She also gives a timing rule that matters for Sŏn more than any other sentence in the chapter: founding documents "become an even more important touchstone when a company grows beyond about 40 or 50 people."

## 2. The reframe: what a founding document is for in a house that does not inflate

Her documents organize arrival. Stripe went from a few hundred to thousands while she was there; the founding documents were the thing that let a person hired in year six hold the same purpose as a person hired in year one, without either of them ever meeting a founder. That is what "touchstone" means in her sentence. The documents replace founder proximity as headcount dilutes it.

Sŏn does not have that problem, and a charter written as if it did would be organizing a growth that is not coming. The record describes "roughly eight leadership lines running the full span of the day against fourteen running one dinner service, and a kitchen of two title-tiers instead of four" (WP p.22). The building opens near full size and stays there. Nobody at Sŏn will be more than one conversation from a founder for years.

So what varies at Sŏn, if not headcount? Four things, and each is a way for a company to stop being one company.

**The hour.** Four service periods run out of one kitchen under one name. The white paper names this the hard problem: "Keeping a restaurant coherent across the day is not obvious, and getting it wrong is common" (WP p.33). Its diagnosis of the peer that failed is precise: "A daypart added as an afterthought, without its own reason to exist and its own operating logic, becomes a worse version of dinner, however well it is executed" (WP p.34). A late-night team and an early-morning team can drift into two companies at one address without anyone hiring a single extra person.

**The domain.** Three founding seats own three domains, and the record's whole structural claim rests on domains that are "small, specific, explicitly defined, interlocking" (WP p.09). Where two domains land on one object, the record predicts what happens: "The absence of a clear path is where politics begin. Politics are a load-bearing structure problem" (WP p.11). Section 7 of this page finds one such seam already in the record.

**Time, and the order things arrive in.** "Dinner opens first and runs on its own for at least a quarter... Lunch and brunch sit further back still, not to be revisited until the restaurant has operated for a full year, and whether lunch is ever built at all is an equal decision among all three partners" (WP p.36). Decisions made while dinner runs alone will bind late night when it opens. A founding document is how a decision made in the first quarter reaches a service period that does not exist yet without being re-derived by whoever is standing there.

**The seat that has not arrived.** The chef partner will join a company that has already decided a great deal. A founding document is how the company tells that partner what was settled before them, what was deliberately held for them, and which is which.

And there is a fifth reason, the one that settles the question of whether Sŏn needs founding documents at all. The record has already spent them. The white paper's onboarding design reads: "Why comes before how. Orientation to the mission, the values, and the behavioral standard happens before operational training, because a person needs the reason before the mechanics" (WP p.20). The white paper also stakes the company on being "built so it does not depend on either founder's memory" (WP p.06). Sŏn's record already orients every new person to a mission and a set of values before their first shift, and already stakes the company on an organization that does not run on founder recall. Neither commitment can be kept by a mission and values that exist only in the founders' heads. The founding documents are not an import from her book. They are a debt the record has already incurred.

**Position 2.1 (Recommended).** Sŏn's founding document is a coherence instrument, not a growth instrument. Its job is to keep one company across four service periods, three founding domains, a phased opening, and a founding seat that arrives late. Every section of it is tested against one question: does this settle something that would otherwise be re-decided at a different hour, by a different domain, or by a person who arrived later? A section that does not pass that test is not founding material and belongs in the operating system (Session 3) or the cadence (Session 4).

**Position 2.2 (Recommended).** The working name for the document is the operating charter. "Charter" rather than "constitution" or "manifesto," because the document assigns and limits authority rather than declaring belief, and because Sŏn's voice rules forbid the manifesto register (see section 6.4). The name is provisional and Brandon may change it; the function is not.

## 3. The standing problem: what governs what

Before the charter can hold anything, it has to have standing, and right now the record does not leave room for it.

### 3.1 Canon already claims the whole company

The deck's cover, marked GOVERNING, reads: "Brand and experiential guidelines. The source of truth for every decision made under this name" (Deck p.01). The text canon's preamble: "The six-tier authority hierarchy governs all decisions. Higher tiers override lower tiers without exception" (Canon preamble). The deck's closing page gives the only conflict rules canon holds: "When this document conflicts with a habit, the document wins. When it conflicts with itself, the higher tier wins" (Deck p.94).

Neither artifact states what happens when canon conflicts with a different founding document. Canon has rules for canon against habit and canon against itself. It has no rule for canon against a charter, because at the time canon was written there was no charter. The session brief for this work asserts a clean split: canon governs how Sŏn speaks, looks, and positions; the charter governs how the company runs, decides, and holds itself accountable. That is a reasonable split. Canon does not agree that it exists. A charter that simply appears beside a document claiming to be the source of truth for every decision is not a founding document. It is a competing claim, and the company would hold two documents each asserting supremacy, which is precisely the drift canon's own rule forbids: "A living document changes by versioned decision, never by drift" (Deck p.94).

### 3.2 There are three documents, not two

The operating agreement Brandon and Dominic have already worked on is the third. The team has not read it; it is not on this program's allowlist and it is a legal instrument. But its existence changes the charter's shape. An operating agreement governs ownership, capital, and the legal authority of principals. A charter that assigns decision rights among the same principals either restates the agreement, contradicts it, or must declare that it sits beneath it. Only the third is safe.

### 3.3 The relationship the charter must declare

**Position 3.1 (Recommended).** The charter opens with a standing clause that names its relationship to the two documents that already exist, in this shape:

- *To the operating agreement.* The operating agreement governs ownership, capital, and the legal authority of the principals. The charter governs operating authority. Where the two touch, the operating agreement wins, and the charter is amended to match. The charter does not restate the agreement's terms.
- *To the brand canon.* Canon governs how Sŏn speaks, looks, positions, and is experienced by the customer. The charter governs how the company runs, decides, compensates, and holds itself accountable. Each cites the other and neither restates the other. Where a single decision falls under both, a seam rule states which governs.

The structure of that clause is the team's to recommend. Its content is not fully the team's: the seam rule (what happens when a canon rule and a charter rule bear on one decision) is exactly the question section 4 lays out, and it is founder-gated.

**Position 3.2 (Recommended).** Canon must be amended, by versioned decision under its own rule, so that "the source of truth for every decision made under this name" is scoped to the decisions canon actually governs, and so that canon states its relationship to the charter in the same words the charter uses for canon. Two documents that describe their boundary differently are already in conflict. This is a change to a governing document; whoever holds versioning authority over canon makes it.

**Position 3.3 (Founder-gated).** Who holds versioning authority over canon, and who holds it over the charter. The record does not name a canon owner. Canon's sonic governance names Brandon at Tier 1 for a sub-system; nothing names an owner for canon itself. Brandon is "the origin of the operating concepts" (WP p.03) and the canon reads as his; but the charter cannot record that as a decision. What the team can say: the charter must name the versioning authority for both documents, and the chef partner's relationship to canon versioning is part of the chef-seat question in section 9. Until the chef is seated, the interim authority must also be named.

**What breaks if this stays open.** Every downstream session writes positions that cite canon or the charter. If the boundary between them is unstated, Session 3's operating system, Session 5's hiring criteria, and Session 13's compensation architecture will each quietly pick one, and the company inherits three different answers to which document governs.

## 4. The authority hierarchy conflict

The two canon artifacts, both on the allowlist, both declaring themselves closed and governing, state materially different six-tier hierarchies. Verified against both source texts.

| Tier | `brand-guidelines.md` section 02 (Version 1.0) | Deck pp. 32 and 93 (Version 3.0) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cultural Frameworks (Jaeyeonmi, Ma, Mahk) | Cultural frameworks (Jaeyeonmi, Ma, Mahk) |
| 2 | Positioning + Playground Philosophy | **Company non-negotiables** (no exploitation, toxicity, or disposable people; employee-first) |
| 3 | Jeong + Nunchi (service philosophy) | Positioning and philosophy (the three qualities; Playground, Jeong, Nunchi) |
| 4 | Brand Architecture | Architecture and identity |
| 5 | Voice and Tone | Executional systems (voice, type, color, space, sound, service, digital, photography) |
| 6 | Persona Profiles | Persona profiles |

The material difference is tier 2. The deck ranks the company's people commitments above positioning, philosophy, architecture, and every executional system, with override authority. The text version does not place them in the hierarchy at all; it states them in section 01 as a standalone block and never ranks them. The deck also carries a sentence the text version lacks: "The company protects four things in order, and the order is the decision. FIRST Bandwidth / THEN Joy / THEN Retention / THEN Results" (Deck p.04). The white paper agrees with the deck's spirit: "Bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results. This is the metabolic sequence... This is mechanical, not aspirational" (WP pp. 12, 16 to 17).

### 4.1 Why this is the load-bearing question for a charter

For a brand document, tier 2 is a placement detail. For an operating charter, it is the whole question. Under the deck's hierarchy, "no disposable people" outranks a brand or service decision, and a service protocol that treated a person as disposable would be wrong by rule. Under the text version, the same commitment sits beside the brand system, and a conflict between them has no stated resolution. "The order is the decision" converts a sequence into a tiebreaker: when bandwidth and results conflict, bandwidth wins. Adopted into a charter, that sentence has operating consequences. A short-staffed night is answered by reducing covers rather than thinning the floor. A profitable service period that consumes the team's bandwidth is cut or restructured before it is defended on results. Those are real commitments with real costs, and they are why the question cannot be settled by picking the more recent version number.

### 4.2 The three rulings available

A founder can rule in one sitting by choosing one of these. Each is stated with what it commits Sŏn to.

**Ruling A: the deck's hierarchy governs; the text canon is out of date.** Commits Sŏn to: the people non-negotiables and the protection order as tier-2 rules inside canon, overriding brand and service decisions. The text canon is re-versioned to match. Consequence: a brand document holds the company's labor commitments and its operating tiebreaker. That is workable, but it means the charter's people section restates canon rather than owning the commitment, and it puts the chef partner's relationship to labor commitments inside a document about brand.

**Ruling B: the text canon's hierarchy governs; the non-negotiables move out of canon entirely.** Commits Sŏn to: canon as a brand document with no people tier, and the non-negotiables and protection order living only in the charter. Consequence: the seam rule in section 3 must then say the charter's people commitments override canon's tiers, or the commitments lose the override authority the deck currently gives them. The deck is re-versioned to remove tier 2.

**Ruling C: the non-negotiables and the protection order are the charter's highest tier, and canon states that all six of its tiers operate beneath them.** Commits Sŏn to: a single top tier shared by both documents, held in the charter and cited by canon. Consequence: canon's tier 1 cultural frameworks, which currently have "veto power over any downstream decision" (Canon 01), would sit beneath the people commitments. That is a substantive change: it means a decision that satisfies Jaeyeonmi, Ma, and Mahk but treats a person as disposable is wrong. The deck already implies this by ranking non-negotiables at tier 2 with veto over everything below but not over tier 1; ruling C moves them one step further. Both artifacts are re-versioned.

The team's observation, offered as argument and not as the ruling: ruling C is the only one of the three that is consistent with position 3.1 (canon governs the customer-facing company, the charter governs how the company runs) and with the white paper's claim that the metabolic sequence is "mechanical, not aspirational." Under A the operating tiebreaker lives in a brand document; under B it loses its override; under C it lives where operating commitments live and canon defers to it. But C also asks whether the cultural frameworks are outranked by anything, which is a question about Sŏn's identity, and that is not the team's to answer.

**Position 4.1 (Founder-gated).** Which hierarchy governs, and where the company non-negotiables and the protection order sit relative to canon's tiers. One of rulings A, B, or C, or a fourth Brandon supplies. Filed as a founder decision below.

**Position 4.2 (Founder-gated).** Whether "the order is the decision" is adopted into the charter as an operating tiebreaker, with the consequences named in 4.1 stated and accepted. The record holds it in the deck and the white paper; the text canon does not carry it; adopting it is a decision about how Sŏn will trade bandwidth against results on a real night.

**Position 4.3 (Recommended).** Canon exists in one version of record. The text canon (1.0) and the deck (3.0) have drifted from each other on a load-bearing point, in violation of canon's own rule. Before the charter cites either, they are reconciled by a versioned decision that names one artifact as the canon of record and the other as a rendering of it. This is filed as a document work item. The charter, once it exists, cites canon by version.

## 5. The operating charter: specification

This is the core deliverable. Downstream clusters build the document from it. Each section states its purpose, what it must contain, what it must do, what it must refuse, where its content comes from, and what breaks if it is wrong or missing.

### 5.1 What the document is

One document, short, declarative, in Sŏn's voice. It states what the company is for, what it protects and in what order, who owns which domain, how decisions are made and how deadlocks break, what it commits to its people, how its service periods stay one company, what it refuses, and what is still open and who closes it. It is read by the founders, by every lead, and by every new person before their first shift. It is not customer-facing; "The parent company is not a brand and is not named in customer-facing contexts" (WP p.36). Investors read the white paper. The white paper argues; the charter states.

### 5.2 Design rules for the whole document

**Position 5.1 (Recommended).** Seven design rules, each derived from a failure already visible in the record.

1. **Commitments and owners, never tools or addresses.** Both canon artifacts point to their record of truth at a ClickUp document that is now archived and excluded from this program; the canon also states the Surface-and-Zone Baseline Document lives there. The white paper names Airtable as the data-layer hub; Airtable is no longer in use. Two governing documents, written within the last year, already carry dead references. A founding document names the company's commitments and who owns them. It never names a tool, a vendor, a system of record, or the address of another document. Those live in the operating system, where they are expected to change.
2. **Every statement is settled, held, or deferred, and says which.** Canon's own discipline, "Every decision in this document is closed unless marked [HELD] or [DEFERRED]," extended with the four marks of this program. A held statement names who closes it: founder, chef partner, or team. A deferred statement names the condition that opens it.
3. **Versioned by decision, never by drift.** Canon's rule, adopted. The charter records its version, the date of each change, and who made it. It names, in its own text, who has authority to version it (position 3.3).
4. **Sŏn's voice rules apply.** No "we believe," no journey, no founder hero narrative (Canon 09). The forbidden lexicon applies. Sentence case. "Customer," never "guest," "in any internal communication" (Canon 01). No code names for service periods; time of day only. See section 6.4 for why this is an asset.
5. **No figures.** No percentage, threshold, or dollar amount appears in the charter. The compensation percentage is "still being finalized" (WP p.20) and the white paper carries "no ask, no terms, and no figures" (WP p.02). Figures live in the compensation architecture and the operating system, where they are versioned on their own cadence.
6. **Never assert the unproven.** "The discipline is to never assert the unproven, to a customer or to a reader" (WP p.36). The charter does not name future brands, the membership tier, or any concept that has not opened. It may state that the connective layer is built to hold more; it may not say what.
7. **Cites, never restates.** The charter cites canon by version and the operating agreement by title. It does not copy their content. A restated rule drifts from its source the day the source is versioned.

What the charter refuses, as a standing list in its own text: to describe tools; to name future brands; to carry code names; to carry figures; to restate canon or the operating agreement; to record a proposal as a decision; to describe any founder's interior qualities, strengths, or working style (those are the founders' own to write, Session 16).

### 5.3 Sections, in order

**Section 0: Standing.** *Purpose:* give the charter authority. *Must contain:* the relationship clause to the operating agreement and to canon (position 3.1), the seam rule once ruled (position 4.1), and the versioning authority (position 3.3). *Must do:* make it impossible for a reader to hold canon and charter as competing claims. *Must refuse:* to claim authority over anything the operating agreement governs. *Source:* structure from the team; seam rule and versioning authority from the founders. *If wrong or missing:* every downstream document picks its own answer to which governs; section 3.3 names the consequence.

**Section 1: What Sŏn is for (mission).** *Purpose:* her mission, in her sense: why the company exists, stated so that no other organization could hold it and so that it is never fully achieved. *Must contain:* one statement, and a note of which entity it belongs to (see 6.1). *Must do:* be true on opening day when only dinner runs, and still true when the layer holds more. *Must refuse:* to be a positioning line, a thesis about compounding, or a description of the restaurant alone. *Source:* Brandon, with the record as input (section 6.1). *If wrong or missing:* every daypart charter (section 11) and every team goal (Sessions 3 and 4) ladders up to nothing, and the onboarding sequence the white paper commits to has no "why" to deliver.

**Section 2: Long-term commitments.** *Purpose:* her long-term goals: the ambitions over years that quarterly and annual goals ladder up to. *Must contain:* a short list, stable across years, stated without figures. *Must do:* give Sessions 3 and 4 a parent for every metric and goal they set. *Must refuse:* to carry a target value, a date, or a future brand name. *Source:* Brandon, with the record's candidates in 6.2. *If wrong or missing:* the operating system sets goals with no parent, and the goals drift toward the lagging indicators the white paper explicitly rejects.

**Section 3: Company non-negotiables.** *Purpose:* the three refusals and the operating philosophy. *Must contain:* "No Exploitation Ever / No Toxicity Ever / No Disposable People. Employee-first operating philosophy" (Canon 01). *Must do:* state whether these outrank canon's tiers (position 4.1). *Must refuse:* to soften any of the three into an aspiration. *Source:* the record; the ranking from the founders. *If wrong or missing:* the company's clearest existing commitment has no operating home.

**Section 4: The protection order.** *Purpose:* state whether "bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results" is a tiebreaker or a description. *Must contain:* the sequence and, if ruled, the sentence that makes it a rule. *Must do:* tell a lead on a short-staffed night what wins. *Must refuse:* to be stated as a rule if the founders have not accepted its consequences. *Source:* record; ruling from the founders (position 4.2). *If wrong or missing:* the white paper's "mechanical, not aspirational" claim is unenforceable.

**Section 5: Principles.** *Purpose:* her principles: how the company operates, stated so they are "deliverable." *Must contain:* a short set, each stated as what the company does or refuses, each traceable to a decision already made or a moment already lived. *Must do:* be the thing hiring scores against (Sessions 5 and 6), feedback references (Session 12), and reviews measure (Session 13). *Must refuse:* virtue words; anything a competitor could claim unchanged; anything not expressed in how Sŏn hires, pays, or leads. *Source:* Brandon selects and words; the record supplies candidates (6.3); the chef partner may add one or more from the culinary domain (chef-gated); revision after the first year of operation is mandatory. *If wrong or missing:* every people session from 5 onward scores against an unstated standard.

**Section 6: The founding seats.** *Purpose:* three job descriptions for three principals. *Must contain:* per seat, the specification in section 7. *Must do:* resolve the OS-surface seam and name who owns culinary direction overall. *Must refuse:* to describe any founder's interior qualities; to describe the chef seat before position 9.1 is ruled. *Source:* record for the domains; each founder for their own seat; founders jointly for the seams. *If wrong or missing:* "Politics are a load-bearing structure problem" (WP p.11), and the record has already produced one seam.

**Section 7: Decision rights.** *Purpose:* the rule the record has been applying without writing down, and the deadlock rule. *Must contain:* the pattern in section 8, the classes of decision reserved to all partners, the deadlock rule, and the interim rule before the chef is seated. *Must do:* let any lead know, before the event, whether a decision is theirs. *Must refuse:* to list every decision; it states the rule and the reserved classes, and the operating system holds the instances. *Source:* pattern from the team; reserved classes and deadlock from the founders. *If wrong or missing:* the eight instances in the record stay instances, and the ninth case is judged during the event.

**Section 8: People and compensation commitments.** *Purpose:* the founding commitments to the people who work here, stated as a philosophy that reaches cases the record did not anticipate. *Must contain:* the list in section 10. *Must do:* let Session 5 write a job posting and Session 13 build an architecture that both satisfy the same commitments. *Must refuse:* any percentage, point weight, or premium. *Source:* record; Brandon confirms the list; Session 13 builds the mechanics. *If wrong or missing:* the compensation architecture is built with no stated philosophy to test against.

**Section 9: One company across the day.** *Purpose:* the commitments that keep four service periods one company. *Must contain:* the phased opening as a commitment (dinner first, alone for at least a quarter); each service period requires "its own reason to exist and its own operating logic" before it opens; lunch and brunch as held in reserve, with building lunch an equal decision among all three partners; the daypart charter (section 11) as the instrument by which a service period earns its opening. *Must do:* bind late night to decisions made while dinner ran alone, and give the lunch decision a form. *Must refuse:* code names; any statement that lunch will be built. *Source:* record; the team for the instrument; the chef partner for the culinary logic of each period (chef-gated). *If wrong or missing:* the failure the white paper diagnoses in its peer.

**Section 10: Structure.** *Purpose:* the organizational commitments the operating system implements. *Must contain:* code-bearing over load-bearing; "Authority lives in the domain. The person who owns the domain is accountable to its outcomes" (WP p.08); domain ownership "small, specific, explicitly defined, interlocking" (WP p.09); the two parallel leads, "Neither reports to the other" (WP p.10); "service authority distributed to the closest-to-customer position" (Canon 01). *Must do:* state the commitments, not the org chart. *Must refuse:* titles, headcounts, reporting lines; those are Sessions 3 and 8. *Source:* record. *If wrong or missing:* Session 8 designs structure against the white paper's argument rather than a stated commitment.

**Section 11: What the charter refuses.** The standing list from 5.2, in the document's own text.

**Section 12: Held and deferred.** *Purpose:* the honest register of what is open. *Must contain:* every held statement with who closes it and every deferred statement with what opens it. *Must do:* make the chef partner's inheritance legible on day one. *Must refuse:* to let anything held read as settled. *Source:* every section above. *If wrong or missing:* "a settled-looking answer to an open question is the tell of a deck that has not been pressure-tested" (WP p.22).

**Section 13: Versioning.** Version, date, author of each change, and the versioning authority.

**Position 5.2 (Recommended).** The section list above, in that order, is the charter's structure. The order matters: standing before content, commitments before structure, structure before the open register. Brandon may rename sections; the set and the sequence are the team's recommendation.

## 6. Mission, long-term commitments, and principles: structuring the decision

This section does not write any of the three. It states what each has to do, what makes one wrong, what Sŏn already holds, and exactly what Brandon supplies.

### 6.1 Mission

**What it has to do.** State why the company exists in one sentence that no other organization could hold and that is never fully achieved. Be true on opening day and true when the layer holds more. Be the thing every daypart charter and every quarterly goal ladders up to. Be deliverable in the onboarding sequence the white paper commits to.

**What makes one wrong.** Her first test: if another organization could hold it, it is a positioning line, not a mission. Her second: if it can be completed, it is a goal. Sŏn's own test, from the record: if it asserts the unproven, it is out. And a fourth, from this session: if it describes the restaurant alone, it misdescribes the company, because "The restaurant is the proof of concept. The connective layer is the asset" (WP p.34).

**The prior question: whose mission?** The canon header names Future Nostalgia Hospitality Group. The white paper says the parent "stays in the background" and "Sŏn is the only brand the customer meets at the building" (WP p.36). Her test says the parent and the restaurant cannot share one mission. A charter for the restaurant is a charter for the proof of concept. A charter for the company must have a mission that is true before the layer has held anything but Sŏn, without naming what it will hold. This is the hardest constraint on the sentence and it is Brandon's to resolve.

**Position 6.1 (Recommended).** The charter is the company's, not the restaurant's, and the mission belongs to the entity that owns the connective layer. Sŏn's service periods sit beneath it with daypart charters (section 11). This is a structural call the team has standing to make; the sentence itself is not.

**What Sŏn already holds that bears on it, quoted, tested honestly.**

- The positioning: "Korean restraint / Texas warmth / Polished but playful" (Canon 01). This is a positioning, a description of register. It passes her uniqueness test only weakly and fails as a mission because it says what Sŏn is like, not what it is for.
- The canon's own example of longer-form copy: "Most restaurants are pointed at the plate. We are pointed at the relationship. The plate is how we earn the right to have one" (Canon 09). This is the nearest thing in the record to a mission sentence. Tested: could another organization hold it? Yes; relationship-over-product is a claim many hospitality companies make. What would make it Sŏn's is what the record adds around it: one address, four service periods, Korean food, employee-first, a layer that carries the customer across their whole day. The sentence as written is register copy, not a decision.
- The white paper's thesis: "Three things can be built to compound... The organization, which compounds what the company knows. The customer, which compounds the relationship. The brand, which compounds earned attention" (WP p.06). This is an investment thesis. As a mission it is held by any company that read the same argument. It is the reasoning behind a mission, not the mission.
- The opening line of the concept: "A restaurant, read correctly, is not a room that serves food" (WP p.02). A negation. A mission cannot be a negation.

**Candidate reading, marked open and awaiting Brandon.** The record's material converges on a mission about the customer's relationship with one company across their day, carried by an organization that does not depend on any one person, through Korean food at one address in Austin. Accepting a mission of that shape would commit Sŏn to: measuring itself by the relationship and the organization rather than the plate; treating the plate as instrumental ("how we earn the right"); and holding the sentence true when the layer carries brands other than Sŏn. It would also commit the mission to a claim about Korean food specifically, or leave that to Sŏn's daypart charters and keep the company's mission cuisine-neutral. That fork is real and it is the kind of thing this candidate cannot decide.

**Position 6.2 (Founder-gated).** The mission sentence, the entity it belongs to, and whether it names the cuisine. Brandon supplies: the sentence; the decision on entity; the decision on whether Korean food is in the company's mission or Sŏn's. The team supplies the tests it must pass and the candidate above.

### 6.2 Long-term commitments

**What they have to do.** Be the ambitions over years that Sessions 3 and 4 hang metrics on. Be stable enough that they read the same after eight years, as Stripe's did. Carry no figure.

**What makes one wrong.** A target value makes it a goal, not a commitment. A future brand name asserts the unproven. A lagging indicator (revenue, labor percentage, food cost) contradicts the record: "these exist because they have always existed. All lagging indicators" (WP p.22).

**What Sŏn already holds.** The record's candidates, quoted:

- The three engines: an organization that compounds what it knows, a customer relationship that compounds, a brand that compounds earned attention (WP p.06).
- The leading indicators the white paper commits to measuring: "Employee NPS: would you recommend working here, tracked regularly rather than annually. Cultural labor score: beyond hours and cost, how the team is actually doing. Customer recognition rate: whether the building is actually recognizing and remembering customers" (WP p.22).
- The cultural measures: "retention rate, internal promotion rate, engagement results across psychological safety and values alignment, and participation in development" (WP p.23).
- The structural commitment: a company "built to run without them" (WP p.06).
- The coherence commitment: one company across the day, each service period with its own reason to exist (WP pp. 33 to 34).

These are unusually well-formed for long-term commitments because none carries a figure and each is already a stated intent. What is missing is selection and ownership: which of these are the company's long-term commitments, which are metrics that serve them (Session 3), and which are structural design (Session 8).

**Position 6.3 (Recommended).** Long-term commitments are stated in the charter without figures; every metric that measures them lives in the operating system (Session 3) and is set on the cadence (Session 4). The candidates above are the input list.

**Position 6.4 (Founder-gated).** The selection and wording of the long-term commitments. Brandon supplies which of the record's candidates are commitments and adds any the record does not hold.

### 6.3 Principles

**What they have to do.** Be "relevant, believable, enduring, and deliverable." Be expressed in how Sŏn hires, pays, leads, and conducts itself. Be the standard Sessions 5, 6, 12, and 13 score against.

**What makes one wrong.** Her warning applies with unusual force to a company that has not opened: principles "so idealistic that they don't resonate with employees because they have no connection to how the company actually operates." Sŏn has no operation yet. Her drafting method, "write down notable moments in the company's history," has no history to draw on. What Sŏn has instead is decisions.

**What Sŏn already holds.** The record is dense with principle-shaped statements, and they fall into registers that must not be confused:

- *Refusals.* "No Exploitation Ever / No Toxicity Ever / No Disposable People" (Canon 01). These are section 3 of the charter and are not principles in her sense; they are limits.
- *Operating tenets stated as mechanics.* "Authority lives in the domain" (WP p.08). "When something goes wrong, the first question is not who failed. It is what allowed this to happen... Management absorbs blame from staff. Post-mortems are private and process-focused" (WP p.17). "If it matters to the experience, Sŏn pays for it. Costs are not shifted to employees" (WP pp. 19 to 20). "There is no hierarchy of importance. Everything is treated as equally important" (WP p.20). "Advancement requires preparation, not permission" (WP p.16). "The discipline is to never assert the unproven" (WP p.36). "Employees are the users" (WP p.17). "Bought where the capability is settled, built where it is the edge" (WP p.21). These are principle candidates.
- *Cultural frameworks.* Jaeyeonmi, Ma, Mahk. These are canon tier 1 and govern the brand system. They are not company operating principles and the charter cites them rather than adopting them.
- *The playground test.* "Does this build or foreclose conditions for agency? If it forecloses agency without a documented reason, it violates the philosophy" (Canon 01). Stated for "customers, founders, and employees," so it crosses the canon-charter seam. A principle candidate.

**Position 6.5 (Recommended).** Sŏn's principles are drawn from decisions rather than from moments, because the company has decisions and no moments. Each principle in version one is traceable to a decision already in the record or a decision Brandon makes in ratifying the charter. Version one is marked pre-opening. A mandatory revision is scheduled after dinner has run alone for its first quarter, and again after the first full year, when the company has moments to test the principles against. That revision cadence is routed to Session 4.

**Position 6.6 (Founder-gated).** The set, the count, and the wording. Brandon supplies: which of the record's tenets are principles and which are operating rules that belong in Session 3; any principle the record does not hold; the wording of each. The team supplies the candidate list above and the register constraint in 6.4.

**Position 6.7 (Chef-gated).** Whether the culinary domain contributes a principle to the company set, distinct from Mahk, which governs the brand. Left for the chef partner.

### 6.4 Why Sŏn's voice rules are an asset here

Stripe's principles are largely virtues: Curious, Resilient, Humble, Macro-optimistic, Exothermic. They name a quality and then explain it. Sŏn's voice rules forbid that register outright. "No 'We believe,' no 'journey,' no founder hero narrative" (Canon 09). The forbidden lexicon rules out authentic, passion, thoughtful, intentional, community-driven, crafted, and most of the standard vocabulary of a values statement. Rule 1: "Copy never names its own effect." Binary test 5: "Could this sentence appear unchanged in a competitor's copy? Out."

That constraint does her work for her. Her failure mode is the principle that sounds good and does not connect to operation. Sŏn's grammar cannot produce that principle. A principle written under Sŏn's rules has to be a statement of what the company does or refuses, in subject-verb form, ending at its last load-bearing word, specific enough that no competitor could print it. "Management absorbs blame from staff" passes every one of her tests and every one of canon's. "We value humility" passes none. The company's voice rules are the deliverability test built into the sentence, and the charter is written under them without exception.

**Position 6.8 (Recommended).** The charter's mission, commitments, and principles are written under canon's voice rules and pass canon's six binary tests. A principle that fails test 5 is not a principle; it is a claim any restaurant could make.

Section 9 takes this up.

### 7.3 The specification for a founding seat description

**Position 7.1 (Recommended).** Each seat's description contains, in this order, and nothing else:

1. **Domain owned.** In the record's words, then in the founder's own.
2. **Outcomes the seat is accountable to.** "The person who owns the domain is accountable to its outcomes" (WP p.08). Stated as outcomes, not activities, and without figures.
3. **Decisions held alone.** The class of decisions this seat makes without consulting the other two.
4. **Decisions shared.** The classes reserved to all partners (section 8).
5. **What the seat does not own.** Explicit, because the seams live here.
6. **Seams.** Every object where this seat's domain meets another's, with the resolution or the mark that it is held.
7. **The seat's relationship to the two parallel leads and to the operating system.** How the seat's authority reaches the floor without becoming a reporting line.
8. **Absence rule.** How the domain runs when the seat is unavailable, because the company is "built to run without them" (WP p.06).

What the description refuses: interior qualities, strengths, failure modes, working style. Those are Session 16 material and each founder's own to write. A job description that describes a person rather than a seat is load-bearing in exactly the sense the white paper diagnoses.

**The chef partner.** Domain from the record: the culinary vision, and culinary execution during service. Not written until position 9.1 is ruled, because the seat's description depends on whether it is a partner seat or a hire.

## 8. Founder decision rights

### 8.1 The pattern the record has been applying

The prior carryover records that the record holds exactly one rule for a cross-domain decision. It holds more than that. The record assigns decision rights at least eight times, and never once states the rule it is applying:

1. **Lunch.** "an equal decision among all three partners, the chef partner included" (WP p.36).
2. **The sonic system.** Three named tiers: Brandon only for criteria and BPM ranges; management sign-off for dinner, late night, and morning exterior track additions; floor management for lunch context, interior morning staff-facing additions, and level adjustments (Canon 07, paraphrased to remove code names). "No streaming algorithm input at any tier."
3. **The floor.** "server owns the table... Floor manager owns the room... Routine recovery pre-authorized. Escalation triggers: financial recovery above threshold / kitchen failure affecting multiple sections / genuine emergency" (Canon 08).
4. **The David protocol.** "system decision, never floor call... Never improvised" (Canon 08).
5. **The design system.** "Governed exclusively by the Design Systems Architect. A request requiring a value outside the token system is an RFC, not a design decision" (Canon 12).
6. **Naming.** "Semantic [tier]: Three-party sign-off. Versioned on change" (Deck p.51).
7. **Content.** "Roughly eighty percent is green-light... Roughly fifteen percent is strategic review... Roughly five percent is leadership review" (WP p.31).
8. **Generosity.** "Staff can be generous without asking permission. Every server carries a bottle to gift as they see fit" (WP pp. 25 to 26); the server "resolves it using their authorized judgment and generosity range" (WP p.13).

The shape every one of them shares: **authority is assigned to the closest competent owner; the limits inside which that owner may act without asking are stated in advance; and the triggers that escalate are named before the event, not judged during it.** The white paper's structural argument says the same thing in the abstract: "Authority lives in the domain" (WP p.08), and "The path is explicit before they start it" (WP p.11). Sŏn has a decision-rights rule. It has never written it down, and so every new case is decided by analogy to the last one.

**Position 8.1 (Recommended).** The charter states the rule: authority sits with the closest competent owner; the owner's range is pre-stated; escalation triggers are named before the event. Every decision-rights instance in the operating system (Session 3) is written in that form: owner, range, triggers. An instance that lacks one of the three is incomplete.

### 8.2 What the rule does not settle

The rule assigns authority downward. It does not say what happens at the top, among three equal partners, when the closest competent owner is all three at once. The record's one stated case, lunch, is "an equal decision among all three partners." Three equal votes with no rule for disagreement is a deadlock waiting for its first instance. And until the chef is seated there are two partners, and two equal votes have no majority at all.

The classes of decision that plausibly belong to all partners, from the record: building or removing a service period; changing a company non-negotiable; versioning the charter; versioning canon at tier 1 or tier 2; admitting a new principal. The team can name those classes as candidates. It cannot set the voting rule, because the operating agreement may already hold one, and because a rule among principals is theirs to make.

**Position 8.2 (Founder-gated).** The deadlock rule among three partners: majority; unanimity for a named class and majority elsewhere; or domain-owner-decides after mandatory consultation. And the interim rule for two partners before the chef is seated. Requires Dominic. Filed as founder decision `86akh2qht`, which already exists; this session sharpens its content.

**Position 8.3 (Founder-gated).** The list of decision classes reserved to all partners. The team's candidate list is above; ratifying it is a founder decision.

## 9. The chef seat

### 9.1 The two accounts

The white paper says "chef partner," consistently, and means a founding seat: "the chef partner owns the culinary vision as an equal founding partner, freed from carrying the rest of the business alone" (WP p.03). The partner has an equal vote on whether a service period is built at all (WP p.36). The white paper is explicit that this is the point of the founding structure: "Sŏn simply hands the two domains outside the kitchen to founders who own them outright, which lets the chef partner own the food as a founding seat rather than carry the whole business" (WP p.05).

Canon says "Executive Chef," consistently, and means a hire. Held items are "PENDING EXECUTIVE CHEF HIRE" (Deck pp. 87 to 89). "The brand owns the culinary identity. The chef executes within it. Mahk standard is an explicit hiring criterion" (Canon, What Remains). The chef's "relationship to Bangjja Yugi, Baekja, Buncheong, and Cheongja" is "a hiring criterion" (Canon, What Remains). And at the architecture tier, a non-negotiable: "culinary identity belongs to the brand, not the chef" (Canon 02; Deck p.33).

### 9.2 Why this is sharper than a disagreement about ownership

The prior session recorded this as a conflict about who owns culinary identity. It is deeper than that. The two artifacts may not be describing the same seat.

A hiring criterion is an instrument applied to a candidate by an employer. It presupposes that the company exists before the person and sets the terms the person must meet. That is the right instrument for a hire. It cannot be applied to a founding partner on the same terms, because a founding partner is part of the company that sets the terms. You cannot recruit a partner with a rubric and then hand them an equal vote on the plan. You cannot hand a hire an equal vote on whether lunch exists.

The consequences run through everything this session touches:

- **Standing.** A partner has a voice in versioning the charter and canon. A hire works within both. Position 3.3 cannot be settled without knowing which.
- **Decision rights.** "An equal decision among all three partners" is coherent only if the third is a partner. Position 8.2 changes shape entirely if the third seat is a hire.
- **Compensation.** A partner sits in the operating agreement. A hire sits in the point system. The white paper's whole compensation argument is "to bring the culinary team into the upside it is usually walled out of" (WP p.20); the chef seat's relationship to that upside differs by an order of kind depending on the ruling.
- **The held items.** Canon holds tableware, centerpieces, culinary philosophy, and the arc in type pending the chef. If the chef is a partner, those are chef-gated founding decisions. If a hire, they are executional decisions made within a brand the chef did not shape. Canon's tier-1 frameworks "cannot change" (Deck p.89) either way; the question is whether a founding partner can be told that about the food.
- **Recruiting.** Sessions 5 and 6 cannot design the recruitment of the third seat until they know whether they are recruiting a principal or hiring an executive.

The white paper's own defense of the structure cuts both ways. "A restaurant built around a specific chef is a dependency, not a business" and "the business does not rest on the survival of any one person in it" (WP p.37). That argues for the brand owning identity. But the same page says "The culinary vision is real and it is led by the chef partner." Led by a partner, owned by the brand: the record wants both, and the charter has to say how both are true at once, or choose.

**Position 9.1 (Founder-gated).** Whether the third founding seat is a partner or a hire, and, if a partner, what "the brand owns the culinary identity" means when applied to a principal. Filed as founder decision `86akh2qrp`, unratified; this session adds the sharpened reading and the consequences above to it. Requires Dominic. Nothing in sections 7, 8, or 10 that touches the chef seat is settled until this is.

**Position 9.2 (Recommended).** Until 9.1 is ruled, the charter uses one term for the seat and marks it held. The two artifacts using two terms for what may be two different seats is itself a drift the charter must not inherit.

## 10. Compensation philosophy

### 10.1 What the record holds

Stated case by case, never as a philosophy:

- "The restaurant industry treats labor as a cost to minimize. Sŏn treats it as an asset to invest in. It is not charity, it is strategy" (WP p.16).
- "If it matters to the experience, Sŏn pays for it. Costs are not shifted to employees" (WP pp. 19 to 20).
- "A real employee area with secure lockers and a comfortable break space is not a perk. It is basic dignity, and dignity is a precondition for presence" (WP p.20).
- Compensation "built to bring the culinary team into the upside it is usually walled out of, and to do it without a charge added at the table. It is not a service charge, and it is never presented as one" (WP p.20).
- "distributed to the whole team, the culinary team included, on a point system weighted by the days each person works. It is calculated daily rather than quarterly and paid on a reasonable rhythm" (WP p.20).
- "there is no hierarchy of importance. Everything is treated as equally important" (WP p.20).
- Cross-strand fluency "recognized in compensation as meaningful" (WP p.11).
- Benefits planned from the start; the employee-first sequence, bandwidth before results.
- And the open item, named by the record itself: "Compensation architecture, how cross-strand fluency is recognized and paid, a separate deliverable" (WP p.15). "The compensation question is settled in principle, built into menu price rather than a tip or a service charge... the remaining open piece is the final percentage" (WP p.22).

### 10.2 The split, recommended and argued

The session brief asks whether compensation philosophy belongs in the charter or waits for Session 13. The answer is that the question conflates two things the record already separates. The record says the question is "settled in principle" and open in architecture. Those are different documents.

**Position 10.1 (Recommended).** The founding compensation commitments belong in the charter. The architecture belongs to Session 13. Specifically:

*In the charter (section 8):* labor as an asset invested in, not a cost minimized; the company pays for what matters to the experience and does not shift costs to employees; dignity as a precondition for presence; compensation built into price, never a tip, never a service charge, never presented as one; distributed to the whole team including the kitchen; no hierarchy of importance across roles; cross-strand fluency recognized in pay; benefits from the start; employees as the users.

*In Session 13:* the percentage; the point system and its weights; how daily calculation and the payment rhythm work; how cross-strand fluency is priced; how the architecture is versioned.

Three arguments for the split.

First, the commitments are commitments and the architecture is mechanics. A charter that carries mechanics dates the day the percentage is finalized, and design rule 5 forbids figures. A Session 13 architecture built without stated commitments has nothing to test itself against.

Second, sequencing. Sessions 5, 6, and 7 (recruiting, hiring, onboarding) run before Session 13. Every one of them needs to state what Sŏn commits to its people, in a job posting, an interview, and an orientation, without stating a number. The charter's section 8 is what they cite. Without it, Session 5 either invents the philosophy or waits for Session 13.

Third, the philosophy has to reach cases the record did not anticipate. The record's commitments are stated as instances (lockers, the break room, the bottle a server may gift). A charter states them as a philosophy so that when a new instance arrives (a piece of equipment, a training cost, a uniform item), the answer is derivable: if it matters to the experience, Sŏn pays for it. "There is never a need to adjudicate what deserves care, because the answer is always yes" (WP p.20). That sentence is already a philosophy; the charter is where it is stated as one.

**Position 10.2 (Founder-gated).** Confirmation of the commitment list above as the founding commitments, and any commitment the record does not hold. Brandon confirms; Dominic, as owner of the financial architecture, must see the list before Session 13 builds against it.

**What breaks if this stays open.** Session 5 writes recruiting materials with no stated compensation position, or with a position it invented. Session 13 builds an architecture and the commitments are reverse-engineered from it.

## 11. Team charters

### 11.1 Her instrument and its unit

Her team charter is a page for a team inside a division inside a company, and its trigger is explicit: "When you grow very quickly, or when your division is large, it can be surprisingly hard to figure out who is responsible for what work, and to what end." The charter answers that by stating the team's jobs to be done, "what others can expect from the team," metrics, risks, and interfaces.

### 11.2 Does the unit exist at Sŏn?

Sŏn has roughly eight leadership lines across the whole day. It has strands, domains, two parallel leads who serve the same center, and four service periods. It does not have teams inside divisions inside a company in her sense, and at eight leadership lines it will not develop them. Her trigger, headcount and division size, does not fire.

But her underlying question, "who is responsible for what work, and to what end," fires at Sŏn along a different axis. The record already answers "who is responsible" with domain ownership, "small, specific, explicitly defined, interlocking" (WP p.09), and the founding seat descriptions carry it at the top. A team charter for the kitchen or the floor would restate the domain owner's description at one remove. That is the case for not using the instrument at the team unit.

"To what end," though, is unanswered at exactly one unit the record itself names: the service period. The white paper's diagnosis of the failed peer is that a service period "without its own reason to exist and its own operating logic, becomes a worse version of dinner" (WP p.34). "Reason to exist" is her mission line. "Operating logic" is her charter. The record has already specified a charter-shaped document and named the unit: the daypart.

### 11.3 The daypart charter

**Position 11.1 (Recommended).** Sŏn does not use team charters at the team unit. Domain ownership and the founding seat descriptions do that work at eight leadership lines, and a team charter would duplicate them. Sŏn uses a **daypart charter**: one page per service period, and no service period opens without one.

**Position 11.2 (Recommended).** The daypart charter's structure, mapped from her template with the changes Sŏn's structure requires:

| Her section | Sŏn's daypart charter section | What it holds |
|---|---|---|
| Mission | Reason to exist | Why this service period exists as part of one company, one or two sentences, laddering to the company mission. Not a description of the food. |
| Vision | What it becomes | What the period is for the customer over years, without figures. |
| Customers | First customer | Which persona enters here first and what they cannot receive (from canon's persona profiles, cited by version). |
| Metrics | What it is measured by | Leading indicators only: recognition, return, employee measures. No revenue target in the charter. |
| Strategic importance | What it gives the layer | What this period contributes to the company that no other period does: the morning is the lowest-friction door and "is enrolled in the layer on the first visit" (WP p.36). |
| Major risks | What makes it a worse dinner | The specific ways this period could collapse into a copy of dinner. |
| Provided interfaces | What it hands on | What this period hands to other periods and to the layer: customer record, standing orders, staff fluency. |
| Dependent interfaces | What it draws from | What this period depends on: dinner's kitchen, the reservation record, the culinary logic the chef partner sets. |

Two sections her template does not have and Sŏn's needs: **operating logic** (how service, authority, and pace differ in this period from dinner, so the difference is designed rather than drifted into) and **held by** (what in this period's charter is chef-gated or team-filled).

**Position 11.3 (Recommended).** The daypart charter is the instrument by which a service period earns its opening, and it is the form the lunch decision takes. "Whether lunch is ever built at all is an equal decision among all three partners" (WP p.36). That decision currently has no form. A drafted lunch charter, tested against "does this period have its own reason to exist and its own operating logic," gives the three partners a document to rule on. If the charter cannot be written, that is the record's "no good answer for lunch," and "it comes out of the plan entirely." Drafting the charter does not assert lunch will be built; the charter carries the mark held until the partners rule.

**Position 11.4 (Chef-gated).** The operating logic and reason to exist of each service period, on the culinary side. The chef partner shapes what each period is in the kitchen.

**Position 11.5 (Team-filled).** Metrics, risks, and interfaces for each period, populated once the leads exist and dinner has run.

**What the daypart charter refuses.** Code names; revenue targets; any description that would read unchanged as a description of dinner; naming a future brand.

## 12. Instrument specifications

In her sense: do we use it, what it must contain, what it must do, what it must refuse. All instruments below are specifications. Building any of them is gated on the document methodology.

**Operating charter.** *Use:* yes; it is the founding document. *Contain:* sections 0 to 13 as specified in 5.3. *Do:* keep one company across four service periods, three domains, a phased opening, and a late-arriving seat; be the "why" onboarding delivers first. *Refuse:* tools, addresses, figures, code names, future brands, restated canon, interior qualities, proposals recorded as decisions.

**Mission statement.** *Use:* yes, as charter section 1. *Contain:* one sentence and its entity. *Do:* pass her two tests and Sŏn's two (never assert the unproven; not the restaurant alone). *Refuse:* positioning register; negation; a claim a competitor could hold.

**Long-term commitments.** *Use:* yes, as charter section 2. *Contain:* a short stable list. *Do:* parent every metric in Sessions 3 and 4. *Refuse:* figures, dates, lagging indicators, future brand names.

**Principles.** *Use:* yes, as charter section 5. *Contain:* a short set, each traceable to a decision. *Do:* be what hiring, feedback, and review score against; pass canon's six binary tests. *Refuse:* virtue words; anything not expressed in how Sŏn hires, pays, or leads; version one unmarked as pre-opening.

**Founding seat description.** *Use:* yes, three, as charter section 6. *Contain:* the eight elements in 7.3. *Do:* resolve every seam or mark it held; state the absence rule. *Refuse:* interior qualities, strengths, working style; the chef seat before 9.1 is ruled.

**Decision-rights rule.** *Use:* yes, as charter section 7. *Contain:* the rule (owner, range, triggers); reserved classes; deadlock rule; interim rule. *Do:* let any owner know before the event whether a decision is theirs. *Refuse:* to enumerate instances; those live in Session 3's operating system in the same three-part form.
