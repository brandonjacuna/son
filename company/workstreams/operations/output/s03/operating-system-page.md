## 1. What she asks for

Her operating system is "a set of norms and actions that are shared with everyone in the company" (Book p.84). It has eight components: founding documents, team charters, strategic and financial planning, resource allocation, annual and quarterly goals, metrics that matter, ownership, accountability mechanisms, internal communications, and operating cadence, with the first two and the last belonging to other sessions. Its purpose, in her words, is that "at every level, a good operating system makes clear the desired results, how and when to communicate progress, and how to measure achievement" (Book p.84), and above that, trust: "People grow uncomfortable when they're unsure what is expected of them" (Book p.84). The Google line she quotes is the whole thing in one sentence: "Every day, you all choose where to put your time. My goal is to give you the information to make the best decision" (Book p.84).

Her apparatus is built for a company scaling from a few hundred knowledge workers to thousands, and nearly every mechanism in it assumes a cascade of company, division, team, and individual, with a manager above each person and a leadership team allocating people and money between competing internal claimants. Sŏn is a code-bearing web at one address that opens near full size and compounds without adding headcount. This page takes her six middle components one at a time and, for every mechanism, says whether it survives intact, survives with its justification replaced, or does not survive, and why. Section 3 is the spine. Sections 5 through 11 are Sŏn's version of each component. Section 4 is the decision-rights rule the accountability material needs. Section 12 is the readiness test that replaces the retired prerequisites checklist. Section 14 specifies every instrument, hers and Sŏn's own, for the cluster that builds them later. Section 15 is the marks register.

## 2. What an operating system is at Sŏn, and when it is written

### 2.1 Her definition, her trigger, her analogy

Her trigger: "Once a company can no longer fit into a single meeting room, it's time to start writing down your operating system" (Book p.86). Before that, she says, it is too early, because "there's no point articulating why you exist if you're still trying to figure out whether you should exist at all." Her analogy: "If the founding documents are like a house's support structure, then the operating system is like a house's mechanical system" (Book p.84), the wiring and plumbing, with the operating cadence as the moving parts.

### 2.2 The analogy tested against a web

The record's Part I is an argument against reading an organization as a building. "The restaurant industry builds teams like buildings. Weight flows downward. Authority sits at the top" (WP p.07). "The organization is not built like a building. It is built like a body and engineered like a machine, and the structural principle is the same at every level" (WP p.14). Her analogy therefore fails at Sŏn on its face, and adopting it would import the frame the record rejects in its first pages.

What survives is the layering, not the house. She separates three things: what is settled once (foundation), what runs continuously (the mechanical system), and what moves on a clock (the moving parts). The record has its own vocabulary for exactly those three layers, and it is more precise than hers. The founding documents are the sequence: "The molecule does not hold weight. It holds information. The integrity of the sequence is the point" (WP p.08). The operating system is the regulation: what the organism senses, what it decides, who acts, what it measures, in the record's terms "what does the system know and how does that knowledge move" (WP p.07). The cadence is the rhythm, the record's "metabolic sequence" (WP p.12) given a clock. The stack is the nervous system, "what allows the organization to feel what a restaurant without it cannot feel, early enough to respond rather than repair" (WP p.12). It is the substrate the operating system's signals travel on. It is not the operating system.

This matters beyond vocabulary. In her house, the mechanical system is installed once and maintained. In a body, regulation is the thing that keeps the organism alive from moment to moment, and its failure modes are named: "CPU failure... Memory failure... Bus failure... Cooling failure" (WP p.13). An operating system at Sŏn is therefore not a set of documents the founders wrote. It is the running condition in which the shift brief happens, the shift close captures, the feedback loop closes, the competency path is visible, and the leads read the nervous system before the financials do. Section 12 tests for that running condition.

**Position 2.1 (Recommended).** Sŏn does not use the house analogy. Its operating system is defined in the record's own two lenses: the founding documents are the sequence, the operating system is the regulation, the cadence is the rhythm, and the stack is the nervous system the signals run on. The operating system is a running condition, not a document set, and it is tested as one.

### 2.3 Sŏn's trigger

Her trigger fires on headcount. Sŏn's does not, for three reasons the record supplies.

First, Sŏn's team never fits in one room, by design, from the first week. The business runs multiple service periods out of one room, and the people who run the early morning are not the people who run late night. The condition her trigger describes, founder proximity no longer doing the work, is Sŏn's starting condition rather than a stage it grows into. The record names the failure this produces when unaddressed: "Bus failure. Information cannot move between domains because the communication infrastructure is verbal handoffs, tribal knowledge, and informal relationships between individuals" (WP p.13).

Second, the record commits to outputs at the first hire that only an operating system can supply. The candidate at first interview "receives a single sheet with everything needed to decide: the org chart, their direct lead, compensation and benefits, communication expectations, the training plan with pay dates, the compensation mechanics, and the advancement path" (WP p.18). Onboarding delivers "orientation to the mission, the values, and the behavioral standard" before operational training (WP p.18). An org chart, a named lead, communication expectations, and an advancement path are operating-system artifacts. They must exist before the first person is hired.

Third, the opening is phased and the third founding seat arrives later. "Dinner opens first and runs on its own for at least a quarter, so the heart is solid before anything is added to it. The other core dayparts follow once dinner is steady" (WP p.36). Each added service period is run by people who did not run the first, and the chef partner inherits an operating company. The record's diagnosis of the failed peer is that a daypart "added as an afterthought, without its own reason to exist and its own operating logic, becomes a worse version of dinner" (WP p.34). An operating system written before dinner opens is what lets the morning arrive as "the same machine met at a different hour" (WP p.02) rather than a bolt-on.

Her "too early" caution has a real Sŏn form, but it attaches to service periods rather than to the company. Sŏn is "late to the cuisine and late to the category, deliberately" (WP p.35); the restaurant is not a product-market-fit experiment. What the building tests is the layer: "can the layer take a customer who entered in the morning and bring them to late night" (WP p.35). The company's operating system is therefore written now. The operating logic of each service period is written when its daypart charter is (S2, position 11.1), and lunch's is not written at all until the partners rule.

**Position 2.2 (Recommended).** Sŏn's trigger for articulating the operating system is the first hire, and the operating system must be running, per section 12, before dinner opens. The trigger is not headcount; it is that the team never co-occurs, that hiring and onboarding already promise operating-system outputs, and that the phased opening and the late-arriving seat require a system that people inherit rather than reconstruct.

**Position 2.3 (Recommended).** The operating system is written for the company once and consumed by each service period through its daypart charter. No operating logic is written for a service period without a ratified daypart charter, and none is written for lunch until the three-partner decision the record reserves (WP p.36) has been taken.

## 3. The reconciliation: code-bearing against her hierarchy

This is the hardest section and everything else hangs off it. Brandon's organizational thesis is that Sŏn is code-bearing: "A code-bearing organization asks: what does the system know and how does that knowledge move?" (WP p.07). Her operating system is written for a load-bearing organization: "who is holding the weight and who is beneath whom" (WP p.07). The two are not the same model. This section either reconciles them or names the departure, mechanism by mechanism. What it does not do is adopt her structure while keeping his language.

### 3.1 Her five assumptions

Her apparatus assumes, at nearly every point:

1. **A cascade.** Company, division, team, individual. "These systems can then replicate down through divisions and teams to help clarify priorities and resolve dependencies" (Book p.84). "It's ideal for company goals to replicate down from the division to the team to the individual" (Book p.103).
2. **Goals that replicate down the cascade** and metrics that "replicate down from the company level to divisions, teams, and sometimes even to individuals" (Book p.115).
3. **A manager above each person** who assigns ownership ("Assigning ownership is an important aspect of management," Book p.121), reviews progress, and escalates or unblocks.
4. **A leadership team that allocates resources between competing internal claimants.** "Headcount planning is fraught, and leaders will tend to read into the allocations and lobby for more" (Book p.99). Her founder "was left feeling like the sole decision-maker, and often like the 'bad guy'" (Book p.100).
5. **A communications function that pushes context down a hierarchy** and a company-wide meeting as the primary forum, with the internal home page as its archive.

### 3.2 What Sŏn holds against each

**Against the cascade: the web.** "No top. No bottom. The web has a center and an outside edge. Neither is superior. They are functionally different" (WP p.09). "Growth is outward, not upward... The web never acquires altitude, only reach" (WP p.09). Below the two leads, "the web is composed of strands, not levels" (WP p.10). The record's units are the company, the domain ("Authority lives in the domain," WP p.07), the strand, the person, and, from Part V and Session 2, the service period, which is the one unit at which "reason to exist" and "operating logic" are answered (S2, section 11.2). There is no division and there is no team in her sense. Her cascade does not exist at Sŏn and this page does not construct one.

The cascade does one piece of work Sŏn still needs: a "conceptual throughline, allowing people from across the company to speak the same language" (Book p.85). At Sŏn that throughline runs through a lattice rather than a chain. Every goal at every unit names the long-term commitment it serves (S2, position 6.3). The direction is upward laddering from the unit that holds the information to the commitment it serves, not downward replication from a leadership team to the units beneath it. The white paper's web says why the direction matters: the edge is "the most sensitive position. They feel the customer, the room, and the cultural temperature of the building before anyone else" (WP p.09). A goal set at the center and replicated to the edge is set by the node with the least information about the thing the goal is about.

**Against the manager above each person: domain ownership and the system as advocate.** "The person who owns the domain is accountable to its outcomes" (WP p.08). The center is "a systems function, not a rank" (WP p.09). Functional designations "mark accountability in the moment, in the domain. They are not permanent elevations above anyone" (WP p.10). Review runs through the system: "Advancement is tied to demonstrated mastery: when a person is ready, they schedule a competency conversation with the appropriate lead" (WP p.19), and "No one's advancement waits on a manager's attention or judgment. The system is the advocate" (WP p.11). Escalation runs through pre-named triggers: "Escalation triggers: financial recovery above threshold / kitchen failure affecting multiple sections / genuine emergency" (Canon 1.0, section 08). The manager as assigner, reviewer, and escalation point does not survive. What replaces it is a domain owner who is accountable to outcomes and is the counterpart for competency sign-off and development review, a system that surfaces advancement without discretion, and triggers that were written before the event.

The record also warns, in the ledger's words, that stability is a property of the system. "Node overload: one domain owner is absorbing load that belongs to adjacent domains, usually a manager doing too much operational work because the parallel structure has blurred. The fix is restoring the Operations Lead and Maitre d separation" (WP p.14). Her accountability chapter assumes the manager is the stabilizer throughout: the one who "unblocks," who "course-corrects," who is "responsible for tracking progress and escalating." At Sŏn, a lead who is doing that by hand is the diagnosis, not the remedy.

**Against the leadership team allocating between claimants: two co-leads serving one center, and no headcount to allocate.** "These roles are parallel. Neither reports to the other. They are co-leads of different systems serving the same center, the culinary and conceptual vision" (WP p.10). Sŏn opens near full size and "does not add people to add capacity" (the brief's framing, consistent with WP p.22's leadership delta). There is no annual contest for headcount between floor and kitchen because the structure is fixed by design and each domain owns its outcomes. Her scarce resources, people and money, are replaced by a different scarce resource, section 6 names it, and her allocation mechanism is replaced by the phased opening and the protection order. Her "bad guy" founder case does recur at Sŏn in one place: decisions among equal partners, which section 4 takes up.

**Against communications pushed down: lateral information on a nervous system.** "Information moving laterally without routing through a chain of command. This is not a management achievement. It is the structure functioning correctly" (WP p.09). The record already names the mechanisms: the shift brief (WP p.14), the shift close (WP p.13), "a dedicated channel that syncs to the execution system, so employees can surface issues, suggest improvements, and see their input lead to action" (WP p.22), and feedback that "runs both directions" (WP p.19). Communication at Sŏn is bidirectional and system-carried; the founders are not the hub and the edge is the primary sender, not only the primary receiver. What survives from her component is the set of tests she applies to any communications program, because tests are not structure.

**Against the team as a unit: the service period.** Her unit for charters, goals, QBRs, weekly meetings, and snippets is the team. Sŏn's nearest unit is the service period, which has "its own reason to exist and its own operating logic" (WP p.34) and, from Session 2, a charter of its own. But a service period is not a team in her sense either: the same kitchen serves every period, the same Maitre d holds the floor across periods, and a server may work more than one. The service period is the unit at which goals, metrics, and review are organized; the domain is the unit at which ownership is held; the person is the unit at which development is held. Sŏn has three goal-bearing units below the company where she has three levels. The difference is that hers nest and Sŏn's intersect.

### 3.4 The departure, stated plainly

Sŏn does not adopt her hierarchy. It adopts most of her tests and several of her mechanisms, each re-justified from the record. The departure is exact and it is this: at Sŏn, **the unit that holds the information sets the goal, holds the metric, and owns the decision inside a pre-stated range; the center integrates and names the commitments; nothing is assigned, reviewed, or escalated by a person standing above another person.** Her mechanisms that assume the reverse do not survive. Her mechanisms that are indifferent to direction, the tests, the definitions, the cadences, the named-owner rule, survive and are often stricter at Sŏn than at Stripe, because the record treats undefined ownership and undocumented paths as structural failures rather than management lapses.

Two honest caveats. First, Sŏn is not self-managed in the sense the profile's sources describe. The founders own their domains outright and set the company's commitments; the leads own theirs. Authority is real and it is located, in the domain. What is absent is altitude, not authority. Second, the web is a claim about how Sŏn is built, and several of its mechanisms are not yet running because the building has not opened. The profile's rule applies: "a diagram is not a system." Section 12 is how the claim gets tested.

**Position 3.1 (Recommended).** Sŏn's operating system is built on the record's web, not on her cascade. Goals ladder up from the unit that holds the information to a long-term commitment; ownership lives in the domain and, per service, in the shift brief's designations; escalation runs on pre-named triggers; communication is lateral and system-carried. Each of her mechanisms is placed per the table in 3.3, and the reasons are given in sections 5 through 11.

**Position 3.2 (Recommended).** The goal-bearing units at Sŏn are four: the company, the service period, the domain, and the person. They intersect rather than nest. Her division and team units are not used.

**Position 3.3 (Recommended).** A lead who produces stability by hand, holding the schedule in their head or acting as the integration layer between floor and kitchen, is read as node overload and the fix is to restore the system, not to praise the lead. Every accountability mechanism on this page is designed so that it runs without a lead's memory.

## 4. Decision rights

Her accountability material assumes a decision-rights structure and never states one, because in a hierarchy the structure is the chart: the manager decides, and the manager's manager decides what the manager may decide. Sŏn has no chart of that kind, so it needs the rule written. The record assigns decision rights at least eight times and never states the rule it is applying. Session 2 found the shape (S2, position 8.1). This section quotes each instance, rewrites it in the three-part form, names what the register does not yet cover, and argues the interim two-partner case to the ceiling one founder can reach.

### 4.1 The rule

**Authority sits with the closest competent owner; the range within which that owner acts without asking is stated in advance; the triggers that escalate are named before the event rather than judged during it.** The record's structural argument says the same thing in the abstract: "Authority lives in the domain. The person who owns the domain is accountable to its outcomes" (WP p.07 to 08). Canon's playground philosophy requires "service authority distributed to the closest-to-customer position" (Canon 1.0, section 01). The deck states the trigger half exactly: "Recovery is pre-authorized inside known limits. Escalation triggers are defined before service, not improvised at the table" (Deck 3.0, p.79).

Three operating rules follow from the form.

1. A trigger names its destination. "Escalate" is not a destination. An entry whose trigger does not say who receives the escalation is incomplete.
2. A range states what the owner may not do as well as what they may. The range is the enabling constraint in the profile's sense: it gives coherence and lets the owner use judgment inside it, and it holds when no lead is present.
3. Ranges are set one level out from the position that holds them: the domain owner sets ranges for positions inside the domain; the founders set the two leads' ranges; the partners set each other's, which is the case section 4.4 takes up. A range with a financial value in it is set with Dominic's domain and the value is not carried on this page.

**Position 4.1 (Recommended).** The rule above is the operating system's decision-rights rule, in the three-part form, with the three operating rules attached. Every decision-rights entry on this page and in every later page is written as owner, range, triggers-with-destination. An entry missing a part is marked incomplete in the register rather than filled by analogy to the last case.

### 4.2 The eight instances, quoted and rewritten

Each instance is quoted from the record first, then rewritten. Where the record leaves a part missing, the gap is stated and the entry is marked incomplete.

**1. Whether lunch is ever built.**

Quoted: "Lunch and brunch sit further back still, not to be revisited until the restaurant has operated for a full year, and whether lunch is ever built at all is an equal decision among all three partners, the chef partner included. If there is no good answer for lunch, it comes out of the plan entirely. That is a real option, not a courtesy" (WP p.36).

Rewritten. *Owner:* all three partners equally; no closer owner exists because the decision crosses every domain. *Range:* no partner builds, schedules, or removes lunch alone; the decision is not opened before the restaurant has operated a full year; the form the decision takes is a drafted lunch daypart charter (S2, position 11.3). *Triggers:* the one-year mark opens the decision; nothing opens it earlier; a lunch charter that cannot be written is the record's "no good answer" and removes lunch from the plan. *Gaps:* no rule for disagreement among three; no possibility of the decision at all while there are two partners. Incomplete on the deadlock part; section 4.4.

**2. Sonic governance.**

Quoted: "Governance: Tier 1 (Brandon only), criteria, BPM ranges / Tier 2 (management sign-off), dinner, late night, morning exterior track additions / Tier 3 (floor management), [lunch] context, interior morning staff-facing, level adjustments. No streaming algorithm input at any tier. Pre-opening full review is non-negotiable" (Canon 1.0, section 07). The deck states the same three tiers: "Tier 1: Brandon only. Tier 2: Management sign-off. Tier 3: Floor management. No streaming algorithm input at any tier" (Deck 3.0, p.75). Transitions are "person-triggered transitions only. No automated timers" (Canon 1.0, section 07), and the late-night transition is "the same trigger as lighting and sonic, same person, same moment" (Canon 1.0, section 08).

Rewritten. *Owner:* Brandon for curation criteria and tempo ranges; "management" for track additions in the dinner, late night, and morning exterior contexts; floor management for the lunch context, interior morning staff-facing content, and level adjustments. *Range:* floor management adjusts levels and adds tracks in its contexts without asking, inside the four curation questions; management adds tracks in its contexts inside Brandon's criteria; no tier admits algorithmic input; no tier changes criteria or tempo ranges. *Triggers:* a track that fails any of the four curation questions moves up a tier; any change to criteria or ranges is Brandon's alone; the pre-opening full review is a gate, not a review. *Gaps:* "management" is unnamed under two parallel leads who serve the same center; the person who triggers the pre-service scene and the late-night transition is unnamed in both canon versions. Incomplete on the owner of the transition trigger. This page recommends that the owner of the room owns the trigger (section 9.2), but the room's owner is itself a conflict in the record (section 13.3).

**3. Floor authority.**

Quoted: "Floor authority: server owns the table, attunement calls, approach timing, whether a dish gets a spoken note or arrives in silence. Floor manager owns the room, coverage, pattern recognition, backs up without intercepting. Routine recovery pre-authorized. Escalation triggers: financial recovery above threshold / kitchen failure affecting multiple sections / genuine emergency" (Canon 1.0, section 08). "Recovery is pre-authorized inside known limits. Escalation triggers are defined before service, not improvised at the table" (Deck 3.0, p.79).

Rewritten. *Owner:* the server for the table; the floor manager for the room. *Range:* the server calls attunement, approach timing, and the spoken note or silence, and executes routine recovery inside known limits; the floor manager holds coverage and pattern recognition and backs up without intercepting, which means the floor manager does not take a table from a server inside the server's range. *Triggers:* financial recovery above threshold escalates from server to floor manager; kitchen failure affecting multiple sections escalates from floor manager to the kitchen's owner of execution during service, the Chef Partner (WP p.10), with the Operations Lead informed; genuine emergency escalates to whoever holds the building's emergency authority that service. *Gaps:* "threshold" carries no value and none is set here; "known limits" are not written; the destination of the floor manager's own escalation is unstated in canon and is supplied above as a recommendation; who holds emergency authority per service is unstated. Incomplete on threshold and on emergency authority.

**4. The David protocol.**

### 4.3 What the register does not yet cover

The rule assigns authority to the closest competent owner and it works wherever a closest owner exists. It does not yet cover five classes, and the register names them rather than leaving them to be decided at the moment they first occur.

**Class A: decisions reserved to all partners.** Session 2's candidate list: building or removing a service period; changing a company non-negotiable; versioning the charter; versioning canon at tier 1 or tier 2; admitting a new principal (S2, position 8.3). Ratifying the list is a founder decision (S2, position 8.3). *Founder-gated.*

**Class B: a domain decision with consequences in another domain.** The record has no rule. This is a recommendation and it can be ratified without the chef seated, because the kitchen's owner of execution during service already exists as a functional designation (WP p.10). *Recommended.*

**Class C: lead-to-lead disagreement.** The Operations Lead and the Maitre d are parallel; "neither reports to the other" (WP p.10). When they disagree inside their shared surface, the schedule against the floor's coverage being the standing case, there is no one above them. The team's recommended destination follows Class B: the founder whose domain holds the disputed outcome. The Operations Lead's outcomes ("labor efficiency, cost targets, compliance, vendor performance, and facility integrity," WP p.10) sit partly in Dominic's domain, the financial architecture, and partly in Brandon's, the operating systems. The Maitre d's outcomes ("experience quality and team health," WP p.10) sit in Brandon's. A disagreement about experience escalates to Brandon's seat; a disagreement about cost escalates to Dominic's; a disagreement about both is a partner decision. *Recommended.*

**Class D: emergency and closure.** Who closes the room, or a service period, for weather, a safety event, a stack outage, or a kitchen failure, and who holds emergency authority during a service. The record holds "genuine emergency" as an escalation trigger and names no destination. The team's recommendation: the floor manager holds emergency authority for the room during service, the Chef Partner for the kitchen, and either may close their side without asking; closing the building for a service period is the Operations Lead's call with the Maitre d consulted; closing for longer than one service period is a founder decision. *Recommended.*

**Class E: changes to the stack.** Adding, removing, or reconfiguring a tool that the leads and the floor depend on. This is Dominic's domain outright ("the technology build," WP p.03), and "employees are the users" (WP p.17) creates an obligation the rule must honor: the structured feedback channel is the consultation path, and no stack change that alters what a lead or a server does during service lands without the affected domain consulted before the event. The OS surface's ownership is separately founder-gated (S2, section 7.2). *Recommended, with the OS surface held.*

**Position 4.3 (Founder-gated).** The reserved-class list (Class A), extended by this session's three candidates. Brandon and Dominic ratify; the operating agreement may already bear on it and must be read first (S2 founder decision on the operating agreement).

**Position 4.4 (Recommended).** The rules for Classes B, C, D, and E as written above.

### 4.4 The interim two-partner case, argued to the ceiling

The record's one stated cross-domain rule is three-partner. Until the chef is seated there are two partners and two equal votes have no majority. Her founder "left feeling like the sole decision-maker, and often like the 'bad guy'" (Book p.100) is this case, except that at Sŏn there is no sole decision-maker at all. An interim rule is needed now, before the first Class A decision arrives, and the first one will arrive before the chef does: the charter must be versioned to be ratified.

The team can argue this to a ceiling. It cannot land it, because a rule among principals is theirs, and because the operating agreement may already hold a voting rule the team has not read.

The recommended interim, in three parts.

1. **Domain decides, after consultation, for everything outside Class A.** For every decision that is not reserved, the domain owner decides under Classes B through E, with the other partner consulted before the event where the consequence crosses into their domain. This is most decisions and it needs no vote.
2. **Both or neither, for Class A.** With two partners, a reserved decision requires both. A disagreement is not a deadlock; it is a hold, and the default is the status quo. Nothing in a reserved class changes until both agree. This is the only rule that does not manufacture a tiebreaker the record never granted, and it is safe because every reserved class is a change to something already settled: a service period, a non-negotiable, a document version, a principal. The cost of a hold is delay; the cost of a manufactured tiebreaker is one partner overruling the other on the things the record says they hold equally.
3. **The lunch decision is frozen, not held.** The record makes it three-partner "the chef partner included" (WP p.36), and the record's own timing, not before a full year of operation, means it will not fall due before the seat is filled if the seat is filled on the record's schedule. If it does fall due first, the two partners do not take it. Lunch cannot be built or removed by two.

What this leaves open, and only the partners can close: whether "both or neither" survives into the three-partner case as unanimity for Class A and majority elsewhere, or whether three partners use majority throughout; whether any external tiebreaker exists in the operating agreement; and whether the chef partner's vote on Class A begins on seating or after a stated period. Session 2's founder decision on the deadlock rule already exists; this section sharpens its content and adds the interim.

**Position 4.5 (Founder-gated).** The interim two-partner rule as argued above: domain decides after consultation outside Class A; both or neither inside Class A with the status quo as default; lunch frozen until three partners exist. Requires Dominic. The three-partner deadlock rule remains open under Session 2's founder decision.

## 5. Strategic and financial planning

### 5.1 "A strategy should hurt"

"The trade-offs, where you invest time and resources, and where you don't, should be painful and disappointing, either internally or to your customers. There's no such thing as a strong strategy that prioritizes everything at once" (Book p.88 to 89). Her test is whether the strategy has made choices that cost something.

Sŏn's strategy is already in the record and it already hurts. The trade-offs, each quoted so the pain is visible:

- Dinner runs alone for at least a quarter before any other service period opens (WP p.36). Every hour the room sits dark in that quarter is foregone revenue chosen on purpose.
- Lunch may never be built. "If there is no good answer for lunch, it comes out of the plan entirely. That is a real option, not a courtesy" (WP p.36).
- No tip and no service charge. Compensation is "built into the menu price itself, on the backend" (WP p.20), which raises the printed price against every peer that hides the same cost on the check.
- No marketing spend. "This part rejects that word" (WP p.28). Attention is earned by documenting the build, which means documenting setbacks: "Setbacks are the strongest content" (WP p.29).
- No espresso machine on the front bar, so the room reads as a dining room at dinner (WP p.04 to 05).
- No host stand (Canon 1.0, section 08). No signage but one restroom identifier; "the building does not announce itself as a restaurant" (Canon 1.0, section 06).
- No review solicitation and no rating scale on any post-visit surface (Canon 1.0, section 12).
- No future brand is named. "The discipline is to never assert the unproven, to a customer or to a reader" (WP p.36). The horizon is "gated on the proof, never a promise made before the building earns it" (WP p.37).
- Servers do not close-clean; an overnight crew does, "at the same cost," so server labor goes to the thank-you note (WP p.26).
- Comps are "a line item to be fully used, not minimized" (WP p.26).

Her test passes. What the record does not do is hold these in one place as the strategy's stated costs, which is what makes them re-decidable at a different hour by a person who arrived later. Session 2 assigns the long-term commitments to the charter (S2, position 6.3). The costs of the strategy belong beside them in the operating system, where the leads read them.

**Position 5.1 (Recommended).** The operating system carries the strategy's stated trade-offs as a single list, each with its source in the record, so that a proposal to reverse one (add a service charge, put an espresso machine on the bar, buy a placement, name a future concept) is recognized as a strategy change under Class A or Class B of section 4.3 rather than an operating adjustment.

### 5.2 The horizons

Two things differ from hers. First, the horizons are not worked on simultaneously. Horizon 2 opens "once dinner is steady" (WP p.36) and not before; Horizon 3's lunch decision is not opened "until the restaurant has operated for a full year." The sequencing is gated on proof rather than run in parallel. Second, the thing that connects the horizons is the layer, and the layer is being tested from day one inside Horizon 1: "The test is exact and it runs with real customers from day one: can the layer take a customer who entered in the morning and bring them to late night" (WP p.35). **Position 5.2 (Recommended).** Sŏn's three horizons are dinner; then early morning and late night in the record's sequence; then the lunch decision and the platform ceiling. ## 6. Resource allocation

### 6.1 What is scarce at Sŏn

"In simple terms, your primary resources are people and money" (Book p.93). At Sŏn, people are near-fixed by design: the house opens near full size, the leadership structure is set by the record, and capacity is not added by adding people. Money is Dominic's domain and not a context source. If her two resources are taken as Sŏn's, the component has nothing to allocate.

The record names what is actually scarce, and it names it in Part I: "Bandwidth is the organizational metabolic rate. It is what the system has available after the work of survival is complete. An organization without bandwidth is one where every person is operating at capacity maintaining what exists. No one has room to feel what is happening, to respond to what is arriving, to improve what is working" (WP p.12). Bandwidth is the resource that a second service period consumes, that the content engine consumes, that a departure consumes ("every adjacent thread compensates at cost," WP p.09), and that a stack outage consumes. It is also the resource the record ranks first: "Bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results. This is the metabolic sequence" (WP p.12), and the deck adds "the order is the decision" (Deck 3.0, p.04).

Three further scarcities sit beside it. The room's character: "Dinner is what everything protects" (WP p.33), and "experiencing the same dining room at every hour would cost dinner its magic" (WP p.33), so every added service period spends some of dinner's protection and must be designed not to. The day's hours: one room, one kitchen, and transitions between periods that are "person-triggered" and take real time (Canon 1.0, sections 06 to 08). And founder attention, which the record commits in three directions at once: the floor ("the founders' presence on the floor runs on the service metronome," context ledger), the content engine ("fifteen to twenty pieces a day is achievable without burning the founders out," WP p.30), and the system build.

**Position 6.1 (Recommended).** Sŏn's resource-allocation component allocates bandwidth, dinner's protection, the day's hours, and founder attention, across service periods and across the build. It does not allocate headcount, because there is none to allocate, and it does not allocate money on this page, because financials are not a context source.

### 6.2 The allocation rule

Her rule is the fixed-ratio split across the three horizons, and the Stripe alternative, "existential risks > core product > new products," followed by a reserve "for foundational company work" (Book p.101). Both are ordering rules with a ratio attached. Sŏn adopts the ordering and carries no ratio.

The ordering is already in the record. Dinner's protection is first. The phased opening is the allocation of the day's hours and of bandwidth across periods: dinner alone, then early morning, then late night, each added only when the prior is steady. The protection order, bandwidth before results, is the tiebreaker when a period's results argue for spending bandwidth the house does not have: under "the order is the decision," covers come down before the floor is thinned, and a service period that consumes the team's bandwidth is restructured before it is defended on its results. Session 2 filed the question of whether that order is a rule or a description as a founder decision, and this component depends on it.

**Position 6.2 (Recommended).** Sŏn's allocation rule is an ordering: dinner's protection; then bandwidth, joy, retention, results in that sequence; then the phased opening's sequence of periods. No ratio is carried. A proposal to add a period, a service, or a content commitment is tested first against whether the house has the bandwidth to absorb it, and the order decides when results and bandwidth conflict.

**Position 6.3 (Founder-gated).** Whether "the order is the decision" is a binding tiebreaker in allocation. This position inherits Session 2's founder decision and adds the allocation consequence stated above so that the decision is taken with its cost visible.

### 6.3 The leadership delta, tested rather than repeated

The record's claim: "The result is roughly eight leadership lines running the full span of the day against fourteen running one dinner service, and a kitchen of two title-tiers instead of four. That is the code-bearing claim from Part I, measured against the best" (WP p.22). And in Part V: "The leadership delta in Part II is the proof that the code-bearing organization carries that coordination without the overhead" (WP p.34).

Her own critique of ratio-based allocation applies to this claim directly: "other companies' benchmarks don't reflect your own company and business model" (Book p.93). Eight against fourteen is a benchmark comparison, and the eight run more hours than the fourteen. The profile's rule is stricter: "Before I call the eight-line leadership structure sound or lean, name the disturbance classes it must absorb across four dayparts" and do not verdict "until the classes are on the table." The requisite-variety test says a regulator must hold at least the variety of what it regulates, and the record's argument for why eight can hold what fourteen held is attenuation: "Most of those positions are not leadership. They are manual integration work that software now does" (WP p.22). That is a legitimate way to reduce the variety a lead must absorb. It holds only while the stack is actually absorbing it, which is why "sequence corruption" (WP p.14) is the failure mode that would expose the structure as under-variety'd.

The disturbance classes the house must absorb, named so the test can be run. No count is attached to any of them; the counts are a gap.

1. **Customer-side.** Persona mix per service (canon's four and everyone else); occasions that must be read without being announced (Marcus); a known customer whose record did not travel (Clara); an unflagged David; dietary and allergy information; recognition failures; a table that needs recovery.
2. **Kitchen-side.** A station failure affecting multiple sections; live-fire variability; a supply or delivery that does not arrive ("a package-tracking layer that watches every incoming order across carriers and removes the daily chase," WP p.22, is the record's attenuator); prep that runs short at a period boundary because the same kitchen serves every period.
3. **Floor-side.** No-shows and walk-ins; table-state timing, including the last turn table that the late-night transition "moves toward" (Canon 1.0, section 08); the person-triggered transitions between periods; the pyeong-sang's pre-designed service running beside the responsive room.
4. **People-side.** Call-outs; tension slack; a node leaving mid-quarter; cooling failure in a period; the ready-now pool being empty when a position opens.
5. **System-side.** A stack outage, given that "the point-of-sale spine" is an open decision (WP p.22); a sensor platform failure; the camera AI layer failing; the record not surfacing preferences at the brief.
6. **Transitions.** Morning window to pre-service scene to dinner to late night, one room reconfiguring, "same person, same moment" for lighting, sound, and service (Canon 1.0, section 08), with the trigger's owner unnamed (section 4.2, instance 2).
7. **External.** Weather on a patio larger than the interior (Canon 1.0, section 06); the sound ordinance compliance the acoustic specification is designed to meet "through speaker directivity and placement, not volume limiting" (Canon 1.0, section 07); regulatory timelines the record itself lists as build content.
8. **Brand-side.** A crisis in public; a documented setback that has to be told honestly at the speed the content engine runs; a review that names the compensation model.

The test: for each class, name the regulator that absorbs it, the stack, a pre-stated range, a lead, or a founder, and check that no class lands on a lead's memory by default. Where a class resolves to a founder by default, the structure is under-variety'd for that class and the fix is a range or a system function, not a founder on the floor. Where a class resolves to nothing, the structure has a hole. The test is run with the actual leadership-line list and, after dinner's first quarter, the actual incident log from the shift close.

**Position 6.4 (Recommended).** The leadership delta is held as a claim to be tested, not a proof to be repeated. The test is the requisite-variety read above: eight disturbance classes, each mapped to its regulator, run against the actual leadership-line list before opening and against the shift-close log after the first quarter. The line count and its load are a stated gap on this page.

**Position 6.5 (Team-filled).** The results of the test: which classes resolve to the stack, which to a range, which to a lead, and which to nothing. Populated once the leads exist and once dinner has run.

### 6.4 The reserve: existential risk and the work no one prioritizes

Her side note survives intact and matters: "A key role of your leadership team should be to invest in critical work that no one else will naturally step up to prioritize. It's rare that a company's plans, incentives, and metrics structures are built to mitigate risk or stop and redo work" (Book p.101). Eric Yuan's line beside it: "My number one priority is to think about the risk factors" (Book p.102).

The record names Sŏn's existential risks, though not as a list. The chef dependency: "A restaurant built around a specific chef is a dependency, not a business" (WP p.37). Undercapitalization and weak controls: "The most common cause of restaurant failure is not the food. It is the controllable set" (WP p.05). Food safety, which the defensive stack is built to prevent (WP p.21). Key-person loss, answered structurally by the code-bearing claim. The compensation model's acceptance by customers. Sound, as an Austin-specific failure. The stack itself: "Software breaks: true, which is why the layer is heavily deterministic" (WP p.22). And the one the record does not name and this page adds: the founders' own bandwidth across the floor, the build, and the content engine.

The work no one will naturally prioritize at Sŏn is system maintenance. The failure-mode reference says so four times: for sequence corruption "the fix is system maintenance, not people management"; for skill invisibility "the fix is documentation and system access"; for cooling failure "the fix is removing friction from the work" (WP p.14). None of that work produces a cover or a plate, and all of it is what keeps the eight-line structure sound. The record gives it an owner: the Operations Lead "oversees those systems and is accountable to their outputs" (WP p.10), and Dominic's seat owns the build.

**Position 6.6 (Recommended).** Every goal set at the company and service-period units (section 7) reserves one line for system maintenance and one for a named existential risk, owned by the Operations Lead and by a founder respectively, so that the work no one prioritizes has a named owner before each period begins. No ratio is attached to the reserve; the reservation is a line, not a share.

**Position 6.7 (Founder-gated).** The existential-risk register itself: which risks are on it, in what order, and which founder owns each. The candidates above are the team's input list.

### 6.5 Interface complexity and the phases of scale

"The more people there are on a team, the more possible interfaces there are" (Book p.94). At Sŏn the variable is not people. Interfaces multiply with service periods, with the number of domains a service period touches, and with the hours at which handoffs occur: the same kitchen serves every period; the same room transforms between them; the customer record must travel from the morning window to the dinner reservation. The daypart charter's "what it hands on" and "what it draws from" sections (S2, position 11.2) are the interface specification, and each added period adds interfaces to every period already open. This is the structural reason the record adds periods one at a time.

Her three phases of scale, from a shared codebase through decoupling to "a true platform or shared services architecture" where "the entire company... needs to be composed of separate units led by very experienced people... loosely coupled with respect to dependencies but tightly aligned via planning and goal setting" (Book p.95), do not survive as phases. Sŏn is built in her third phase from the first day: domains with documented interfaces, loosely coupled, aligned through the commitments and the goal layer, on a stack that is the shared-services architecture. The record makes exactly that claim about why the structure can run lean: "The stack does not add complexity. It moves the complexity that was always there off of people and into software" (WP p.21). The claim is honest and it is untested, which is why section 6.3 tests it rather than adopting it.

**Position 6.8 (Recommended).** Interface load at Sŏn is counted in service periods and handoffs, not in people. Each new period's daypart charter specifies its interfaces before it opens, and the operating system adds no period whose interfaces are unwritten.

### 6.6 What does not survive

Her advice to "publicly celebrate managers who are actively improving their operational efficiency, for example by coming in under budget at the end of the fiscal year or 'giving back' headcount allocation" (Book p.99) does not survive. There is no headcount to give back, and the record's peer recognition platform rewards "excellence in the moment" from colleagues (WP p.19), not budget performance. The Sŏn analogue of an efficiency gain is friction removed, and the record's own signal for it is bandwidth, read through the leading indicators. Her objective-measures-against-lobbying tactic ("revenue per head," Book p.99) does not survive either; there are no internal claimants and the measure is lagging.

**Position 6.9 (Recommended).** Efficiency at Sŏn is read as friction removed and bandwidth recovered, not as budget or headcount returned, and it is recognized through the mechanisms the record already runs.

## 7. Annual and quarterly goals

### 7.1 The goal-bearing unit

Her goals "serve as a contract between the accountable team and the rest of the division about the work that will be completed" (Book p.102), and "it's ideal for company goals to replicate down from the division to the team to the individual" (Book p.103). Neither the division nor the team exists at Sŏn (section 3.2). The question this component has to answer is what the goal-bearing unit is in a house with strands and domains, and the answer is the four units of position 3.2, each carrying a different kind of goal.

**The company.** The long-term commitments (S2, position 6.3) are the parent of every goal. The company's annual goals are the phase gates of section 5.4 and the "what must be true" list of section 5.3, set by the founders in their domains.

**The service period.** Once a period has a ratified daypart charter, its goals are set against that charter's "reason to exist," "first customer," "what it is measured by," and "what it gives the layer" (S2, position 11.2). A service period's goals are one set across both strands and the kitchen; there are no floor goals and kitchen goals for a period, which is her rule "never organize team goals by function" (Book p.106) with the unit translated. The Maitre d, the Operations Lead, and the Chef Partner set them together, because the period is the unit at which their domains intersect.

**The domain.** Each domain's goals are its contribution to the period goals it touches plus its own outcomes: the Operations Lead's "labor efficiency, cost targets, compliance, vendor performance, and facility integrity" (WP p.10), the Maitre d's "experience quality and team health" (WP p.10), the kitchen's, chef-gated. Domain goals are set by the domain owner and are the layer at which the reserve lines of position 6.6 live.

**The person.** Development goals, in the inverted web: "deeper into their domain and across into adjacent ones" (WP p.11). Section 7.5.

The direction is upward. Every goal at any unit names the long-term commitment it serves and, for period and domain goals, the period or domain goal it contributes to. A goal that cannot name its parent is not a goal; it is an activity someone wanted to do. This inverts her replication and keeps her throughline.

**Position 7.1 (Recommended).** Goals are set at four units: company, service period, domain, person. Each goal names the long-term commitment it ladders to. Period goals are one set across strands and kitchen. Goals are not replicated downward; they are set by the unit that holds the information and laddered up.

### 7.2 Binary states and ongoing metrics

"There are two kinds of goals: binary tasks... and ongoing metrics" (Book p.102 to 103). Survives intact, with one renaming: the record's grammar is states, not tasks. A binary goal at Sŏn describes a state the house is in, checkable yes or no: "the shift close captured every incident in every service this quarter" rather than "implement the shift close." Her heuristic is the same: "States, not activities" (Book p.106).

### 7.3 What a good goal is at Sŏn

Her "good goals" sidebar is adopted nearly whole, with the unit translated and one thing added.

Why goals exist: "Define success... Focus... Allow for autonomy" (Book p.104 to 105). The third is the one the record cares most about: "Aligning around and committing to a shared definition of success creates accountability. Doing so without mandating specific activities allows accountability to coexist with autonomy and creativity." That is the enabling-constraint form the profile prefers and the playground philosophy requires: "Clear enough to feel safe; open enough to stay" (Canon 1.0, section 01).

What good goals look like: focused, concise, comprehensible, memorable; "People can remember about three to five things" (Book p.105); objectively assessable; challenging but possible; user-oriented, "set your goals as close to the customer as possible" (Book p.106), which at Sŏn means set at the edge, where the customer is felt first; states, not activities; sensitive and specific.

Her heuristics for testing goals, all adopted: a goal that starts with a verb is an action, reframe it; no function-split goals; no more than a page, three to five per unit; if you do not wince at what was left out, you have not focused; if two people could disagree on whether it was achieved, it is not specific; if it could be achieved and you would still be dissatisfied, something is missing; if you could succeed without it, it is over-specific. Grove's test is the standard: "Did I do that, or did I not do that? Yes. No. Simple" (Book p.109).

The thing added: canon's voice rules apply to goals. A goal is internal copy, and "the forbidden lexicon applies without exception" (Canon 1.0, section 01). A goal written under the six binary tests (Canon 1.0, section 09) is objectively assessable by construction: it cannot name its own effect, its nouns carry weight, it ends at its last load-bearing word, and it could not appear unchanged in a competitor's plan. "Elevate the experience" fails test 1 and the lexicon. "Every known customer is recognized before they are seated" passes every test and Grove's.

OKRs and SMART: she notes they are "not mutually exclusive" and that "OKRs are just goals with a particular structure" separating the objective from the definition of success (Book p.103, p.111). Sŏn adopts the structure, an objective stated as a state and key results that are binary or metric, and does not adopt either acronym as a brand. The workbook's discipline, three to five objectives with one to five key results each, one page, published (Workbook p.26), is adopted as the form of the goal page (section 14).

**Position 7.2 (Recommended).** Sŏn's goals are written as states, three to five per unit, one page per unit, each with binary or metric key results, tested by her heuristics and by canon's six binary tests, and published where any team member can read them (section 11.7).

### 7.4 Committed and aspirational, declared up front

"Take care to specify which goals are aspirational versus committed" (Book p.112). Her conditions for a committed goal: an existential threat; another team blocked; "a customer has been told a product or project will be delivered on a particular timeline" (Book p.112).

Each condition has a Sŏn form. The existential class is section 6.4's register. The blocked-team case is a period whose opening waits on another period being steady, or a domain whose goal waits on the stack. And the customer-told case fires unusually often at Sŏn, because the documented build tells the pre-opening community what is coming in real time: an opening date announced on a stream is a commitment to a customer in her exact sense. The record's discipline, "never assert the unproven" (WP p.36), is what keeps the committed class small: an announcement is made only once the gate behind it has been passed.

**Position 7.3 (Recommended).** A goal is committed if it gates an opening, protects a non-negotiable or a food-safety condition, or has been stated to customers through the documented build. Every other goal is aspirational. The class is declared when the goal is written, and a committed goal that trends behind is protected by sacrificing aspirational ones, per the workbook's rule (Workbook p.26). No target values are attached to the classes on this page.

### 7.5 Personal development goals

"Every quarter, an individual should have at least one or two personal development goals in addition to the work output they expect to complete" (Book p.113). Survives, with the justification replaced and the mechanism improved. Hers is manager-set. Sŏn's is system-surfaced: "After a set period in any role, the next skill set unlocks automatically. It is self-paced. When complete, the practical assessment auto-schedules with the appropriate domain lead" (WP p.11). The person's development goals for a quarter are the skill sets the system has unlocked and the person has chosen, deeper in the domain or across into an adjacent one, and cross-strand fluency, "the real development marker" (WP p.11), is the goal class the record values most. The domain lead's part is the competency conversation and the sign-off, not the assignment.

**Position 7.4 (Recommended).** Every person carries one or two development goals per quarter, drawn from the skill sets the invisible advocate has unlocked and chosen by the person, with cross-strand fluency as a named class. The domain lead signs off; the system surfaces. A development goal that waits on a lead's attention to exist is a skill-invisibility failure.

### 7.6 The "how," and the Pyrrhic victory

"Performance = results × behaviors. It's multiplicative. Hitting targets while fomenting unrest and backstabbing should get you a 0" (Book p.115). "Yes, the product launched on time, but the team and the relationships therein barely survived" (Book p.114).

This survives and the record strengthens it into a rule rather than a review note. The protection order puts bandwidth before results, and the deck states "the order is the decision" (Deck 3.0, p.04). A goal met by consuming the team's bandwidth is a cooling failure: "It runs people at full capacity until they stop, and calls it dedication until it becomes turnover" (WP p.13). At Sŏn that is not a Pyrrhic victory to be reflected on afterward; it is a failure of the system scored as such at the time. The leading indicators are the read: a goal achieved while employee NPS or the cultural labor score fell is scored against the fall.

Her instruction to "say the thing you think you cannot say" and "acknowledge that a certain project was accomplished at too high a cost... That includes acknowledging your own role in that reality" (Book p.115) maps to the record's post-mortem rule: "the first question is not who failed. It is what allowed this to happen... Management absorbs blame from staff. Post-mortems are private and process-focused" (WP p.17).

**Position 7.5 (Recommended).** A goal's score is read against the leading indicators of the period in which it was pursued. A goal met while the nervous system's read fell is scored as a system failure, not as a win, and the post-mortem asks what allowed it. This position depends on the founder decision on "the order is the decision" (position 6.3).

### 7.7 Goals before there is a baseline

Her FAQ: "What if I have a metric but I don't know what the right target is?... Take a guess at a reasonable number, be honest that it's a stab in the dark" (Book p.110). The record forbids the guess: "the discipline is to never assert the unproven" (WP p.36). Pre-opening, Sŏn has no operating history and cannot set a target on any rate.

The resolution is her own distinction. Pre-opening goals and first-quarter goals are binary states, not metric targets: the readiness rows of section 12 are the pre-opening goals in their entirety, and the first quarter's goals are of the form "a baseline exists for X, computed by the stack, reviewed at the leads' review." Targets on rates appear from the second quarter, set against the first quarter's baseline and stated as relative movement, which the workbook asks for anyway ("Is the metric relative, not absolute?", Workbook p.25). This also answers what a goal is in the pre-opening period: a state the operating system must reach by a gate.

**Position 7.6 (Recommended).** Pre-opening goals are the readiness rows. First-quarter goals are binary states and baselining goals. No target on a rate is set before a baseline exists, and targets from the second quarter are relative to that baseline.

### 7.8 Scoring, changes, and disagreement

Mid-quarter and end-quarter scoring in green, yellow, and red, "on track, off track, or at risk" (Workbook p.29), survives intact and is placed on the cadence by Session 4. Her rule that heavy scoring means the goals were not internalized (Book p.108) survives. The workbook's change rules survive: adding or dropping a key result is documented and impacted units notified; a dropped objective stays on the page for scoring "and discussion" (Workbook p.29), which at Sŏn is the versioned-decision rule applied to goals. Her diagnostic for disagreement, work from vision and strategy down through priorities, interpretation, incomplete success statements, feasibility, capacity (Book p.110 to 111), survives intact, and its top rung is the charter: if two units disagree on a goal because they read the mission differently, the disagreement is Session 2's to resolve, not this page's.

**Position 7.7 (Recommended).** Goals are scored mid-period and end-period on the three-state rubric; changes are versioned and notified; disagreements are diagnosed top-down from the charter. Cadence to Session 4.

## 8. Metrics that matter

### 8.1 Her structure and the record's constraint

"Long-term metrics tend to be lagging indicators. They represent the output of a ton of operational, short-term 'input' metrics" (Book p.115 to 116). Her layers: charter metrics, three to five, stable for two to five years, lagging; operating metrics, three to five, "real-time or leading indicators that measure activities or intermediate outcomes" (Book p.119); zero targets; shared definitions; a dashboard; regular review.

The record's constraint is the ledger's carryover three and it is absolute on the primary read: "Revenue, labor percentage, food cost: these exist because they have always existed. All lagging indicators. By the time they signal a problem, the damage is done. Sŏn measures what predicts problems before they compound" (WP p.22). The three named leading indicators: "Employee NPS: would you recommend working here, tracked regularly rather than annually. Cultural labor score: beyond hours and cost, how the team is actually doing. Customer recognition rate: whether the building is actually recognizing and remembering customers" (WP p.22). The cultural measures, "tracked with the same seriousness as cost": "retention rate, internal promotion rate, engagement results across psychological safety and values alignment, and participation in development" (WP p.23). And the qualitative side, "the part most operators never gather": "Stories of how the culture shows up in daily problem-solving. Exit interviews read for what could have been better rather than filed and forgotten. Customer feedback about the team's experience... And how people grow beyond the building" (WP p.23).

Her premise that long-term metrics are lagging is inverted at Sŏn, and the inversion is not a preference; it is the record's thesis about what compounds. The three engines compound over years, and the record's measures of them are all leading in her sense: they are inputs that predict the lagging outcomes. What differs between Sŏn's two layers is horizon, not lag: the charter metrics are the same nervous system read as a trend over years; the operating metrics are the same nervous system read at service tempo.

### 8.2 The metric layer

Every metric ladders to a long-term commitment. The commitments are founder-gated in selection (S2, position 6.4), so the layer is built against the record's candidate commitments and re-parented once the selection lands. No figure and no target value is attached to any metric on this page.

**Charter metrics, the long horizon, three to five.** Candidates, each with its parent commitment and its owner (section 9):

1. *Employee NPS, as a trend.* Parent: the organization that compounds, "labor as asset" (WP p.16). Owner: the Maitre d, accountable to "team health" (WP p.10), with Brandon's seat as the company-level owner of the operating system.
2. *Customer recognition rate.* Parent: the customer that compounds, belonging. Defined as the share of visits by a known customer at which the record's held preferences were acted on before being asked. Owner: the Maitre d, who owns "customer relationship systems" (WP p.10). Section 9.3 splits it at the domain seam.
3. *Cross-period movement.* Parent: the platform, "what the building is actually proving" (WP p.35). Defined as the share of known customers with visits in more than one service period. This is the company's exact test written as a metric, and it is leading for everything Horizon 3 depends on. Owner: split, section 9.3.
4. *Retention rate and internal promotion rate.* Parent: the organization that compounds; "direct evidence the development commitments are real rather than stated" (WP p.23). Owner: the Maitre d for retention, the Operations Lead for the invisible advocate's mechanics, since the auto-unlock and the ready-now pool are stack functions.
5. *Cultural labor score.* Parent: the protection order, bandwidth first. The record gives it one line and no definition. Founder-gated, section 8.5.

**Operating metrics, service tempo, three to five per service period and per domain.** Candidates:

1. *Shift-close capture completeness.* Every incident, every recovery, every generosity use captured. This is the metric that tells the leads whether the nervous system is intact; its failure is sequence corruption.
2. *The record travels.* The share of known-customer reservations at which daypart history and preferences were present at the brief before seating. Clara's test as a metric. Owner: the Operations Lead, because it is a stack function.
3. *Recognition delivered.* Given that the record traveled, the share at which the floor acted on it. Owner: the Maitre d. Metrics 2 and 3 are the same charter metric split at the seam (section 9.3).
4. *Feedback loop closure.* The share of items filed in the structured channel that reached a visible status inside the loop window, and the age of the oldest open item. "Feedback without follow-through breeds cynicism. The loop gets closed" (WP p.22). Owner: the domain the item names; the Operations Lead for the mechanism.
5. *Generosity range utilization.* The share of the pre-authorized range used per service. Under-use is the failure: "a line item to be fully used, not minimized" (WP p.26). Owner: the Maitre d. The counterweight is recovery frequency, not cost, because cost is a lagging figure and not a context source.
6. *Employee NPS at its stated rhythm,* which is the charter metric's operating read; the record says "tracked regularly rather than annually" and Session 4 sets the rhythm.
7. *Kitchen operating metrics.* Chef-gated. The record's candidates are waste tracking at plate and scrap level (WP p.21) and sensor-based food-safety logging.

**Counterweights.** The workbook asks whether a metric "has counterweight metrics" (Workbook p.25). Every leading indicator on this page carries one, and the record supplies them from canon's persona failure conditions: recognition rate is counterweighted by the Mia failure, "the floor explains the food she grew up with" (Deck 3.0, p.22), so a recognition that becomes unsolicited instruction counts against; cross-period movement is counterweighted by dinner's protection, so a morning customer moved to dinner in a way that costs dinner its register counts against; generosity utilization is counterweighted by recovery frequency, so a range spent on recovery rather than belonging is a signal; employee NPS is counterweighted by bandwidth, so a high score at zero bandwidth is read as a cooling failure in progress.

**Position 8.1 (Recommended).** The metric layer is two horizons of the same leading indicators: charter metrics as trends over years, operating metrics at service tempo, each laddered to a long-term commitment, each with a named owner and a named counterweight. The candidates above are the input list. No target value is carried.

**Position 8.2 (Chef-gated).** The kitchen's operating metrics and the kitchen's contribution to the charter metrics.

**Position 8.3 (Team-filled).** The baselines for every metric, established in dinner's first quarter, and the targets set relative to them from the second quarter.

### 8.3 Zero targets

"'Zero targets,' meaning a measure for which the desired outcome is no incidence of the issue occurring" (Book p.116). Survives intact. Sŏn's candidates: food-safety critical events, read from the sensor logging the defensive stack runs; David protocol breaches, meaning kitchen access improvised on the floor; a known customer treated as a first-time customer (the Clara failure); a candidate ghosted ("No one is ghosted," WP p.18); a feedback item past the loop window with no status; and one the language constant makes checkable, the word the brand does not use appearing in any internal or external copy.

**Position 8.4 (Recommended).** Zero targets are set for food-safety critical events, David protocol breaches, known customers unrecognized on arrival, ghosted candidates, and feedback items open past the loop window. Each has an owner and each is a committed goal by section 7.4.

### 8.4 The lagging indicators

They are not discarded. "Lagging indicators, revenue, labor cost, food cost, are the X-ray after the fracture" (WP p.12). An X-ray is read after the fact, by the person whose domain it is. Revenue, labor percentage, and food cost live in Dominic's domain and in the Operations Lead's outcomes ("cost targets," WP p.10). They are reviewed on the cadence and never first: no accountability mechanism on this page opens with a lagging figure, and a proposal to promote one to the primary read has to argue against WP p.22 in writing.

**Position 8.5 (Recommended).** Lagging indicators are owned in Dominic's and the Operations Lead's domains, reviewed on the cadence, and never the first item in any accountability mechanism. A proposal to make one primary is a Class A decision (section 4.3) because it changes what the company counts as success.

**Position 8.6 (Founder-gated).** Which lagging figures, if any, are shown to the team at all, and on which surface. The record commits to unusual transparency at hiring (the compensation mechanics on a single sheet, WP p.18) and says nothing about operating figures. This is the transparency line section 11.2 needs and only the founders can draw it.

### 8.5 Shared definitions, the hardest part

"Having shared definitions of core concepts is more critical than you might think. For example, what is a customer?... As you establish your operating systems, the measurement piece is often the most difficult and critical part" (Book p.120). She is right and Sŏn's grammar makes the problem sharper, because the record uses the same word for things that must be counted differently.

The definitions the metric layer needs, with what the record already supplies and what it does not:

- *Customer.* The record's word, deliberately (WP p.25). For counting: a person with a record in the identity spine. The record says "SevenRooms as the customer identity spine" (WP p.21) and canon uses "record" for "what the brand holds about a customer" (Canon 1.0, section 09).
- *Known customer.* A customer whose record is "resolved across every channel and every daypart, so the morning customer and the late-night customer are known to be the same person" (WP p.27). The definition depends on the identity resolution actually running.
- *Recognition.* A held preference acted on without being asked, before or at the moment it matters. Not a greeting by name alone, and never an explanation (the Mia counterweight).
- *Return.* Canon's word ("return" not "loyalty," Canon 1.0, section 09). A subsequent visit by a known customer, in any service period. The fifth visit is the record's arc marker (WP p.25), so "fifth-visit rate" is a candidate operating read of belonging.
- *Service period.* One of the periods with a ratified daypart charter. Not "daypart," which the record uses for both an operating period and a brand expression.
- *Incident.* Any event the server or floor manager resolved inside or outside their range, captured in the shift close. The record's list: "the system captures the incident in the shift close, the appropriate lead reviews, the process that allowed the failure gets updated" (WP p.13).
- *Closed loop.* A feedback item with a visible status and, where the status is "acted on," a named change. The window is Session 4's.
- *Employee NPS.* The record's line is the question: "would you recommend working here" (WP p.22). The rhythm and the population (all team members, or by strand) are unstated.
- *Cultural labor score.* "Beyond hours and cost, how the team is actually doing" (WP p.22). This is the least defined of the three and it is Brandon's concept. The team can specify what it must do: read bandwidth rather than output, be captured at service tempo rather than annually, and not reduce to hours. It cannot define it.
- *Customer recognition rate.* Defined above, and it needs the "record travels" and "recognition delivered" split to be computable.

**Position 8.7 (Recommended).** The operating system holds a metric definitions register: one entry per term, with the definition, the source in the record, the calculation the stack can run, the owner, the counterweight, and the review cadence. A metric with no entry is not published. Changing a charter metric's definition is a Class A decision.

**Position 8.8 (Founder-gated).** The definition of the cultural labor score. Brandon originated it; the record gives it one line. The team specifies what it must do and cannot supply what it is.

**Position 8.9 (Team-filled).** The rhythm and population for employee NPS, and the wording of every definition once the leads exist to test them against real service.

### 8.6 The write-up form and the review

The metrics write-up sidebar survives intact as a form: objectives answer "Where do I want to go?", metrics answer "How will I know I'm getting there?", one objective may carry several metrics, a dashboard exists, and "metrics can't help you if you don't use and review them regularly" (Book p.118 to 119). At Sŏn the dashboard is the stack's, on the OS surface, and the review is the leads' review (section 10.2). Her rule that the review's frequency should match how fast the metric can be moved (Book p.126) is applied in section 10.5.

## 9. Ownership

### 9.1 Where her hierarchy is thinnest and the record is strongest

"Goals and metrics should have owners who are ultimately responsible for completing the work... each company target should have an owner or owners" (Book p.121). "If you don't define ownership early in a project, division and team culture will suffer... teams that lack clear accountability risk becoming political" (Book p.122).

This is the component where the record does not need translating. "Authority lives in the domain. The person who owns the domain is accountable to its outcomes" (WP p.07 to 08). Domain ownership is the rigid phase of the silk: "small, specific, explicitly defined, interlocking. The server who owns the table relationship. The chef partner who owns their station" (WP p.09). And the record's account of politics is structural where hers is cultural: "The absence of a clear path is where politics begin. Politics are a load-bearing structure problem. They exist because people are trying to position themselves closer to the weight. In a code-bearing structure there is no weight to position yourself under" (WP p.11). Undefined ownership at Sŏn is a code-bearing gap, the same class of failure as skill invisibility, and it is fixed the same way, by documenting and opening access.

**Position 9.1 (Recommended).** Every goal, every metric, every accountability mechanism, and every automated alert has exactly one named owner before it is published, and the owner is the domain that holds the outcome. This is the record's rule applied to her instruments; it is stricter than hers because "owner or owners" is not permitted.

### 9.2 Ownership on two clocks

Her ownership is standing: a person owns a target for a quarter. Sŏn's runs on two clocks, and the record names both.

**Standing ownership** is the domain: the two leads, the founders in their domains, the Station Leads, the Head of Beverage, the Lead Host, each server for their tables and their book of regulars ("Each server has the tools to build and maintain their own book of regulars," WP p.25).

**Per-service ownership** is the functional designation: "functional designations describe who owns what on a given night. The Lead Host owns the door and the host-stand function... The Chef Partner owns culinary execution during service... The Head of Beverage owns beverage program execution for this shift. These designations mark accountability in the moment, in the domain. They are not permanent elevations above anyone" (WP p.10). The instrument that assigns per-service ownership already exists: the shift brief. It names tonight's designations, tonight's flagged records, tonight's emergency authority, and the person who will trigger the transitions. A service that begins without the brief naming these is her "wing and a prayer" run at tempo.

Three per-service ownerships the record leaves unassigned, with this page's recommendations: the transition trigger, "same person, same moment" for lighting, sound, and service (Canon 1.0, section 08), recommended to the owner of the room; the David flag, recommended to the Maitre d (section 4.2); emergency authority, recommended in section 4.3, Class D.

**Position 9.2 (Recommended).** Ownership at Sŏn runs on two clocks: standing ownership by domain, and per-service ownership by functional designation named in the shift brief. The shift brief names the designations, the transition trigger's owner, the flagged records, and emergency authority for that service, or the service does not begin.

### 9.3 The single-owner rule and the two parallel leads

Her hardest case: "For teams that must collaborate to achieve an outcome, like product and engineering, you might find yourself assigning ownership to a pair of people, risky but possible" (Book p.122). Her better answer is the one she actually used: split the metric. The head of sales owned "'what's on the truck' revenue" and the product team owned the new-product line (Book p.121). "It's worth surfacing and teasing these things apart."

At Sŏn the two leads are parallel by design, "neither reports to the other" (WP p.10), and several of the metric layer's entries cross their seam. Pair ownership is refused, because a metric with two owners has none when it fails. The record's structure gives the split its lines: the Operations Lead owns whatever the stack does and is "accountable to their outputs, not their manual production" (WP p.10); the Maitre d owns what people do with what the stack surfaces. So customer recognition rate splits into "the record travels" (Operations Lead) and "recognition delivered" (Maitre d). Cross-period movement splits into "the profile is one across periods" (Dominic's data architecture, executed through the Operations Lead) and "the customer was invited across" (the Maitre d and the servers' books). Retention splits into the invisible advocate's mechanics (Operations Lead) and the team's health (Maitre d). Where a metric cannot be split at the seam, its owner is the daypart charter's "held by" entry for that period, and its escalation is section 4.3, Class C.

The center is not a person. The leads serve "the same center, the culinary and conceptual vision" (WP p.10), and Session 2 found that who holds culinary direction overall is unresolved (S2, section 7.3). The operating system as a whole has one owner: Brandon's seat owns "the operating systems" (WP p.03). The stack has one owner: Dominic's seat owns "the technology build" and "the data architecture" (WP p.03). The OS surface, which is where the two meet, is founder-gated (S2, section 7.2).

**Position 9.3 (Recommended).** No metric has two owners. A metric that crosses the seam between the two leads is split at the seam into a stack-side measure owned by the Operations Lead and a floor-side measure owned by the Maitre d. The operating system has one owner, Brandon's seat; the stack has one owner, Dominic's seat.

**Position 9.4 (Founder-gated).** Ownership of the OS surface, which carries both the dashboard and the team home, inherits Session 2's founder decision and is not re-decided here.

**Position 9.5 (Chef-gated).** Ownership of the kitchen's metrics and of culinary direction overall, as distinct from execution during service.

### 9.4 The "wing and a prayer" anti-pattern

"One of the worst management mistakes you can make is to put a task out to your team... and, using what I call the 'wing and a prayer' method, hope someone will step up and volunteer" (Book p.122). Survives intact, and the record has already converted it into a mechanism for the one case where it would otherwise recur every night: the shift brief's designations. The operating rule is position 9.1. Its floor-level form is that no incident, once captured in the shift close, leaves the leads' review without a named owner for the process change, because "the process that allowed the failure gets updated, and the next server inherits a system that has already learned from it" (WP p.13) only if someone owns the update.

## 10. Accountability mechanisms

### 10.1 What the record already runs

"Accountability mechanisms are tools that both leaders and managers should use to review progress toward their goals and missions... These mechanisms include elements like meetings to review plans and action items, metrics dashboards, and written project snippets" (Book p.123). "The first step in implementing accountability mechanisms is to identify who is going to participate in the mechanism and the cadence" (Book p.123).

Sŏn already runs mechanisms the record names, and this section places them in her frame rather than inventing a parallel set. In the record:

- **The shift brief.** Pre-service. Named in the failure-mode reference: "The shift brief is not happening" is sequence corruption (WP p.14). It is where preferences surface, where designations are named, where the David flags reach the kitchen ("pre-service kitchen communication," Canon 1.0, section 08). Also the record's rhythm: "Beginning of shift, prepare for the customer" (WP p.26).
- **The shift close.** Post-service. "The system captures the incident in the shift close, the appropriate lead reviews, the process that allowed the failure gets updated" (WP p.13). "Post-shift documentation is not captured" is sequence corruption (WP p.14). And "End of shift, reflect and strengthen the relationship" (WP p.26): the thank-you note is written at the close.
- **The structured feedback channel.** "Syncs to the execution system, so employees can surface issues, suggest improvements, and see their input lead to action... The loop gets closed" (WP p.22).
- **The development review.** "Reviews happen every three to six months and focus on development, growth, and the path forward rather than a verdict... a formal review never contains new information" (WP p.19).
- **The competency conversation.** Auto-scheduled by the invisible advocate (WP p.11, p.19).
- **The ninety-day plan** for every new person, "with regular check-ins and course correction" (WP p.18).
- **Peer recognition,** in the moment, through a platform (WP p.19).
- **The post-mortem,** "private and process-focused" (WP p.17).
- **The exit interview,** "read for what could have been better" (WP p.23).
- **The content rhythm:** "A quick daily pulse... A weekly read... A monthly strategic review" with revenue attribution underneath (WP p.31).
- **The pre-opening full review** of the sonic system, "non-negotiable" (Canon 1.0, section 07), which is a gate mechanism.

Placed in her frame: the shift brief and shift close are accountability mechanisms at the service-period unit, daily. The feedback channel is an accountability mechanism at the company unit, continuous. The development review, competency conversation, and ninety-day plan are accountability mechanisms at the person unit. The content rhythm is a domain-level mechanism in Brandon's domain. Peer recognition is reinforcement, her "rewards and recognition" row (Book p.88). The exit interview and post-mortem are learning mechanisms that feed the qualitative side of the metric layer. None of these is monitoring in her sense; monitoring is section 10.7.

**Position 10.1 (Recommended).** The record's existing mechanisms are the operating system's accountability mechanisms, placed at their units as above. The operating system adds three that the record implies but does not name: the leads' review (10.2), the house review (10.4), and the phase-gate review (10.4). It adds nothing else at the company level.

### 10.2 Her two minimums, translated

"I recommend at least these two forms of accountability mechanisms for every team: Weekly team meeting... Weekly team metrics review: Use the first 15 minutes of your team meeting to review your metrics" (Book p.125 to 126).

Neither has a weekly team to attach to. The service period's team meets every service, at the brief. So the daily brief is the period's team meeting, and it does one of her jobs: "keep everyone on the same page about priorities, progress, and action items, as well as who owns those action items" (Book p.126). What the brief cannot do is cross periods, and crossing periods is where the record's bus failure lives. The weekly mechanism that crosses periods is therefore the leads' review: the Operations Lead, the Maitre d, the Chef Partner in their functional role until the seat is a partner, and the founders as their domains require. It reviews the week's shift closes across every period, the feedback channel's open items, the goal pages, and the incidents whose process changes need an owner.

Her second minimum survives intact: the leads' review opens with the metrics, and at Sŏn "the metrics" means the nervous-system read, the leading indicators at service tempo, before anything else. "Doing so ensures that everyone is focused on the same numbers at the same time" (Book p.126). The lagging figures, if reviewed, come last.

**Position 10.2 (Recommended).** The shift brief is the service period's team meeting, daily. The leads' review is the cross-period mechanism, weekly, and it opens with the leading indicators. Its participants are the two leads, the kitchen's owner of execution, and the founders as their domains require. Its exact cadence and agenda template are Session 4's.

### 10.3 Snippets

"Each Sunday night ahead of our standing Monday meeting, the Stripe leadership team shares snippets of key information from the past week, priorities for the upcoming week, and progress on action items. Much of the Monday meeting agenda is derived from those snippets" (Book p.123).

The snippet survives with its direction and form replaced. Hers is written by leaders for the leadership meeting. Sŏn's edge writes daily: the shift close is the snippet, structured, short, captured in the system at the end of service by the people who felt the room. The center writes weekly: each lead files a short note before the leads' review, priorities and open items, and the review's agenda derives from the closes and the notes. The record's "snippet" is therefore two things, and the more important one is written at the edge.

**Position 10.3 (Recommended).** The shift close is the edge's snippet, daily. The lead's weekly note is the center's, and the leads' review agenda is derived from both. Neither is long-form; both are system-resident.

### 10.4 The QBR, the house review, and the phase-gate review

Her QBR is a division-level mechanism for "companies that have reached a certain level of complexity, for example more than 200 employees and many products and teams" (Book p.123). Sŏn will never meet that trigger and has no divisions. Her reasons for it survive anyway: it "keeps key stakeholders and leaders on the same page about the division's or team's focus areas and progress," problem-solves persistent issues, "aligns leadership on upcoming priorities," and "serves as an accountability mechanism for the unit's leader and management team" (Book p.124). Sŏn's units are the service periods and the domains, and the leads are the people the mechanism holds accountable.

Sŏn runs one house review, quarterly, for the whole company, with each open service period reviewed against its daypart charter and each domain against its goal page. Sŏn also runs a phase-gate review, once per opening, which is the house review's form applied to the readiness test of section 12 and to the founder-gated "steady" condition of position 5.6.

**Position 10.4 (Recommended).** Sŏn runs one quarterly house review, structured per the table above, with the five failure modes as its standing scorecard and the leading indicators as its spine, and a phase-gate review per opening that applies the same form to the readiness test. The QBR per division is not used.

**Position 10.5 (Team-filled).** The house review's first instance, and the calibration of its length and sections against what the leads can actually prepare between services.

### 10.5 The frequency rule

"The frequency of the accountability mechanisms should correlate with how quickly your team can impact the metrics in question" (Book p.126). Adopted as the rule that assigns each metric to a mechanism. Recognition delivered and shift-close completeness move per service and are read at the close and at the brief. Feedback loop closure and generosity utilization move over days and are read at the leads' review. Employee NPS moves over weeks and is read at its stated rhythm. Retention and internal promotion move over quarters and are read at the house review. Cross-period movement moves over months and is read at the house review as a trend. Reading a metric faster than it can move is, in her words, "demoralizing," and at Sŏn it is also a bandwidth cost.

**Position 10.6 (Recommended).** Each metric in the definitions register names the mechanism that reads it, chosen by how fast the metric can be moved. The specific rhythms are Session 4's.

### 10.6 The reset

"Constantly tweaking your company mechanisms can be counterproductive, but taking stock at least once a year can help you evolve as you scale. At Stripe, we usually reset some of our meetings and accountability mechanisms in January" (Book p.125). Survives intact, and Sŏn's first reset comes earlier than a year: the mechanisms are designed pre-opening against no operating history, so they are reviewed after dinner's first quarter, at the same point Session 2 placed the first principles revision, and annually after that.

**Position 10.7 (Recommended).** The accountability mechanisms are reviewed as a set after dinner's first quarter and annually thereafter, with feedback gathered through the structured channel. Placed on the cadence by Session 4.

### 10.7 Monitoring, and the named actor

"Accountability mechanisms are not the same thing as monitoring. You'll also want to have an automated dashboard that tracks whether something out of the ordinary has happened... the accountability mechanism you need to put in place is to determine ahead of time who is responsible for acting on a sudden change in the dashboard and how that person should communicate the root cause and the solution to the relevant stakeholders" (Book p.126 to 127).

This is the point where the vocabulary line does the most work. Sŏn's monitoring is the stack: "The OS surface is the home where the software agents live, each view sourced from several systems plus camera AI, updating live" (WP p.21), with "sensor-based food-safety logging and predictive maintenance" and "a weapon-detection layer on the camera feed" (WP p.21) among the defensive tools. That surface monitors. It does not hold anyone accountable, and the record is explicit that AI is "never to anything load-bearing" (WP p.21). Her rule supplies what the record leaves implicit: for every alert the surface can raise, a person is named in advance to act on it, and the path by which they report the cause and the fix is written.

The ledger's rule sharpens hers. The named actor is never a founder by default. A dashboard whose alerts route to Brandon is a system that produces stability by a founder's hand, which is node overload at the top of the web. Alerts route to the domain owner whose outcome they touch: food-safety to the kitchen's owner of execution during service, with the Operations Lead as the compliance owner; a stack fault to the Operations Lead; a customer-record fault (Clara's history not traveling) to the Operations Lead before service and to the Maitre d at the brief; a weapon-detection alert to whoever holds emergency authority that service (section 4.3, Class D). The founders are the destination of escalation, not of alerts.

**Position 10.8 (Recommended).** The operating system holds an alert register: every automated alert the stack raises, with its threshold's owner, its named actor, the actor's range, and its report path. No alert is enabled without an entry. The named actor is never a founder by default, and an alert that routes to a founder is a design fault to be corrected, not a founder's job.

## 11. Internal communications

### 11.1 The trigger

"Dunbar's number... At around 150 people, it becomes harder to remember everyone's name" (Book p.128). Sŏn will not reach it and would not need the trigger if it did. Its trigger is the hour: the person who ran the morning window is not in the building at late night, and the two never meet unless the operating system arranges it. Her phrase for the risk is exact anyway: internal communications "can become either an equalizing or a discriminating force" (Book p.127), and at Sŏn the line of discrimination would run between service periods, between the people who were in the room when a decision was made and the people who work a different hour. The record's bus failure is this: "When the relationship breaks, the information stops. This is not a culture problem. It is a systems problem" (WP p.13).

**Position 11.1 (Recommended).** Sŏn's internal communications trigger is non-co-presence by hour, and the system is designed so that no service period's team learns of a decision later, or differently, than another's.

### 11.2 The four tests

"Is it complete?... Is it accessible?... Is it reliable?... Is it transparent? Transparency doesn't mean sharing everything. It means that everyone has clarity about what will be shared, when, and with whom" (Book p.131). All four survive intact. Their Sŏn-specific edges:

- *Complete.* The information people need to do their jobs is, at Sŏn, unusually specific and already promised: the competency path ("Every position has documented competency requirements visible to anyone," WP p.11), the range and triggers for their position (section 4), tonight's designations and flagged records (the brief), the goals of their period and domain (section 7).
- *Accessible.* Her segments are "tenure, geography, and language." Sŏn's are hour, strand, and language, the last because the record lets a candidate "choose the interview language" (WP p.17), which implies a team for whom English is not everyone's first language, and the operating system must be readable by that team.
- *Reliable.* The shift brief happens every service; the feedback loop closes inside its window; the leads' review runs every week. Reliability at Sŏn is the readiness test's rows 7 through 9.
- *Transparent.* What is shared with whom. The record commits to sharing the org chart, the lead, the compensation mechanics, and the advancement path with every candidate before hire (WP p.18). It says nothing about operating figures, individual reviews, or the financial model. The team's recommended line: goals, metrics, owners, competency paths, ranges, and the decision-rights register are visible to everyone in the building; individual reviews are visible to the person and their domain lead; the financial model and lagging figures are visible to the founders and to whoever the founders name (position 8.6). The line is drawn by the founders.

**Position 11.2 (Recommended).** The internal communications policy is tested against the four criteria, with accessibility read across hour, strand, and language, and with the transparency statement written out: what is shared, when, with whom.

**Position 11.3 (Founder-gated).** The transparency line for operating figures, individual review content, and the financial model. The team's recommendation is above.

### 11.3 Three times, three channels

"Plan to communicate important information at least three times using different mediums or channels. People tend to get desensitized to communication forums" (Book p.132). The rule survives; the justification is replaced. At Sŏn the reason is not desensitization, it is that no single channel reaches everyone, because the people are not in the building at the same time. Sŏn's three channels are the brief (spoken, per period, by the lead), the system (written, on the team home, versioned), and the person (the domain lead or the peer mentor the record assigns to every new hire, WP p.18). Anything that changes a range, a trigger, a goal, a designation, or a mechanism goes through all three, and it goes through the brief of every open service period, not only the period in which the decision was made.

**Position 11.4 (Recommended).** Important information travels three ways: spoken at the brief of every open service period, written on the team home, and carried by the domain lead or mentor. A change that reaches one period's brief and not another's has not been communicated.

### 11.4 The writing culture, tested

Her three arguments: writing is an equalizer for "the people who were not in the room"; writing "leads to higher-quality thought"; writing is efficient because "when the context already exists in a document, you can spend less time getting on the same page" (Book p.129 to 130). Her cost: "you end up with a lot of documents," which requires "a clear information hierarchy" and "a means to deprecate out-of-date content" (Book p.128, p.130).

Tested against a workforce that does not sit at desks, does not read long documents between shifts, and works different hours by design.

The equalizer argument survives, strengthened: the person who was not in the room is, at Sŏn, most of the team at any given hour, and the third founding seat arriving later. The record's whole memory-failure diagnosis is the absence of a writing culture by another name: "Institutional knowledge lives in people, not in the system. Every departure takes knowledge with it" (WP p.13). The higher-quality-thought argument survives at the center: the founders and leads write the goal pages, the definitions, the house review, and the argument for any Class A change, in prose, and the record's own document is the proof that the founders think this way. It does not survive at the edge as long-form: the edge writes in structured capture, the shift close and the feedback channel, short and fielded, at the end of service. The efficiency argument survives and is the reason the capture is structured: a server should never explain the same incident twice.

Form, then, is the departure. Sŏn's writing culture is structured capture at the edge, short prose at the center, and every internal surface under canon's voice rules, which already govern "any internal communication" (Canon 1.0, section 01). The forbidden lexicon and the six binary tests are what keep internal writing short enough to be read between services. Her lexicon of company terms survives as the metric definitions register and the standing vocabulary (customer, record, return, service period, the strands, the failure modes). Her style guide already exists; it is canon's verbal identity, and it applies inward.

Deprecation survives intact and the record supplies the rule: "A living document changes by versioned decision, never by drift" (Deck 3.0, p.94). Every operating document carries a version and an owner, and the token system's discipline, "SemVer with full deprecation cycle" (Canon 1.0, section 05), is the model.

**Position 11.5 (Recommended).** Sŏn's writing culture is structured capture at the edge and short prose at the center, every surface under canon's voice rules, every operating document versioned with an owner and changed only by versioned decision. Long-form is reserved for the center's arguments; the edge is never asked to read it between services.

### 11.5 Crisis and change

"In a crisis, the rate of company communication tends to taper off. Don't make the mistake of thinking you need all the answers before you communicate" (Book p.132). The record has the same rule for the brand: "Crisis communication is transparency first... the response is honesty about what happened, the real impact, and the fix" (WP p.31 to 32), and the same for the table: "be direct about what happened, acknowledge the real impact, and make it right" (WP p.26). The inward version is the same logic applied to the team.

Her Goldilocks case, an event "not widely known but... affecting the company" (Book p.133), has a Sŏn form the record names in advance: a key person leaving, which the record treats as "a bounded, temporary, calculable" cost (WP p.14) and which the floor will feel as tension loss before it is announced. The team's rule: the departure is told at the next brief of every open service period, in person, by the domain lead, before it is written, and the tension map (which nodes are compensating) is stated at the same time, so the team hears the structural read rather than a rumor about a slot.

**Position 11.6 (Recommended).** Crisis and change are communicated inward on the record's brand rule: what happened, the real impact, the fix, at the next brief of every open period, in person before in writing. A key-person departure is announced with its tension map.

### 11.6 The company-wide meeting

"Company-wide meetings should be scheduled sparingly... Reserve company meetings for a chance to hear from and emotionally connect with leaders and highlight critical business results and achievements" (Book p.133). Sŏn cannot hold one in her form without closing a service or finding a window every period's team can attend. The brief is a service period's all-hands, every service, and it does her routine job. What it cannot do is put the whole house in one room, and the record's Part I says why that matters: "great nights feel like distributed intelligence" because "every element in relationship with every other" (WP p.09), and a team that has never been in one room has fewer of those relationships across periods.

The team's recommendation: a whole-house gathering once a quarter, tied to the house review and, at its first two instances, to the principles revision Session 2 scheduled. It requires either a closed service period or a shared window, and either costs something the founders must weigh. No figure is stated.

**Position 11.7 (Founder-gated).** Whether Sŏn holds a quarterly whole-house gathering, and at what cost in closed service. The team recommends it, tied to the house review. Session 4 places it on the cadence if approved.

### 11.7 The team home

"By the time your company hits 150 people, you should have an internal company website" (Book p.128). Stripe Home is "a central place that makes it easier for employees to collaborate and find information" (Book p.134). The workbook's first exercise says where the operating system lives: "This information should be documented and easily accessible on your team's and division's internal homepages... be sure to include similar detail about your top objectives and metrics with targets for the year" (Workbook p.23).

Sŏn needs the same thing and must not confuse it with the OS surface. The OS surface is "the home where the software agents live" (WP p.21), a monitoring and operating surface for the leads. The team home is the human-readable face of the operating system: for every unit, the workbook's table, mission (or reason to exist), objectives with owner, key metrics, accountability mechanisms, operating cadence, and beside it the decision-rights register, the competency paths, the strategy's trade-offs, the definitions, and the standing vocabulary. It is what the single hiring sheet points to and what onboarding delivers. It is the web "rendered as a designed organizational artifact rather than a chart," which the record lists as an open item (WP p.15).

Whether the team home is a view on the OS surface or a separate surface depends on who owns the OS surface, which is founder-gated (S2, section 7.2). Its contents are not.

**Position 11.8 (Recommended).** The team home is the human-readable face of the operating system, specified in section 14, distinct from the OS surface in name and in purpose. Every team member can read it, in the languages the team speaks, between services.

**Position 11.9 (Founder-gated).** Where the team home lives relative to the OS surface. Inherits the OS surface ownership decision.

## 13. Findings inside Sŏn's record

### 13.1 The stack's data hub

The record: "Five layers. The stack is roughly four dozen bought tools across ten categories... The data layer is Airtable as the hub for everything that is not CRM, with SevenRooms as the customer identity spine underneath and Hang as the loyalty and customer-data engine on top of it. (Airtable is retired; financial figures come only from the Investor Review workbook.)

This program's source boundary states that the hub tool is no longer in use. The profile for this seat, written before the current boundary, instructs it to pull every count from that tool, never from memory.

The discrepancy: the record names a specific tool as the hub for everything that is not customer data, and the program's own boundary says the tool is out of use. The hub is load-bearing for this page. The metric layer (section 8) runs on data the hub holds; the shift close, the feedback channel's sync to "the execution system," the alert register, and the readiness test's row 15 all assume a hub exists and is running. If the hub has changed, the record's stack description is stale at its center, and the AI layer's description ("reading across the hub") is stale with it. If the hub has not changed, the program's boundary is wrong about the business. Either is possible from where this page sits. The page names the discrepancy and does not resolve it, because the stack is Dominic's domain outright.

**Position 13.1 (Founder-gated).** Dominic confirms the current data hub of record and whether the white paper's stack description at p.21 is current. Until then, every reference on this page to "the stack," "the system," or "the hub" is to whatever holds that role, without naming a tool.

### 13.2 Three service periods or four

Canon, Version 1.0: "At 207 E St. Elmo Rd, the building operates four dayparts under one master brand" (Canon 1.0, section 01), with a four-row table of early morning, lunch, dinner, and late night, and the header rule that "Every decision in this document is closed unless marked [HELD] or [DEFERRED]." Canon, Version 3.0: "One name. Four dayparts. One roof on St. Elmo Road" (Deck 3.0, p.02); "Four dayparts at one address" (Deck 3.0, p.06). Lunch has a full register, a theme in the token system, a menu structure, a uniform role, a sonic context and tempo range, and a persona-to-daypart matrix entry in both versions. Nothing about lunch is marked held or deferred in either version.

The white paper: "The core of it is three dayparts: early morning coffee, dinner, and late night... Lunch and brunch are held in reserve, not part of the core and not assumed" (WP p.35 to 36). "Lunch and brunch sit further back still, not to be revisited until the restaurant has operated for a full year, and whether lunch is ever built at all is an equal decision among all three partners, the chef partner included. If there is no good answer for lunch, it comes out of the plan entirely. That is a real option, not a courtesy" (WP p.36).

Is this the same fact at different altitudes? Partly. A design system must be ready for a period before the period opens, and canon specifying lunch's register is consistent with lunch being an option: the specification is what the period would be, not a decision that it will be. But canon does not say that. Its header rule makes every unmarked decision closed, and lunch is unmarked. Canon states lunch as an operating fact of the building; the white paper states it as an open option that may be removed by a decision that cannot yet be taken. On status, that is a disagreement, not an altitude difference. Brunch is a second, smaller disagreement: the white paper holds it in reserve beside lunch, and neither canon version mentions it at all.

For the operating system the question is exact: is it built for three service periods or four? This page's answer is that it is built for neither number. Under position 2.3 and position 12.1, row 17, the operating system runs whatever set of service periods holds a ratified daypart charter, and the count is a parameter, not an assumption. Dinner alone is the first configuration. That resolves the operating system's dependency without resolving the record's disagreement, which the partners own.

**Position 13.2 (Founder-gated).** Whether canon's four-daypart statement is a design readiness or a decision, and whether canon is versioned to mark lunch as held pending the three-partner decision the white paper reserves. Brandon and Dominic; the chef partner's vote on lunch itself is the white paper's rule. Routed to the canon reconciliation Session 2 filed.
