# S9 digest: diagnosing team state, team changes and restructuring, (re)building the team

Book pp. 282 to 303. Workbook pp. 90 to 92 (career conversations).

## Part 1: what the old page holds that's worth keeping

### Topic: diagnosing team state (book pp. 282 to 286)

- The white paper's own diagnostic reference sits outside any people section, at the end of Part I: "Use this as a diagnostic against any operational problem. Before intervening at the people level, identify which failure mode is actually present" (WP p.14). It names five modes, each with a stated fix: tension slack (a role occupied but not carrying load; fix is not replacing the person, but diagnosing which adjacent nodes are overloaded and re-establishing tension); node overload (one domain owner absorbing adjacent load, usually a manager doing operational work because a parallel structure blurred; fix is restoring a stated separation); sequence corruption (information not flowing when needed; fix is system maintenance, not people management); skill invisibility (advancement requirements undocumented, politics fills the vacuum; fix is documentation and access); cooling failure (bandwidth at zero, joy absent, retention suffering; this is a bandwidth problem, not a morale problem; fix is removing friction) (WP p.14). Three of the five fixes state explicitly they are not a person-level move.
- Behind the five modes sit: the four machine failures (CPU, memory, bus, cooling) as source (WP p.13); tension loss rather than slot vacancy on departure (WP p.8 to 9); the two parallel leads (WP p.10); the inverted web and invisible advocate (WP p.11); bandwidth as metabolic rate, leading indicators as the nervous system (WP p.12); a key departure's cost as "bounded, temporary, calculable" because knowledge stayed in the system (WP p.14).
- Option: build the restaurant's diagnostic as this WP p.14 reference, assembled into one instrument from what earlier sessions built around it (a standing quarterly scorecard, readiness-test mappings, check-in load triggers, recruiting-failure routing, training-infrastructure lines, room-designation triggers). Reasoning: the book's diagnosing chapter (a survey plus Lencioni's five dysfunctions plus a skill-will placement, book pp. 283 to 286) opens by reading the person; the white paper's rule is that the failure mode is identified before anyone intervenes at the person, on every read, at every rhythm, the old team held this rule of order should govern instead of the book's method, with the book's chapter consumed rather than replacing it.
- Research: a "diagnosis" at this restaurant reads five kinds of object, only one of which is a "team" in the ordinary sense: the whole company (the only true team, "distributed intelligence rather than coordinated direction," WP p.8); a manager's set of reports (a domain); the people named for one service (a service roster, closest to the book's "boat," dissolves at close); a role/seat on a staffing sheet; and a service period (opened by a charter). The person is a sixth object and is reached last, and only by one path (see below). The order every read follows: name the object; name the failure mode from a record source; name the mechanism the fix touches; only for tension slack, and only after the domain-level fix is made, may a read reach a person.
- Failure-mode evidence line (the unit of every read): seven fields only, mode (one of the five, never a blend); object (house/domain/service roster/seat/service period, never a person); signal source (must be an openable record: a close, a capture, a brief record, a check-in line, a schedule fact, an inventory row, a readiness row, a variety-pass entry); count (services, reviews, check-ins, never money, never a generated percentage); compensation (which adjacent seats/designations/mechanisms are absorbing the load); the record's named fix and the mechanism it touches; state (present / not present / cannot read yet, with reason). The line names no person, carries no verdict, and is written identically at every rhythm (weekly feeder, quarterly scorecard, reset).
- Book's survey-before-an-offsite method (book p.283, sent by "the team leader, an HR partner, or an outside facilitator"): Option: refuse as a form. Reasoning: a slower, self-reported channel duplicates what four faster mechanisms already read continuously (an employee pulse, a feedback channel, a check-in's own upward question, and the shift close written "at the edge by the people who felt the room"); it would fail a test of what a new mechanism has to buy to justify existing.
- Lencioni's five dysfunctions (book p.283) as the diagnostic frame: Option: refuse, for two reasons, and instead map each dysfunction (where present) to one of the five failure modes as a symptom with its own fix, rather than a conversation. Reasoning: (1) dysfunctions are read at a team's interior, which the "identify the mode before the person" rule forbids opening; (2) each dysfunction the book names is explainable as a symptom of a specific failure mode (e.g., absence of trust → sequence corruption or cooling failure depending on source; avoidance of accountability → node overload or a structural hole). The psychological-safety half of "absence of trust" is out of scope for this diagnostic; only its signal (capture/error-reporting rates) is read.
- Skill-will matrix (book pp. 284 to 286) and effort-impact matrix (book p.287): Option: refuse both as instruments used to place or prioritize people/work. Reasoning: the skill-will matrix opens at the person (forbidden order), asks for a "will" reading that isn't one of the fixed evaluation dimensions, and issues a verdict a routine check-in already refuses to make; a person is never placed on it. Its two team-level readings survive only as mechanism reads (skill ceiling with no next unlock = skill invisibility; overcommitment = goal-count/bandwidth flags), and its motivation questions route to other chunks (coaching/hard feedback; performance handling). The effort-impact matrix is absorbed only as the existing habit of periodically reviewing every mechanism against its own cost and benefit and continuing/changing/ending it; fix-ordering instead follows the modes' own causal order: sequence corruption first (a degraded nervous system corrupts every other read), node overload second, then skill invisibility and cooling failure, then tension slack last (the only mode whose read can reach a person, read only after the others are known).
- "Model the new behaviors and hold the team accountable to the plan" (book p.284): Option: refuse as a manager posture, replace with a versioned change plus a scheduled read-back on the next scorecard by the same source that raised the issue. A lead who holds a team to a plan "by presence" is read as node overload, not modeling.
- The book's "boat" callout (people a leader needs regardless of reporting line, book p.282): read as already answered by the service roster, the one cross-domain grouping the structure has for a single service; nothing needs to be separately assembled.
- Recurrence thresholds (generalizing three ad hoc rules already in use): one occurrence → a weekly feeder note; the same signal on the same object across three consecutive weekly leads' reviews → a partners'-review entry (possible node overload or structure question); the same mode on the same object on two consecutive quarterly scorecards after a fix was applied → a mechanism-reset agenda item (was the fix or the mode wrong); a designation held by one person every service for a quarter → a reset question about the seat/rule; a mode present at any phase-gate readiness review → the gate does not open around it. All thresholds are counts of reviews/services, never money.
- A domain that repeats (research/method): when a domain shows recurring open-seat, tension-loss, or hold-open signals, the read moves up from seat to domain and asks, in order, before any hiring question: is the seat count itself wrong (a charter/count problem)? is the staffing-sheet description wrong (entry horizon, shift shape, physical demands set above what the labor pool holds)? is the domain lead's own seat overloaded (absorbing the room/door/rotation work)? Only after those three does any hiring-quality question apply, and it is answered by a separate hiring-calibration mechanism, never by this diagnostic. A domain producing repeated departures is read as cooling failure first, not as a reflection on its lead's character (the record's own rule: universal problems are design problems, WP p.17).
- Rhythm/sequencing risk and dependency: the diagnostic is designed to run on four rhythms that already exist (weekly team-lead review, quarterly companywide review's scorecard section, an annual/first-quarter/first-year "mechanism reset" as the correction point, and phase-gate readiness reviews) and adds no new meeting. Inside a stated first-quarter "freeze" period (protecting the room's cadence mechanisms while the first quarter of dinner service runs), the diagnostic may read everything but change almost nothing on the room's mechanism map; anything it would otherwise change is logged and held for the first reset, with its date, so the reset opens on a list rather than memory.
- Pre-opening first run (research on sequencing before any team exists): before opening, a first run of the diagnostic is possible using the readiness test's failed rows, a pre-opening structural gap-mapping pass ("variety pass"), staffing-sheet gaps, and the pre-opening hiring pipeline; it should also record, with equal standing, what it cannot yet read (no occupied roles yet for tension slack, no pulse baseline, no shift-close log, no recurrence history). One pre-opening signal is explicitly node overload from a stated moment: once domain leads are in the building a set number of weeks before opening, any seat still opened or hiring packet still decided by the pre-opening hiring lead in that domain is node overload at the center, and the first run records the date load handed over.
- Findings, internal tensions in the record's own account, relevant to how a diagnostic reads at Sŏn (kept because they bear on decisions to make, not resolved by the old team):
  - The four "machine failure" modes (WP p.13) and the five diagnostic failure modes (WP p.14) don't map one-to-one; "memory failure" (knowledge leaving with a departing person) has no direct twin among the five modes. A departure's cost is called "calculable" (WP p.14) but no unit, owner, or method for calculating it appears anywhere in the record.
  - Cooling failure's stated evidence includes retention (WP p.14), but the record elsewhere treats retention as a lagging/"X-ray" indicator (WP p.12) read over time, not a real-time diagnostic signal, a tension between what the diagnostic is told to read and what the record calls too slow to diagnose from.
  - The record says a departing person's institutional knowledge "does not leave, it lives in the system" (WP p.14) but elsewhere says customer relationships belong to the person and may follow them to another location (WP p.25/27), two accounts of what a departure actually costs.
  - The record commits to running feedback "both directions" and reading exit interviews "for what could have been better" (WP p.19, p.23), but names no reader, rhythm, or destination for either, both are currently dead ends for a diagnostic that would otherwise use them.
  - The record says cross-training is "career development" for everyone (WP p.19) but elsewhere says cross-functional aptitude is rare and some people are simply better suited to one side of the operation (WP pp. 10 to 11), a tension for how a skill-invisibility read should treat someone who hasn't cross-trained.
  - The Maitre d role is described as owning the "invisible advocate function" for development in one place (WP p.10) and as "the system is the advocate, no one's advancement waits on a manager's attention" in another (WP p.11), unclear whether skill-invisibility findings have a named seat-owner or are purely systemic.
  - Tension slack is defined as compensation happening "without visibility" (WP p.14), yet the system's whole premise is that it lets problems be felt early (WP p.12), meaning tension slack's evidence has to be reconstructed indirectly from records kept for other purposes, since the system as designed doesn't surface it directly.
- Stripped from this topic: a company-values/non-negotiables framing and a "training philosophy" requirement used to test the skill-invisibility fix, both resting on excluded brand material; a vocabulary conflict between a floor-service term for "reading customers intuitively" and the word "diagnostic," resting on the same excluded brand material; a "living documents change only by versioned decision" citation from the excluded brand deck used to distinguish daily process updates from formal changes (the underlying distinction, routine fix-and-log versus a formal versioned change, is kept above without the brand citation).

### Topic: team changes and restructuring (book pp. 287 to 293)

- Book's reorganization triggers: structure not matching strategy, and a talent issue (a leader leaving/changing roles), plus a misused third trigger, treating an underperforming team as if restructuring itself would fix it (book pp. 288 to 289). Option: there is no "org chart" to reorganize in the book's sense; growth here is adding service periods and locations, not layers. Reasoning: the first trigger's only real-world form is a new service period being formally chartered (which can add seats/roles but only through that formal process); the second trigger is simply a departure, handled as tension re-establishment (below) with a lead change carried on the departing/arriving person's own record, not a wholesale rethink of the domain; the book's warning about the third trigger is exactly the record's own rule, identify the failure mode before treating it as a people problem, and a domain that keeps failing is diagnosed (see "a domain that repeats" above), never restructured to mask the real cause.
- Book's "don't leave the ice cream on the counter" speed rule (book p.290, act fast once others are involved because uncertainty breeds instability): Option: absorb as the required pace for any change entry, proposed one week, decided the same or next week, reason written before the proposal, so nothing sits in limbo. The book's secrecy-phase advice (very few people informed early) has no real equivalent needed, because the actual order already controls disclosure: a change is first discussed at the team-lead review, then told to the directly affected person at their private check-in (before any written record or floor announcement), and only then reflected in written records or spoken to the floor, a change that reaches the floor before the affected person's own check-in is treated as a process failure.
- Book's caution that reorg timing always feels wrong, and to avoid re-reorganizing too soon after "turning up the heat" (book pp. 290 to 291): Option: timing is already governed by two existing rules, structural changes happen at formal gates and at the periodic "reset," while seat/role changes can happen in between; and nothing in the day-to-day operating rhythm changes during the first quarter of a new service's life. The book's advice to be transparent about an admittedly imperfect interim structure is already the practice for pre-opening structural gaps that get temporarily routed to a founder.
- Book's "is this the person you want to break your structure for?" (book p.291): Option: refuse entirely and by design. Reasoning: staffing-sheet rows carry no names; a role may not be invented or retitled to fit a specific person; a floor "designation" (a temporary functional assignment) is never made permanent; a lead role vacancy is filled from an internal readiness pool with a written justification if it isn't, closing off any path by which a valued person's threat to leave, or a desire to reward them, could bend the structure. What survives from the book is its own warning that a team will lose trust if it believes structure is being optimized for one individual.
- Book's three-phase reorg process with a clock (design phase, conversations phase, announcement phase; book pp. 292 to 294): Option: refuse the phases and clock as such. What survives: the design phase's "every person accounted for" becomes a documented before/after list of affected roles; the conversations phase becomes the affected person's private check-in (plus a scheduled follow-up) rather than a special "reorg conversation," with the person's own reaction recorded in their words; the announcement phase becomes: tell those most affected first, then the wider team hears it structurally (not a formal "announcement") the next time it's operationally relevant, e.g., stated at a routine team briefing.
- Moving people, merging/splitting groups, and communicating change (book p.294): Option: people move roles only through the existing internal-mobility process (an internal candidate pool plus a hands-on trial), never by simple reassignment; merging two domains under one leader is refused outright as a step backward from having distinct operational leads; splitting a domain (adding a new leadership line) is refused except through the formal structural-gap process tied to a phase gate. How deeply the wider team should be told about changes beyond the minimum described here is left as an open question for a different chunk (communication norms).
- Departure = "tension re-establishment," not a backfill problem (WP p.8 to 9, p.14): Option: read a departure through the failure-mode diagnostic rather than as a straightforward vacancy. Reasoning/method: in the week of a departure, read in order, a note on the staffing record of what the departing role actually held; a note on the weekly leads' review of what adjacent roles are now carrying; a check of the internal readiness pool before posting the role externally; and, over following weeks, whether adjacent roles' workload signals rise (showing where the tension actually redistributed). A second read asks whether what the departing person held was actually captured in the restaurant's own systems (process documentation, customer preferences, cross-training records) or lived only in their head, anything "in their head" is itself a finding (a documentation gap or an undocumented process), which is how the record's claim that a departure's cost is "bounded, calculable" gets made concrete rather than asserted. A third read, once the role is refilled, checks whether the adjacent-role strain actually falls back down, that's how the diagnosis of a departure formally "closes."
  - What is told to the floor about a departure: Option, read consistently across sources with some prior wording narrowed: the floor hears a short, factual coverage note at the next team briefing (which role is open, who's covering its functions this shift) with nothing about the departed person, no reason given, no farewell, no judgment; this is treated as routine operational coverage information, not an announcement about a person leaving.
- An open seat as a load reading on the domain, not (yet) a recruiting problem: Option: before treating an unfilled role as a hiring failure, read the adjacent roles' strain signals, whether other people are now double-covering functions the open role used to hold, and whether the staffing sheet itself (entry requirements, shift shape, physical demands) describes a role the labor market doesn't actually supply. Only after that is it treated as a recruiting/hiring-calibration question. A special case: when a lead deliberately holds a role open rather than lower the hiring bar to fill it, the diagnostic reads whether adjacent roles' strain keeps climbing over consecutive weekly reviews, if so, the answer under a stated "bandwidth before hiring corners" principle is to reduce coverage/hours elsewhere before lowering the bar, a decision the old team treated as needing sign-off from the top rather than being made unilaterally by the diagnostic.
- A designation (a temporary functional assignment, distinct from a formal role) held by the same person on every shift for a full quarter: read at the reset as a question about (a) that lead role's own scheduling/hours, (b) whether only one person is actually eligible/trained to hold the assignment (a training gap), or (c) whether the lead is simply naming the same person by preference (in which case a minimum-rotation rule should be written into how the assignment is granted). Explicitly never treated as evidence that the assignment should become a permanent role, and never treated as a performance read on the person holding it.

### Topic: (re)building the team (book pp. 294 to 303)

- Book's premise: "the bedrock of your team is the people on it," and a manager's job after inheriting/restructuring/hiring a team is to personally evaluate each person's skill and will (book p.294). Contrasting record claim: an organization that holds knowledge in its systems gets smarter as it ages, while one that holds knowledge in people resets with every departure, and the cost of losing a person falls as the system holds more of what people know (WP p.8, p.14). Option: treat "rebuilding" as something the system does through documented processes and an internal readiness pool, not something a manager does by re-evaluating people. Reasoning: there's no "new team" to build after a departure in the book's sense, no headcount growth is implied by rebuilding, only maintaining an existing complement of roles; new hires structurally enter at the outer edge, not the center; only lead roles are ever hired externally, and only pre-opening (afterward, only with documented proof no one internal is ready), meaning rebuilding a lead position is primarily an internal-pool question, not a recruiting one.
- Book's "deeply understand each team member" (forming a manager's own impression of skill/will, book p.294): Option: refuse a manager's personal impression as a mechanism, and place "understanding a person" onto four things where the person speaks in their own words instead: a persistent personal record written and read back at check-ins; the check-in conversation itself, including a standing "what's in your way" question; a "working with me" document that exists only for leads (never for hourly staff), so a lead's own working style is self-declared, never a subordinate's; and a career conversation (below). None of these four permit a lead's own read of anyone's skill or will to be written down anywhere.
- Rebuilding a domain after its lead leaves, or inheriting one: Option: a departing lead is handled through the same departure process as anyone else (tension re-establishment, internal pool checked first), plus a formal record of the leadership change; book's picture of an arriving manager forming impressions of an "inherited" team is refused, an arriving lead reads the domain's existing records, structure, and open items, and gets to know people only through what those people have written in their own words, not through the new lead's own evaluation.
- A new lead arriving into a running domain (book p.283's "especially important to take stock" when starting to manage a new team): Option: an arriving lead reads, in order, the domain's structure (open roles, gaps, unresolved decision-rights items), its plan (goals, projects in flight), and its history of diagnostic evidence, and reads the people only through their own existing written words. Their first concrete read is arithmetic, not judgment: whether the number of check-ins their new seat is responsible for is even feasible against their actual working schedule (an early overload warning sign); their second read is who currently holds which informal floor-level assignments and how often. They perform no personal skill/will assessment, run no survey, and write nothing about anyone before their first scheduled one-on-one.
- Open dependency (flagged, not resolved): diagnosing a lead's own overload is described as "half-blind" until it's decided who conducts a lead's own one-on-one check-in, the diagnostic can read the outside evidence of a lead being overloaded (missed/moved check-ins, escalations, things falling through), but the one direct signal (a lead's own "what's in my way") has no assigned recipient yet, and a founder's personal impression of a lead is explicitly not allowed to substitute as a source.
- Delegation (book pp. 296 to 303): book frames delegation as a manager consciously handing work down, covered in one-on-ones, using an impact/"trapdoor" (reversibility) matrix to decide what to keep versus delegate, sometimes formalized as a written "DRI" (directly responsible individual). Option: refuse manager-held delegation as a concept; authority instead is pre-placed with whichever role is the closest competent owner via a decision-rights structure that states in advance what a role may decide alone, what triggers escalation, and to whom, set one level broader than the role strictly needs. Reasoning: a role's own staffing sheet already states its scope of work and decision range; nothing about "the job" needs covering in a one-on-one, and one-on-ones are designed to refuse task assignment altogether. A "DRI" is refused as redundant: a named decision-owner with a stated range already carries more accountability than a DRI does, and a DRI without actual decision authority is exactly the kind of "responsible but not empowered" pattern the record forbids.
  - Book's under-delegating warning signs (decisions bottleneck on the manager; things fall apart when the manager is away; the manager feels overwhelmed/firefighting; people bring problems but not solutions, book p.298) and over-delegating signs (consistently low-quality output; problems surface too late; reports feel overwhelmed; manager can't summarize recent team output, book p.299): Option: read each sign as evidence of a specific failure mode rather than as a delegation habit to adjust, e.g., decision bottlenecks read as a decision-rights range being too narrow or unstated; things falling apart in the manager's absence reads as node overload; low-quality output reads as a training/readiness gap; late-surfacing problems read as sequence corruption (information not reaching a review in time); team members' own overwhelm reads as cooling failure. None of the eight signs is treated as something a manager fixes by simply "delegating more" or "delegating less."
  - Book's impact/trapdoor delegation framework (book pp. 300 to 301): Option: absorb only the underlying test, not the four-quadrant tool, an outcome that would be reversible-but-high-impact if wrong, i.e., a "trapdoor," must be built into the role's stated escalation triggers so it's never left inside a role's ordinary decision range by default.
  - Book's three reasons a manager might keep doing "delegable" work themselves, modeling, urgency, resource constraints (book pp. 301 to 302): translated respectively to: paired-service mentoring/shadowing rather than a manager doing the work to demonstrate it; a documented incident-and-fix process for urgent issues rather than manager intervention; and treating "I'm not scaling my team adequately" as itself a node-overload finding requiring a systemic fix, not personal overwork.
  - Book's seven-step delegation conversation (book pp. 302 to 303): Option: mostly absorbed into the format for logging a formal process change (the change stated in one line, its owner, the date, what it displaces); two of the seven steps refused outright, "explain why this assignment is right for the person" (because assignments go to roles by domain, not to people for development reasons) and "get buy-in" (because accepting a role means accepting its stated decision range up front; there's nothing separate to buy into per task).
- The career conversation (book pp. 294 to 296; Workbook pp. 90 to 92): book's version is a single scheduled ~60-minute "get to know you" conversation, held a few months into the relationship and before any formal performance review, that walks the person's life history (childhood, school, past jobs) through to a five-year projection, closing with the manager writing down two or three development goals to track together; it's meant to serve later as a reference point at reorg/review/delegation moments.
  - Contrasting record claim: the path to advancement is meant to be fully visible and documented without anyone needing to ask, and "advancement requires preparation, not permission" (WP p.11, p.14), a conversation whose purpose is for a manager to learn someone's ambitions in order to decide what they should focus on cuts against a system explicitly designed to remove the manager's personal attention from that judgment. The record does separately call for a periodic review focused on "the path forward" and frames cross-training explicitly as career development.
  - Option: replace the book's conversation with one scheduled conversation, held once, in the window after a new hire's initial probationary/ramp period closes and before their first formal review, paid as ordinary working time, roughly 30 minutes as a starting parameter, between the person and their direct lead. It opens on the person's own existing self-written notes about what they want next (from an earlier check-in) plus documents they already have access to (their role's advancement criteria, visible role-progression tracks, cross-training record), adding nothing they haven't already been told. It produces: a re-confirmed or re-written personal statement of what the person wants next, one specific next track named in their own words, and a note of what the business itself needs to provide to support that (which becomes a training/infrastructure gap if the business hasn't supplied it yet).
  - Explicitly refused: a five-year projection ("which causes most of us to shut down," book p.295); any title, level, rank, or promise of a specific role; any promise or discussion of pay; a date commitment beyond existing progression timelines; questions about the person's life or history before joining the company (echoing the book's origin/school/family questions, book p.91), refused on the reasoning that hiring criteria never asked for that history, and a manager probing personal/family background after the person is already hired risks exactly the kind of overreach the company's stated people principles exist to prevent; development goals written in the manager's own words rather than the person's; and any use of the conversation's content as an input to a later delegation, placement, or assignment decision. Book's three stated purposes for the conversation ("establishing that you care," learning a person's narrative arc, creating a shared long-term reference point) are each translated: "establishing you care" is refused as an explicit purpose (care is meant to already be structural, not performed in a special conversation); the "narrative arc" becomes simply the person's own stated near-term interest; the "shared reference point" becomes the ongoing personal record itself, revisited at ordinary check-ins whenever something relevant changes (a new opportunity opens, a training milestone is hit), not re-held as a special conversation.
  - Note: this design point (refusing personal-history questions post-hire) is close to an HR/legal-adjacent judgment call about what's appropriate to ask employees about their personal background, worth flagging as a question to confirm with counsel or an HR advisor rather than treating as purely a design preference, though the old material does not itself frame it as a formal counsel question.
