## 1. Her ask against the record, and how much is decided

### 1.1 What she asks

Her thirty-five pages are two chapters in one. The first is managing managers: move "from being a captain to a colonel" (Book p.449); be a manager's "coach, sounding board, and unblocker" (p.450); answer a management problem with "What are you going to do?" and Toyota's Five Whys (p.450); shift the 1:1 from technical to adaptive questions, with Table 10 pairing an individual contributor's questions (where is my time going, how do I solve X) against a manager's (what is success in a year, do we have the right systems, do I have the right team) (pp. 450 to 451); make the 1:1 "their time," on a mutual document with action items, career notes, and the report's agenda (p.451); start every 1:1 by asking how someone is doing and taking the most important item first (p.451); when you are not the expert, be a thinking partner on strategy, communication, and problem-solving, introduce them to leaders, get them resources, unblock them, do skip levels, and set expectations in a "Working with Me" document (pp. 452 to 453); spend time on the ground, two hours on a team's daily tasks or a founder's "engineerication" of several days (pp. 453 to 454).

The second is managing out, firing, and layoffs. Return to outcome 3, where the person "needs to leave the company" (p.454); most of the time manage out, meaning "you and your report come to the conclusion that they should leave," and rarely hold a formal termination conversation with support from your manager and HR (p.454); "fire fast but fire well," because bad hires linger and "high performers will see that low performers aren't being managed out" (p.455); follow three steps, pre-work (assemble written feedback and any prior plan, learn local employment law, inform your manager and HR), the departure conversation, and logistics (pp. 455 to 456); at a junior level, summarize the plan, ask how they think it went, and say "I haven't seen enough progress... I want to agree on a timeline for your departure," which "is not up for discussion," then move to constructive tactics in at most two sittings, with two exceptions, a legal threat and new information (pp. 456 to 458); at a senior level, hold two or three conversations over months in which the leader may "see the writing on the wall," negotiate the departure against "a severance matrix, the framework your HR and employment legal teams should have developed," and let HR or counsel "run interference" (pp. 458 to 459); on a legal threat, know your country's law, pause at "retaliation" or "discrimination," and bring in HR and legal (pp. 459 to 460); fire for gross misconduct in one conversation with same-day departure after an investigation whose conclusion the person hears within about a day (pp. 460 to 461), and "yes, this means you need a code of conduct" (p.461); run a layoff as "a role elimination due to restructuring," directed by HR and legal, with tightly managed communications (p.461); when something bad happens to an employee, be empathetic, ask what they need, take over their work, observe an ongoing hardship and share an observation without prying, know your legal obligations on harassment and discrimination, keep it confidential, and never fail to act (pp. 461 to 465); and, in her final thoughts, remember that management "touches people" and that its reward arrives "months or even years later" (pp. 465 to 466). The workbook's Managing Out Checklist is eight boxes: feedback, documentation, a formal process if relevant, local regulations, inform your manager and HR, "the coaching-out conversation," the timeline and communication plan, the announcement and transition plan (Workbook p.124).

Every page assumes a manager of managers who holds 1:1s with managers and does skip levels; a company that can fire fast after a documented case; an HR function and employment lawyers who run separations; a severance matrix and exit packages as negotiating tools; a layoff as a funded company's restructuring; and a manager who handles a person's crisis with discretion and HR beside them.
