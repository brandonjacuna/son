# S6 digest: hiring (book p.193 to 215)

## Part 1: what the old page holds that's worth keeping

### What the record already fixes, upstream of this chunk

- Stages are fixed by seat class: every seat gets application, first interview, decision, answer; high-volume seats add a phone screen; physical-skill seats add a paid practical; leadership and salaried seats add a dinner in the room; domain-owner seats add an adjacent-domain second interviewer; pre-opening every seat adds a founder stage.
- The four reads are fixed and assigned per stage: competency for the strand at entry level; growth potential as self-directed mastery; cultural alignment (no evidence of forbidden conduct, comfort with distributed authority, comfort with the customer as customer); what the building must supply for this person to succeed.
- No hiring committee, no hiring meeting, no recruiting seat: the decision is the domain lead's, on a complete packet of independently submitted scorecards, made the day the packet completes, answer sent the same day. The applicant tracking system refuses a decision on an incomplete packet.
- A decline names a read or a must-have and nothing else. A lead who is one of two first-interview interviewers requires the second. A hole in a seat is held rather than filled below the bar.
- Five instruments are refused outright, each with something that does its work instead: a work-style/personality grid (may return only as a self-awareness tool in onboarding, never in interviews); résumé review and take-home assignments; the hiring committee and hiring meeting; an outside recruiter's interview loop and scorecard; a bias-neutralizing copy tool.
- Interviewer training is an obligation of the house, recorded on the person's page, enforced by the software, with a re-training trigger when a scorecard comes back with no named read.
- WP p.18: "Interviewers are trained, because asking a good question and reading the answer is a learned skill, not an instinct that arrives with a title. Interviews are semi-structured with documented questions, and they assess specific competencies, cultural alignment, and growth potential rather than gut feeling. The goal is not only whether this is the right person, but whether the building is equipped to create the environment this person needs to succeed."
- WP p.18: "For roles with a physical skill component, the second interview is a paid practical in a real environment... Speed is respect. When the skills and the alignment are there, the decision is made quickly and development starts immediately... For leadership and salaried roles, the final round is a dinner in the room, which is evaluation and recruitment at once... No one is ghosted."
- WP p.17: self-scheduling, language choice, format choice, phone screen for high-volume roles, no unanswered applications, the transparency sheet at the first interview.
- Structural context: new people always enter at the outside edge (WP p.9); every position has documented competency requirements visible to anyone (WP p.11); advancement requires preparation, not permission (WP p.14); the two leads are parallel, neither reports to the other (WP p.10); the review system develops people rather than judging them, reviewing every three to six months on development and growth rather than a verdict, feedback running both directions (WP p.19); the training system is a learning platform any qualified team member can build modules on, with competency validation before anyone touches a table (WP p.19).
- Financial figures (salary bands, compa-ratio, pay parity) are explicitly out of scope for this program; wherever a decision needs a figure, that gap is named rather than filled.

### Book's ask (p.193-215) and how much of it the record already answers

- Book p.193-194: interviewing is a learnable skill best run inside a set process; her process guidelines (clear job description, decide who interviews, an eight-person ceiling reduced to four or five for entry-level/well-understood roles, trained interviewers, a shared rubric with elements split across interviewers, a kickoff for custom roles, a narrowing first round, a hiring committee or candidate review, references by recruiter and hiring manager, the offer).
- Book p.194-196: consistency ("the same questions of every candidate... while also leaving space to probe") as her most effective interviewing tactic; STAR as the probe framework; four common mistakes (not using the rubric, not interviewing for the needed capabilities, assessing liking over role-success, over-weighting candidate experience over trajectory/aptitude); the five things the best interviews suss out; the self-awareness test ("how would your colleagues describe you," probing for constructive feedback and what the candidate did about it); the "I" vs. "we" read; three question types (situational, behavioral, strict competency); working values into questions without naming them; the written exercise; candidate experience as the thing most candidates will ever know of the company.
- Book p.196-198: non-leader roles (a wide interviewer pool for scaled roles, the same small panel for infrequent roles); named outside-reviewer/high-performer interviewer programs; an executive offer-approval channel with a fast turnaround commitment; consultative decision-making with the hiring manager as primary decider plus checks and balances; job-level selection as the next step after selecting a candidate.
- Book p.203-209: candidate-selection committee practice (live discussion mainly on disagreement/newness/uniqueness; a standing veto right for anyone on the committee); the steward's four goals (guard candidate experience, listen to interviewers, bring the organization along, decide for the company); reference-checking rules ("not making reference calls is a mistake," a lukewarm reference is disqualifying, the percentile question, seven behavioral reference questions); the offer (verbal before written, nothing a surprise, an automatable legal document, a "sell chat").
- Book p.209-215 (leadership hiring): transparency with limits, layering, candidate confidentiality, a CEO decision on a consultative process run as change management (two excited stakeholders, identified stakeholders, a feedback-collection loop, an announcement), the interview "experience trap" (experienced candidates interview well; probe with context questions, "what did you specifically do," and "did it work"), hiring disagreements in both directions, references and offers for leaders, negotiation handled by someone other than the CEO/hiring manager.
- Book p.213-214 (internal hiring): decide whether there's one internal candidate or several before running any process; don't run a sham process for a foregone conclusion; a lightweight internal loop for multiple candidates with a feedback loop for those not chosen; meeting a few external candidates to show rigor; confidentiality; the announcement.
- Workbook p.43-77: a full worked hiring framework (recruiter screen, team screen with a written project, four onsite interviews with named runners/competencies/lengths, three-to-four-column rubrics with anchors), sample questions, a graded written exercise, and a five-step candidate-review decision framework.
- Honest accounting of what applies: roughly a third of the book's material is absorbed nearly whole because the record already asks for it (trained interviewers, consistency, documented questions with STAR probes, three question types, values worked in unnamed, candidate/interviewer prep, the "experience trap" counters, verbal-before-written, no surprises, references where reference calls make sense, no sham process, leadership-candidate confidentiality). Roughly a third is refused as form though its underlying function is worked elsewhere (the hiring committee, the candidate-review panel, named interviewer programs, the offer-approval channel, job levels and everything downstream, the veto as a standing right, the sell chat as a performed conversation, external validation interviews for internal hires, a "not sure" response). The remaining third doesn't apply because Sŏn lacks the thing it assumes (a recruiting function, an organization to bring along, a person being "layered," a board, job ladders, and a volume of hires/years of performance data to calibrate against).
- The hardest inheritance: an earlier chunk refused a formal candidate-review panel for a house of roughly eight leadership lines (WP p.22). The book's candidate-review mission is "a consistent, efficient, and transparent decision-making framework that seeks to control for bias" (book p.205), that's four functions, not a form, and a house that refuses the panel still owes each function a home or an honest statement that it has none (see "function mapping" below).

### What an interview may and may not conclude

- May conclude: whether evidence for each assigned read was heard against the entry bar; whether a disqualifier was present; what the candidate said they need from the building; a recommendation with a read named.
- May not conclude: a felt cultural fit not tied to a named read; presence, charm, energy, or "how the room would feel" about a candidate; a competency the house is supposed to develop rather than require at entry (scoring that as absent scores the house's own obligation as the candidate's failure); tempo/procedure reproduction (only the paid practical can conclude that); month-six success (that's what the onboarding plan and check-ins are for); anything from a candidate's chosen interview format; an offer, a figure, or a level.
- Book allows an interviewer to weigh "trajectory and aptitude to learn" against experience (p.195); Sŏn's sheets carry no experience requirement at all, so there's nothing to weigh it against. Book allows an ambiguous founder "no" on a candidate who's "just fine" (p.171); the record here removes it, a no with no read named is itself a re-training trigger for the interviewer.
- Book's four interviewer mistakes (not using the rubric; not interviewing for the needed capability; assessing liking over role-fit; over-weighting candidate experience) map to four failure modes the training program names and the calibration read scans for: the unasked question, the misfiled evidence, a subjective "presence" word on a scorecard, and reaching for years-of-experience where the sheet has none to reach for.

### The loop, stage by stage, research, sequencing, and options

- Candidate-facing stage note: what a stage is and is for, its length (and the house then keeps to it), who's present by seat (never by a title implying rank), format/language confirmed, what the candidate will be asked to do, what the stage reads and does not read, what to wear, how/when they'll hear (a five-day window), and that they may ask questions, never the questions themselves or the anchors. Book's version: she sent candidates a guide describing what to expect, and found candidates appreciated it (p.193).
- Interviewer prep note: the current seat sheet, the stage's kit and anchors, only the reads that stage assesses, must-haves/disqualifiers, the candidate's chosen language/format, what the candidate was already told, the candidate's fourth-read answer carried forward, and the interviewer's own training currency, but never another interviewer's scorecard or conclusion. Book's version explicitly allows sending "brief feedback, but not so much that you create bias" (p.212); this is refused in favor of no prior-interviewer feedback at all, since independent, unbiased submission before any discussion is what replaces a committee.
- Book's interviewer-count ceiling (max eight people, four or five for well-understood/entry-level roles, p.193) is not the binding constraint here, every current seat class sits under it. The binding constraint instead is a floor: no packet should be one person's read alone. Open question, filed rather than resolved: if a future seat class would produce a one-scorecard packet (no physical skill, no leadership, no adjacent domain), does its first interview get a second interviewer added.
- Book's kickoff meeting to align everyone on a bespoke role, rubric, and their part (p.194) and her narrowing "first round of three" panel structure (p.194) don't apply, there's no panel to protect and the documented seat sheet functions as the kickoff.
- Phone screen: reads must-haves and availability only, run by a trained interviewer from the seat's domain who isn't the domain lead; roughly fifteen minutes; scripted order (seat/strand statement, each must-have as an open behavioral question, availability by period, legal requirements, next-stage language/format, candidate questions); may not conclude anything about manner, voice, or "sounding like the house." Risk named: the phone screen is where a subjective "sounds right" bias enters cheapest, since a voice on a call is nearly all first-impression and there's little time for a candidate's story to correct it, the calibration read should scan phone-screen declines for reasons not on the seat sheet.
- First interview: 45 minutes for edge seats, 60 for domain-owner/lead seats, run in a fixed order (one background question, then the four reads in a fixed sequence ending on "what does the building need to supply," then the transparency sheet read aloud with the compensation-mechanics item stated as filled or "being finalized," then canditate questions, then next steps). A cut-short interview should be re-run rather than rushed. The prior-employer question is asked as "what would you have changed about that place" rather than inviting a candidate to name and potentially indict a specific person. The pay-range portion is read as what the seat holder decides, never as a list of restrictions.
- Realist's load note: pre-opening, only the founders (then two leads) run these forty-five-to-sixty-minute interviews for an entire cohort of floor and kitchen seats, on top of running the build itself, the hiring calendar has to schedule this as real blocked time, not assumed slack.
- Adjacent-domain second interviewer (domain-owner seats): assesses the same four reads from the interface side (what they need from the seat beside theirs, what they hand off and in what state, what they do when the adjacent seat is short-staffed); interface evidence files under the competency read, not as a fifth read; submits an independent second scorecard.
- Lead-seat first interview: replaces the strand-competency block with a block testing whether the candidate can hold a domain with no one above or below them, read a problem as a systems question first, and say what they'd refuse to do. Book's "experience trap" governs technique: watch whether an experienced candidate asks about context before answering a scenario, always ask "what did you specifically do," always ask whether the project succeeded (book p.214).
- Dinner in the room and founder stage: the dinner reads the candidate as a customer (their treatment of the server, their read of the room, whether they hold the table without performing for the partners) via a small documented list (one open question about what they noticed of the service, one question about a great customer experience elsewhere, one deliberate silence), not a scripted interview. The founder stage reads only the non-negotiables and the "what does the building need to supply" question, in a short check-in format, with three fixed questions; a founder's "no" must name a specific gap or it isn't a valid no.
- Hiring calendar as gates, not dates (carries dependency chain): the chef seat ruling is upstream of the entire kitchen pipeline (menu, kitchen seat sheets, kitchen practicals, kitchen hires, training, dinner's opening gate), no link is skippable. Both leads must be in the building at least six weeks before dinner's gate (so the leads' review has six consecutive weeks of records). The compensation architecture is on the critical path: the transparency sheet with pay mechanics is handed at the first interview, so the leads' first interviews (which happen months before a downstream compensation-ratification session could plausibly run) can't be offered against until that architecture exists, meaning ratification has to be pulled ahead of its default place in sequence, or the leads' offers (and dinner's gate) wait. This sequencing conflict is named as a founder decision, not resolved. What the calendar refuses categorically: choosing a start date first and scheduling a practical to hit it; running a lead's first interview before their interviewer training is recorded; making an offer while the compensation-mechanics sheet item reads "being finalized"; running a kitchen practical before the menu exists; opening a gate around a failed readiness check.

### The questions and anchors (interview kit), what it needs to specify

- Consistency rule: every documented question asked of every candidate, in kit order, in the candidate's chosen language; a skipped question is recorded as skipped with a reason, not left blank.
- Every read's question set should contain at least one situational, one behavioral, and (where the read permits) one strict-competency question; probes follow a situation/task/action/result shape; "what did you actually hold/do" is the mandatory probe against vague "we" answers.
- Values/principles should be worked into behavioral questions without naming them (book p.196), this can't be built yet because company principles beyond the baseline conduct standard aren't ratified (see item on ratifying principles below); until ratified, the cultural-alignment read runs on baseline conduct and distributed-authority questions only.
- Every anchor should be written at three evidence levels (below entry bar, at it, beyond it) and read a capacity that could show up in any domain (a kitchen, a shop, a classroom, a family), not experience specific to fine dining, because sheets carry no experience requirement. No anchor at any level should contain a subjective "presence" word (energy, warmth, charisma, polish, confidence, "would fit in"); a candidate's own word choices are not scored either way.
- Interview kit questions live on the public seat sheet (so a candidate can prepare); the anchors themselves are not public.
- First-read (competency) questions differ by whether a seat is outward-facing (customer-facing) or inward-facing (behind-the-scenes/support), each with roughly four questions built around noticing needs before being told, describing something familiar in one's own words, handling competing demands at once, and recalling being on the receiving end of good/bad service.
- Second-read (self-directed growth) questions: book's three-part self-awareness question (how colleagues would describe you, what constructive feedback you've gotten, what you did about it) as an anchor question at every first interview; a question about something the candidate mastered on their own initiative, unprompted by any program; a question describing the actual self-paced advancement mechanism and asking what the candidate would do with it in month one.
- Third-read (conduct) questions: behavioral questions surfacing whether a candidate normalizes exploitation/toxicity or treats people as interchangeable, without naming those words; questions testing comfort with genuinely being handed authority to resolve a problem without asking permission; for customer-facing seats, a question about what a customer is owed and a time the candidate had to be honest with one.
- Fourth-read questions (what the building must supply): language for interviewing/training, shift-shape needs, how the candidate learns best, what got in their way at a past job that wasn't their fault, what they'd need on day one but be embarrassed to ask for, and (worded by the person owning compliance, with counsel) anything about stated physical job demands the candidate wants accounted for. Recorded in the candidate's words, never scored on the candidate, and carried forward to their onboarding check-ins.
- The outgoing-profile distortion (risk/failure mode): later, higher-energy service periods select for a more extroverted profile, and the people doing later hiring have themselves been selected that way, creating a self-reinforcing bias toward one kind of personality that the anchors, phone-screen design, and practical scoring are built to resist but can't fully catch on their own; the calibration read's cross-scorecard pattern check (scanning for a "presence" word lexicon, and for whether behind-the-scenes seats decline candidates at a different rate than customer-facing ones) is named as the only mechanism that can see a pattern across many packets that no single packet would show, including a domain lead unconsciously hiring people who resemble themselves over years.
- The interview-kit anchors are explicitly provisional: written now from the book and the white paper with no real candidate answers yet behind them; a first cohort's real answers are expected to show the wording wrong in places, and a first revision (by the interviewers who actually asked the questions) is intended once there's a cohort to learn from.

### The paid practical

- Not a full shift, a scored block covering pre-service setup (procedure read), the first seating wave (load read), and treatment of the people around the candidate (steward, downstream teammate, trainer) throughout. A first practical should never run a whole shift; hours are stated on the seat sheet and in the candidate's note, and paid regardless of outcome or whether it's cut short.
- Scoring rows: one row per entry-level procedure (marked reproduced / reproduced-with-correction / not-reproduced), two universal rows (asked-before-guessing, cleaned-as-they-went), one load row (only scored when the practical actually runs under real service load, training-service practicals are marked "not read" for load, never inferred), three people rows against the baseline-conduct standard (any flag on these ends candidacy regardless of everything else), and a closing debrief question. No composite score; a recommendation names which row(s) drove it.
- The assessor (a trained person from the seat's strand who never decides the hire) is a different person from whoever is teaching/standing beside the candidate; the assessor must be placed on the schedule as an extra body, not pulled off their own station to score, or nothing scored is trustworthy. An assessor's first-ever scored practical is shadow-scored by a second trained assessor as their own training completion.
- What the practical may never conclude: month-six performance, load readiness from a load-free training-service run, presence/vibe, a competency the house is supposed to develop, speed relative to the existing seat holder, a customer's reaction, or that being corrected multiple times is worse than being corrected once (a correction is teaching, not failure).
- What the candidate is owed: full advance notice of what's being scored (since the rows are public); a person beside them the whole time; a properly set-up station (not one where the person teaching is themselves short-staffed); never being someone's only point of service; never handling recovery/generosity decisions; a paid stop if the service turns short mid-block; pay after for hours worked or told, whichever is greater; and, if declined, being told which specific rows they didn't reproduce.
- Realist's cost accounting: a practical costs the seat holder's teaching attention, an extra scheduled body for the assessor, a measurably slower first wave, and a briefing line, it should run only on a service the leads' review hasn't already flagged as short-staffed, one practical per station/section per service, never on a new period's first service, and never as sole coverage. Pre-opening, cohort practicals run in training services without load; the Realist's addition is that the cohort's first four weeks of real dinner service become the actual load read (recorded weekly at check-ins), a hire who struggles under load in that window is treated as a training/staffing question, not a hiring failure, since the practical itself never claimed to test load.

### The scorecard's four dimensions

- Constraint stated by Brandon: whatever review rubric a later chunk builds must score against the same dimensions this hiring rubric uses, so a person isn't hired against one standard and reviewed against another.
- The four dimensions are simply the four reads given their "in role" meaning over time, not a fifth thing: (1) competency for the seat, scored at entry against the sheet's entry-level requirements and, in role, against the same document at the person's current horizon; (2) self-directed mastery, read at entry from behavioral evidence and, in role, from the advancement system's own record (modules completed, unlocks taken) rather than a manager's impression, a review that scored this from a manager's gut feeling would reintroduce exactly the discretion the system is designed to remove; (3) conduct under baseline standards and within the seat's decision-rights range; (4) what the building must supply, which, uniquely, reviews the house rather than the person, both at hiring and at every check-in's "what's in the way" question.
- What a review rubric should not add: cross-domain and cross-period skill-building are development markers, not new dimensions, since they aren't read at hiring; leadership is not a fifth dimension (for a lead seat it's dimension 1); an unscoreable "fit" quality is never a dimension at either stage.
- Recording mechanics: an evidence line per question (a specific instance, never an impression that could've been written before the interview happened); a mark per dimension (does-not-meet / meets / meets-with-evidence-beyond / not-assessed-with-reason); a disqualifier flag (none, or named with evidence, never weighed, always ends candidacy); a recommendation (advance/decline only, with a dimension named on any decline).
- Reasons given for refusing any free-text verdict field: the decision is structurally the domain lead's on the packet, so a verdict from any other scorer would be an unauthorized second decision; a verdict field is exactly where an unexamined first impression gets written down; a verdict can't be calibrated the way an evidence line against a written anchor can; and a verdict can't be turned into an answerable decline letter the way a named dimension and evidence line can.
- What the applicant-tracking configuration should refuse: a "not sure"/neutral/mixed response value (an honestly unassessed dimension should read "not assessed" with a reason instead, which then makes the packet incomplete by content); any numeric scale or composite; any scorecard field that writes back to a person's ongoing employee record; any offer-approval chain beyond the refusals already built into the system.

### Interviewer training and calibration

- Problem: the record requires trained interviewers, but training happens on a learning platform that doesn't exist before there's a team, and by the record's own definition the very first interviewers (the founders, then two leads) are untrained. A training record is supposed to exist on a person's page before they run any stage, but the person-page system may not exist yet either.
- Options argued for training the founders before any platform exists: (1) build training content as documents first, ahead of anything else on the hiring calendar; (2) founders train each other by running the same sequence (read modules, live practice session, observed stage, paired stage with independently scored practice runs on consenting people who are not real candidates) with both founders reading the practice scorecards together as their own calibration; (3) an interim, versioned training record kept as a manual entry until the software can write one automatically; (4) the first real lead hire's dinner-in-the-room scorecards, read jointly by both founders, serve as the very first real two-person calibration exercise. A related open question, named but not resolved: whether to pay for an outside trainer (argued against, since an outside interviewing product would import industry norms this program doesn't want) and how to run a first interview in a language neither founder speaks (a first hired lead who speaks it, an early cohort hire who speaks it, or an interpreter in the room, each with different tradeoffs).
- Training content (module outline): the hiring system and the four reads/dimensions; the seat sheet; question discipline (consistency, STAR probes, "what did you actually hold" probe); the anchors (with written, never real-candidate-derived, worked examples); writing an evidence-based scorecard (including the subjective-language scan and why a hit triggers re-training); the hand-off (reading the compensation-mechanics sheet item aloud, and firmly what may never be said, a figure beyond the sheet, hints at an offer, questions about current pay); disqualifiers and flags; a mutual-commitments reference document; assessor-specific scoring; phone-screener-specific content; partner-specific (dinner/founder-stage) content; correspondence rules (never hint at outcome, never deliver a "no" directly); records rules (everything stays inside the applicant-tracking system; a referrer can't run a stage for the person they referred).
- Training sequence: modules read on scheduled shift, then a live practice session, then an observed stage (no scorecard submitted), then a paired stage (scorecard submitted alongside a trained runner and read side by side as the record of completion), then solo. Assessors add a shadow-scored first practical. Re-training (triggered by a scorecard with no named read, a subjective-language hit, an off-sheet decline reason, or a calibration-read finding) sends someone back to a module and one more paired stage before their next solo one, explicitly never treated as a sanction or recorded anywhere but the training record itself.
- No elite interviewer class: every trained person is in the interview pool for their own strand's seats; this is a deliberate contrast with the book's selective high-performer-interviewer program (book p.197), reasoned as inconsistent with a house this size having a separate class of "culture stewards."
- Ninety-day calibration read stands in for the book's interviewer-accuracy-against-outcomes method (which needs hiring volume and years of performance data Sŏn won't have): at ninety days per hire, the person's original packet and their actual on-the-job record are read side by side, producing one anonymized line per interviewer per hire on a running calibration record, a leading indicator rather than the book's lagging one. It explicitly does not create an interviewer class, does not merge the two records together, and treats a hire who struggles under load in the first weeks as first a question about the practical/training design, only second a question about the interviewer.

### Calibration, bias control, and the candidate-review functions

- Book's candidate-review mission (p.205): "a consistent, efficient, and transparent decision-making framework that seeks to control for bias," achieved through a standardized process, a consistent decision framework, bidirectional feedback to/from a review lead, and bias control through measuring outcomes (including against self-identification data, checking for no statistically significant difference by group). Also named: an outside-team steward with veto power over any hire, an early-stage practice of an executive reviewing every hiring packet, and a "TBD, sent back for more information" outcome category.
- Function-by-function mapping, argued (no committee or panel proposed for any of them): consistent framework application lands in the documented seat sheet and generated kit; scorecard verification lands partly in the system's refusal of an incomplete/no-verdict scorecard, partly (weakly) in the deciding lead's own read, and mainly in the monthly calibration read, the only place a leniency/strictness pattern across many packets would show; verification by someone other than the decider lands only partially, the founder-stage check pre-opening, then after that sunsets for entry-level seats, only after the fact via two triggers (a decline contradicting every scorecard, and its proposed mirror, a hire made despite a "does not meet"), a real gap named below; feedback to interviewers lands with whoever runs the calibration read; bias-control-through-measurement lands as an honest, untargeted pattern read on stage and source, since volume is too small to claim significance, and whether to collect self-identification data is an open founder-level question tied to team-composition (see item list); the book's veto lands narrowly as the baseline-conduct flag, raisable by anyone with evidence and overridable by no one; "TBD" lands as a same-stage re-run, not a committee send-back.
- Gap surfaced, not resolved: once the founder-stage check sunsets for entry-level seats post-opening, no outside-domain read remains on those packets except the monthly, after-the-fact calibration pattern. Three options argued: keep the sunset and rely on separation-of-duties, the scored practical, the calibration read, and the hire-against-a-flag trigger (lightest, but the outside check becomes monthly not per-packet); reverse the sunset and keep the founder stage for entry-level seats indefinitely (keeps a founder in every hire in a house built to run without them, WP p.6); or make the adjacent-domain second interviewer standard for every seat, not just domain-owner ones (changes fixed staffing and costs a trained person's time on every packet).
- Calibration read as specified: monthly, run by one named person (a substitute pre-opening), reading every completed packet's scorecards for: subjective-language hits, unasked questions, misfiled evidence, a to-be-developed competency scored as absent, an off-sheet decline reason, per-interviewer leniency/strictness patterns (a pattern, never a number), the outgoing-profile check, hires against a "does not meet," declines contradicting every scorecard, and any unevidenced flag. Produces an anonymized monthly record and per-interviewer notes; refuses a candidate's name, a reopened decision, a numeric target, a meeting, or a verdict on an interviewer.

### The decision and the packet

- Packet contents: every required scorecard independently submitted, the sheet and compensation-transparency-sheet versions handed, the candidate's fourth-read answer, the completion date, the named-read decision, the deciding lead's name, plus (as extensions) a shadow-scored practical form where relevant, a paired trainee's second scorecard where relevant, a lead's written dissent line where relevant, and a reference note for the seat classes that get one.
- "Incomplete by content": a packet is treated as incomplete (not just by a missing scorecard, but) when a stage that was supposed to assess a dimension marked every one of its scorecards "not assessed" on it, the fix is re-running that stage inside the response window at the house's cost, with the candidate told why, rather than any committee send-back.
- Reading order for the deciding lead, deliberately sequenced: disqualifying flags first (ends everything if present); then the fourth read (what the building must supply, an unmet need here is read as the building's failure, not the candidate's, and any "unknown" item must be resolved same-day before deciding); then the other three dimensions, evidence before marks, practical rows before interview notes for physical-skill seats; then recommendations last (so the lead forms their own read before seeing what each interviewer concluded); then any reference note.
- What the lead may never do: hire below the bar to fill a hole; override a disqualifying flag; decide on a packet missing a scorecard; decide their own seat's replacement without the second decider's scorecard; edit or annotate any scorecard; ask a runner to change a mark before or after submission; discuss a candidate with a runner before that runner's own scorecard is in; hold a completed packet open to compare it against another candidate; decide the day after completion (it's same-day); decline on presence, format, or a to-be-developed competency; decline a candidate who simply asked about pay, range, or scheduling; vary an offer's terms from the sheet; or carry a candidate's identity into any conversation outside the applicant-tracking system.
- Dissent, absent any committee to carry it: an overridden scorecard stays in the packet unchanged and permanent; the lead writes a dissent line naming the runner's dimension, the runner's evidence, and the packet evidence read as outweighing it; the runner is told the outcome and the dissent line the same day, through the system, with no right of appeal (their original scorecard is the appeal, and it's already preserved). Repeated overrides of one runner (in either direction) become a calibration-read pattern, read first as a question about the anchors, second about the runner, third about the lead.
- The book's standing committee veto (p.203) becomes a single form: the baseline-conduct flag, raisable by any runner with evidence, unoverridable by anyone including a founder. Ordinary competency disagreement is never a veto, it's carried as a dissent line instead.
- Book's offer-approval channel with a leader's fast turnaround (p.197) is refused as a review-from-above (there's no one structurally positioned above the deciding lead to review them), the same protective work is done instead by the system's own hard refusals (no decision on an incomplete packet, no hire with an active flag, no offer before pay mechanics are filled, no start date before onboarding basics are ready) plus the two calibration triggers, with same-day decision-and-answer already faster than any approval turnaround would be.

### References

- Book's position: reference calls are a mistake to skip; a lukewarm reference (voluntarily provided by the candidate) is disqualifying; a percentile question ("top 50%? 20%? 10%? 5%?") is reliable because people tend to answer data questions honestly; seven behavioral reference questions are specified; more senior roles get more calls, with the hiring manager making at least one; for leadership candidates, the current employer typically can't be called, back-channel references are used only with the candidate's consent, described as "risky."
- The record itself says nothing at all about references, no reference stage or note exists yet in the upstream packet design; this whole area is new work, not something upstream already decided.
- Whether Sŏn calls references splits by seat class, argued: for high-volume/physical-skill (edge) seats, no calls, reasoned on several fronts, prior employers in this labor market are often unreachable within a short response window; a prior manager referenced is, not infrequently, exactly the kind of manager a candidate's own account described leaving because of; a reference call in this industry frequently returns nothing but dates of employment; and a candidate who left a genuinely bad workplace can't offer that manager as a reference and would be penalized for exactly the thing the baseline-conduct standard exists to protect against. Against all that: the paid practical already is a legitimate work-sample test, which is generally a stronger predictor than reference checks anyway, and calls cost time in a process that's trying to move fast. For lead seats, domain-owner seats, and one branch of the chef seat, calls are argued for, since those roles have no practical equivalent and a person about to hold a domain alone deserves outside input.
- Reference calls aren't proposed as a new formal stage, they're due diligence gathered by whoever is deciding, between the first interview and the final round, so the note is ready when the packet completes; the note is a packet item with no mark or verdict of its own, never the sole basis of a decline, and requires the candidate's named consent for each specific person called.
- What's asked, adapted from the book's seven questions: kept largely as-is (three-year outlook, the percentile question, a disagreement anecdote, a coaching anecdote, a "1-10 with no 7" score); reworded to focus on how the candidate treated people without authority over them (rather than "helpfulness" generally) and on self-directed skill growth; two additional questions added for lead-seat candidates specifically (what they took on that wasn't theirs, and what they'd refuse to do).
- Tested and partly refused: the book's absolute rule that any lukewarm reference disqualifies is argued to be right only when the candidate freely chose the reference and the reference has no reason to hold back, and wrong in two named cases: a lead candidate's current employer who can't be asked directly and may be deliberately vague to protect the candidate or themselves; and a reference from someone the candidate correctly stood up to, whose lukewarm account might actually be positive evidence of comfort with distributed authority read the opposite way from the book's rule. Conclusion argued: a lukewarm reference is evidence to weigh, never an automatic disqualifier by itself, and a decline can never rest on a reference note alone since the candidate has to be answerable with a read from a stage they actually experienced.
- Back-channel references (reaching people the candidate didn't name) are adopted only with the candidate's explicit, named consent, exactly as the book frames it, refused entirely without consent.

### The offer and close

- Book's core rules, largely absorbed: an offer letter is a legal document deserving serious/automated handling; the offer is extended verbally before any written document, so nothing in the written offer should be a surprise; "time kills all deals" argues for speed; a "sell chat" follow-up call from someone senior helps close candidates.
- The transparency sheet (handed at the first interview, with pay mechanics on it, "no surprises and nothing withheld," WP p.18) already does most of the "nothing a surprise" work by the time an offer happens, the offer conversation is essentially the same sheet read aloud with a start date and mentor assignment added.
- Offer conversation content and order, specified: naming the seat and strand; confirming the sheet version and any change since the first interview; naming the domain lead the person will work with; reading the compensation range as something the seat holder decides within, not a restriction; the start date and its conditions; the mentor assignment; that a matching written offer follows; how long the offer stands open; an invitation for questions.
- Who extends it: the domain lead who made the decision (not a founder, on the reasoning that a founder extending an offer the lead decided would place someone above the lead at the exact moment the candidate is deciding who they'll work for), except for the two lead seats and the chef seat, where a partner extends it.
- The written offer is generated from the packet and sheet, must match the verbal conversation in substance, carries legal terms set with counsel, and its acceptance is the trigger that creates the person's ongoing employee record and starts onboarding.
- The book's "sell chat" (a senior leader's enthusiasm call, book p.209) is explicitly distinguished from the dinner in the room (a scored pre-decision stage) and is otherwise refused in its performed form, what stands in is the transparency sheet and the documented public build answering the "why should I be excited" questions in writing beforehand, plus an unscored, candidate-initiated conversation available on request during the open offer period.
- Offer stands open for a fixed response window (five days), extendable once to a stated date at the candidate's request, after which the seat reopens with a "we've moved on but you're in the pool" answer, the house is argued not to chase further.
- No offer negotiation, argued as a structural rather than case-by-case answer: pay mechanics are public before a candidate ever meets a decision-maker and are meant to be identical for everyone in the same seat; a candidate can still ask to adjust a start date, shift shape, or accommodation within what the sheet already allows, but nothing else is negotiable and no lead or partner can create a side deal for one person in a seat.

### Levels, and what hiring actually needs from them

- Book's model: a multi-level job-ladder table (six levels by years of experience plus scope) that then drives compensation placement, alongside a caution against overworking job ladders because they harden into checklists that don't capture real capability, and a manager's complaint about needing that structure to function ("How am I supposed to set expectations and compensation without that?" book p.198).
- The record instead has no levels at all: progression is described as developing more connections rather than altitude, with new hires always entering at "the outside edge," documented per-seat competency requirements at two horizons (entry, and what unlocks after a set period), an automatic advancement-scheduling mechanism, and a pool of people ready for their next seat. A separate, still-open question (not closed here) is whether the house needs any titling/leveling convention at all, and it's explicitly routed onward rather than answered in this material.
- What the hiring loop concretely needs regardless of that open question, argued as only three things: a documented entry bar per seat; a fixed, sheet-published unlock period per seat (needed before any advancement-path promise can be made to a candidate); and, from a separate compensation-design chunk, an entry pay rule, including what "salaried" means for the lead seats, since that word appears once in the record's design and is never defined against its actual point-based pay mechanism. Hiring doesn't need any placement decision (unlike the book's sequence of select-candidate-then-set-level-then-set-pay) since everyone enters a seat at the same point regardless of outside experience.
- Consequences named if the compensation-design work never answers "no levels, and here's the pay rule": no offer can legally/practically go out; if unlock periods aren't fixed on sheets, the advancement-path promise made to candidates during interviews becomes false; if a later review rubric ends up scoring against something other than the same seat-sheet competencies used at hiring, the exact mismatch this whole coupling is meant to prevent reappears; and the two lead seats (which are hired straight into the center of the structure rather than at an "edge") are the one place "no altitude" concept needs a real, still-undefined word for what they enter at.
- The book's ladder caution (checklists crowd out real capability) is treated as already addressed rather than ignored: entry competency requirements are kept from calcifying into a checklist because they're validated by an actual scored practical assessment at each unlock rather than self-reported. The book's separate idea of jobs carrying a set salary tied to the role itself (changing only with market data or a role change, book p.201) is noted as a shape worth considering for compensation design, without any figure attached.

### Leadership hiring: the two leads and the third (chef) seat

- Book's leadership-hiring process assumes five things Sŏn largely doesn't have pre-opening: an existing organization to bring along through change management; a person who might be "layered" or displaced by a new hire; a board member available to help sell a candidate; a committee run as a feedback-collection forum; and roughly six months of runway. Only the fifth (that it simply takes the time it takes) applies, and the hiring calendar is built to honor that rather than force a date.
- Element-by-element adoption: candidate confidentiality is adopted in full; the book's "get two important people excited" becomes "both founders independently score a stage on every lead candidate," with the word "excited" itself refused in favor of dimension-and-evidence scorecards; "identify stakeholders" becomes the other, not-yet-hired lead (once one exists) sitting as the second interviewer for the next; the book's pre-interview email to interviewers becomes the standard prep note, explicitly without the book's allowance for "brief feedback... but not so much bias" from prior interviewers; the book's feedback-collection deliberation forum is refused as a meeting, with its actual function (hearing every interviewer's read before deciding) done instead by independent scorecards plus a written dissent line; the book's celebratory announcement has no organization to address pre-opening, so it's replaced with a simple factual statement (once there's a public-facing build) that the seat is filled and by whom, with no celebratory language and only reachable with the candidate's consent; the "experience trap" interview technique is applied in full; reference calls are made (per the references section above); and there is no compensation negotiation (per the offer section above).
- Timing note, self-aware: because this hiring design assumes a prior chunk's decision structure (the packet, the practical as work sample, the founder stage as a scored stage rather than a review, the pay-transparency-sheet gate, the readiness checklist this material extends, and the four review dimensions inherited by a later chunk), doing this leadership-hiring work before that upstream structure existed would have made the still-open chef-seat hiring loop the template for everything else, the reverse of the intended design order.
- The third (chef) seat's hiring loop is designed for both un-ruled branches of an open decision about whether that seat is a negotiated partner or a hired employee, without picking a branch: under a partnership branch, the "hiring loop" is mostly a partnership negotiation rather than a staged process, with confidentiality, the interview "experience trap" technique applied to how the candidate discusses cooking for the house, and due-diligence-only reference calls (with consent) rather than a formal packet; under a hire branch, the full leadership loop applies nearly whole (first interview with both founders and the transparency sheet, a cooking practical scored on founder-built rows, a substitute-dinner stage, the founder stage, a reference note with both founders calling, a same decision process, an offer with no negotiation). What's shared either way: the timeline places this seat first ahead of the rest of the kitchen chain, and the interviewer-training work above (specifically the module on judging depth of culinary knowledge) needs to be done before either branch's loop can run, since the same two people would be doing the reading under either branch.
- Later lead hires (after the pool has had a chance to produce an internal candidate): if the internal-promotion pool holds no one on track, an outside hire for a lead seat needs a written explanation on record; if it does hold someone, the internal-hiring process (below) applies. An open, explicitly filed-rather-than-answered question: whether a domain's own team members should ever join a later lead-seat hiring loop as interviewers (they currently don't, by structural rule).

### Hiring internally

- Book's process: figure out early whether there's genuinely one internal candidate or several before running anything; don't fake a competitive process for a foregone conclusion; for multiple candidates, run a lightweight loop (a couple of leaders interview each, then a hiring-committee discussion) with a feedback loop for those not selected and confidentiality; optionally meet a few outside candidates too, to demonstrate rigor.
- The record's actual mechanism is structurally different: skills unlock automatically after a set period in role; a validated practical assessment auto-schedules; someone who's ready but has no open seat enters a pool of people ready to move; that pool is read before any external search when a seat opens, and an outside lead hire after a certain point requires a written justification.
- For exactly one qualified pool candidate: the book's "don't run a sham process" concern simply doesn't arise, because the pool membership itself was validated by a real, failable assessment, and the seat's own practical assessment (also failable, scored on the same rows as any outside candidate) is the actual test, no separate interview is layered on top, since the person's other three dimensions are already documented from months of on-the-job record; what is asked again is the "what does the building need from you" question, since needs can change with a new seat. A candidate who doesn't pass the practical isn't declined outright, they're told which specific rows, and their pool membership stands with a next assessment date, which is described as the book's "help them understand what to develop" idea built into the system itself rather than delivered as a one-off conversation.
- For multiple pool candidates: everyone on that track takes the seat's practical on identical rows, scored by an assessor who doesn't decide; those not chosen get the same named-rows treatment and keep their pool standing.
- For lead seats filled internally: the person still runs the lead-block interview and the dinner in the room (since holding a domain without an apex above hasn't been demonstrated just by holding a lower seat, and the dinner specifically tests being a customer, which prior floor experience doesn't substitute for), but skips reference calls since their own ongoing employee record already serves that function.
- Book's "meet a few external candidates to show rigor" is explicitly refused as itself a kind of sham process, running outside candidates through stages the house never intends to decide on is treated as a worse breach of "no ghosting" than skipping the gesture; the pool-first, practical-as-rigor logic is offered as the substitute.
- What the first internal loop owes people not selected: the specific rows/dimension in writing the same day; their pool standing unchanged with a next assessment date (not treated as a rejection); nothing added to their ongoing employee record beyond that date and those rows; and, at a structural level, the loop's own record becomes the house's first real check on whether its stated internal-promotion commitment is actually holding, since that's a named measure the house commits to tracking.
- Visibility/confidentiality argued: each person sees only their own pool status; domain leads and the training owner see the pool for their own tracks; no one else sees who's in a pool; an internal candidate's practical is scheduled without naming the seat on any public schedule; any announcement names only the new seat holder, never who else was considered.

### Decision rights for hiring (ledger entries)

- Format used throughout: an owner, a range of what they may/may not do, and named triggers pointing to where an exception or disagreement gets routed, with anything the record can't yet supply an owner for marked explicitly incomplete rather than filled in by guesswork.
- Extending an offer: owned by the deciding domain lead (a partner for the two lead seats and the chef); may not extend while pay-mechanics are unfilled, may not vary sheet terms, may not extend to a candidate whose seat count already filled.
- Setting a level (or its absence): no one owns "placement," since everyone enters at a seat's fixed entry point; unlock-period-setting is owned by the domain lead on the sheet; the entry pay rule itself is explicitly incomplete, owned by a downstream compensation-design chunk and the founders.
- Overriding a scorecard: owned by no one, a scorecard, once submitted, is permanent and can only be dissented against in writing, never edited.
- Stopping a hiring loop mid-stream: the candidate may withdraw any time; any interviewer may end it via a documented flag; a domain lead may close a seat's pipeline only when the seat itself closes or fills; a founder may not unilaterally stop a loop for an entry-level seat.
- Calling references, reopening a declined candidacy, changing the question set/anchors, and certifying an interviewer as trained each get their own owner/range/trigger entry, cross-referenced above.
- Explicitly incomplete entries, named rather than guessed at: the entry pay rule and what a lead seat "enters at"; interviewer certification before any training platform exists; the disagreement rule between the two founders on a lead-seat hire pre-opening; the domain-lead owner for two specific floor seats; and every kitchen-hiring entry, pending the still-open chef ruling.

### Findings: conflicts, silences, and unresolved tensions inside the record itself

- The record uses two different vocabularies for what hiring reads and what performance reviews focus on, and never explicitly states they're meant to be the same list measured twice, this is exactly the seam a later review-rubric chunk is asked to close, and the four-dimension framing above is offered as one way to close it, not a final answer.
- Two different, seemingly conflicting accounts exist of how advancement actually gets triggered: one where the system alone schedules a validation assessment with no manager judgment involved, another where a person schedules a conversation with "the appropriate lead" who signs off, a judgment call the first account says advancement should never wait on. Bears directly on hiring because hiring strictly separates who assesses from who decides, and it's an open question whether advancement should mirror that same separation. Who resolves this and how it interacts with a person's competency spanning two domains is unresolved.
- The record's stated design principle that "everyone always enters at the outside edge, never at altitude" has no accounted-for exception for the two lead seats and the chef seat, all three of which are necessarily hired straight into central positions since there's no "inside" yet to enter at the edge of, the record has no word yet for what a lead "enters at" that isn't a rank, which matters for both the leadership hiring design and a later compensation-architecture chunk.
- The book's language-choice promise for candidates (interview in their own language) currently has no trained interviewer behind it for languages neither founder speaks, pre-opening, named as a real, near-term gap tied to the founder-training decision.
- A canon-level service standard requiring genuine "felt knowledge" of dishes has no accounted-for path into the interview's competency read, since (a) an entry-level interview can only test the capacity to develop that felt knowledge, not the knowledge itself, and (b) the actual menu it would apply to is itself gated on a still-open chef decision, meaning there's currently no stated interval between the menu existing and the floor being trained on it before service starts.
- The record's stated instinct to treat problems as process failures rather than individual failures ("blame the process, fix the process") has no explicit hiring-side counterpart: nothing currently states whether a new hire who doesn't work out is treated as a person problem or a hiring-system design problem, or feeds anything back into how interviews are run, the ninety-day calibration read is offered as a way to actually close that loop, not as something the record already does.
- The term "salaried" appears exactly once in the source material (describing which seats get a dinner-in-the-room stage) and is never reconciled with the described day-weighted, non-hierarchical pay-pooling mechanism, leaving open what "salaried" concretely means for pay purposes, which a downstream compensation-design chunk and the founders need to resolve.
- Cross-training a person into another domain (e.g., a floor person training into the kitchen) raises an unresolved question of who signs off a competency that spans two domains and two different leads, a working assumption (the receiving domain's lead signs off, with the person's own lead kept informed) is offered as a starting position, not a settled answer.
- A specific floor leadership role is described in one place as something that would be functionally absorbed by software and in another place as an active, staffed function with its own responsibilities, leaving genuinely open whether that seat exists on the hiring inventory at all, which is upstream of writing a seat sheet for it.
- The record names leading indicators for the organization broadly (things like customer recognition rate) but names no leading indicators specific to the hiring system itself (time-to-decision, share of hires reaching their first real skill unlock, early retention), the pipeline read and the ninety-day calibration read are offered as filling that gap, not as something the record already specifies.
