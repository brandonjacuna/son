## 1\. Her ask against the record, and how much is already decided

### 1.1 What she asks

Her four sections ask a manager to do four things. Coach continuously and by hypothesis: gather data ("You have more than you realize and need less than you think," Book p.387), form a hypothesis about a person's strengths and weaknesses from observation, and test it by saying it to the person as an observation about behavior and its impact, "not a judgment about who they are as a person" (Book p.390). Give hard feedback as "an explorer, not a lecturer" (Book p.392): open with a neutral open-ended question, or share an empathetic observation that is "supportive, objective, and specific instead of emotional and generic" (Book p.394), own it as the manager's perception, and cycle between the two until the person and the manager share an account of the development area. Build a culture of informal feedback, in which people offer one-off observations unasked, praise publicly and criticize privately, present team feedback publicly as data, and in which the leader models it by asking for feedback "on different occasions and through different forums," letting it sit, and following up (Book pp. 397 to 398). And, in the chapter's introduction, hold the manager between the "extreme coach" who "spends too much time involved in the day-to-day, providing endless feedback" and the "forgot-to coach" who is "clear about what results they want" and "unclear on how to help" (Book p.382), with the manager's posture "one of service" (Book p.382).

The workbook adds one instrument that touches this range: a written recap sent after a hard-feedback 1:1, and three follow-on letters, each structuring feedback as "the situation, the observable behaviors, and the impact they had" (Workbook p.113).

Every piece assumes a salaried knowledge worker, a manager who assigns and reviews and who sits above the person, a weekly 1:1 with a shared document, a manager's notes, meetings the manager may join "to get more insight" (Book p.390), a promotion the manager can grant as a test (Book p.391), and a written record the manager writes about the person. None of that is Sŏn.

### 1.2 What the record already holds

The record holds a performance philosophy, a blame rule, a failure-mode discipline, and four surfaces, and it holds them as mechanics rather than as posture.

The philosophy: "Performance systems develop people rather than judge them" (WP p.19). "Reviews happen every three to six months and focus on development, growth, and the path forward rather than a verdict. The governing rule is no surprises: continuous communication means a formal review never contains new information. If something is wrong, the person knows before the review. If something is excellent, they know before it too. Feedback runs both directions" (WP p.19). Onboarding promises "clear milestones, skill assessments, and feedback, so the person always knows what they are learning, why it matters, and how mastery is demonstrated" and "regular check-ins and course correction" (WP p.18).

The blame rule: "when something goes wrong, the first question is not who failed. It is what allowed this to happen. Systems are designed to prevent, catch, and recover from errors. Management absorbs blame from staff. Post-mortems are private and process-focused. The system gets fixed so it does not happen again, to anyone" (WP p.17). "Restaurant founders build systems for themselves without realizing it, then blame employees when those systems do not work for anyone else" (WP p.17). Toward the customer: "the customer should never feel at fault, even when they are" (WP p.26).

The failure-mode discipline: "Before intervening at the people level, identify which failure mode is actually present" (WP p.14), with tension slack as the one mode described at a person and its fix stated away from the person: "The fix is not replacing the person. It is diagnosing which adjacent nodes are overloaded and re-establishing tension in that domain" (WP p.14). The implementation sequence for a problem in service: "the server owns the table relationship and resolves it using their authorized judgment and generosity range, the system captures the incident in the shift close, the appropriate lead reviews, the process that allowed the failure gets updated, and the next server inherits a system that has already learned from it" (WP p.13).

The surfaces: peer recognition on a platform, "which lands differently than management praise because it comes from the people who understand the work" (WP p.19); the structured feedback channel with "The loop gets closed" (WP p.22); the shift close's capture (WP p.13); and the review (WP p.19). The Maitre d owns "Team development, training, and the invisible advocate function" and is "Accountable to experience quality and team health" (WP p.10). Advancement waits on no one's judgment: "No one's advancement waits on a manager's attention or judgment. The system is the advocate" (WP p.11).

Canon governs the register and the floor. "Present without performing" is the master register (Canon 1.0, section 01; Deck 3.0, p.28). "Warmth is in the attention, not the adjectives" (Canon 1.0, section 01). Nunchi is "Developed through mastery, exercised through judgment," and "Not diagnostic inquiry. Not a checklist" (Canon 1.0, section 01; Deck 3.0, p.15); Jeong "cannot be scripted" (Deck 3.0, p.15). The Playground Philosophy prohibits "training systems that measure procedure execution without building toward judgment" and requires "training architecture that progresses from procedure mastery to autonomous judgment" (Canon 1.0, section 01), and the deck states both edges: "Rules so tight the result is foreclosed" and "Openness so loose the space stops feeling safe" (Deck 3.0, p.14). The service model gives the server the table and the room's owner the room, with recovery "pre-authorized" and escalation triggers "defined before service, not improvised at the table" (Canon 1.0, section 08; Deck 3.0, p.79). The non-negotiables are three lines: "No exploitation ever. No toxicity ever. No disposable people" (Deck 3.0, p.4; Canon 1.0, section 01). The room carries "No spectacle during dinner service" (Canon 1.0, section 06).

Sessions 3 to 11 built on that and left this page a fenced site. The check-in is the shift-native 1:1, on the clock, placed by the stack, with three questions, an upward question, and one line in the person's words read back; it refuses verdicts, task assignment, pay, and discipline; the lead writes on the person page at the check-in and nowhere else (S4 P15, P18, P20). The person page carries states and the person's own words and refuses ratings, free text about the person, and a probation label (S7 P25 to P29). The environment names no person in a room, and the private correction is the check-in's (S10 P14, P15). Nothing reaches the house that is not on a record first, and a person's own words are theirs (S11 P10). Upward feedback runs on the channel and the fourth question; recognition is a peer's words about an act (S11 P30, P31). The diagnostic writes nothing about a person and reaches one by one path, for tension slack only (S9 P41, P42). The skill-will matrix is refused as a read of anyone (S9 P40). The four scorecard dimensions bind the review and nothing is added at review that was not read at hiring (S6 P33). A designation is never a status and feedback to its holder is about the range (S10 P33; S8, `86akhcz71`). The response by type of failure was named as this page's by Session 10 and not designed (S10 P14).

### 1.3 The gap

Three things are not held. First, the inside of the check-in. Session 4 built the form and refused four contents; it did not say what a lead does inside the fifteen minutes when something about a person's work needs to change (`86akh67y5`). The record promises "course correction" (WP p.18) and never says what a correction is, who says it, or where. Second, the response by type of failure, which decides whether a capture is met with a process fix or with something said to a person (S10 6.2). Third, and the session task's own premise: what happens during a live service. The record's implementation sequence runs from the server's range to the capture to the lead's review to the process fix, and no step in it is a person being told anything. The record has no spoken act of correction at all except one addressed to a customer: "be direct about what happened, acknowledge the real impact, and make it right" (WP p.26). Her chapter is entirely about that spoken act, addressed to a person on the team, and the record is silent on it.

The silence has a shape. Every prior session that touched a person's work routed it to a record and refused the room, and every one of them handed the remainder here. This page is where the remainder lands, and it lands on a fixed constraint: the correction is the check-in's, the check-in is placed by the stack, and the lead writes nothing between. The report's job is to listen and decide to act" (Book p.391); praise publicly and criticize privately; let feedback sit and follow up; and her Operating Principle 2, say the thing you think you cannot say, which Session 3 placed at the post-mortem and which this page places at the check-in and in the range's live call.

### 1.5 The session task's premise, tested

The task predicts direct transfer: hard feedback fast and in the moment is native to a kitchen and a floor, and the adaptation is "not losing what she gets right." The prediction is half right, and the half matters.

What a kitchen and a floor are native to is the live call: fire, hold, re-plate, re-set, take that table, hands. Those are fast, in the moment, and about a person's work in the sense that a person is doing the work. They are not feedback about a person. They are the range operating on its object, and the record already permits them: the server owns the table and the room's owner owns the room (Canon 1.0, section 08), the sequence designation owns the floor's side of the pass (S8 P22), the pass owner is command-and-control (S8 P23, chef-gated). The kitchen's other native act, the shouted correction of a cook in front of the line, is not what she describes and is what the non-negotiables name: "No toxicity ever" (Deck 3.0, p.4). Her hard feedback is a private conversation between two people about a pattern, framed as a partnership, with time to cycle between a question and an observation. That is not native to a floor at tempo. Nothing at 8:15 has fifteen minutes and two chairs.

So the premise transfers as two things, not one. The live call transfers whole and needs no new mechanism, only a rule that keeps it about the work (section 6.1). Her conversation transfers as a way of talking and is constrained hard on where it happens: the check-in, which the stack can place forward to the next shift the two people share (section 6.4). What she gets right, and what this page keeps, is that the hypothesis is said soon, said as a perception, and said to the person before it is written anywhere; at Sŏn the last of those is stronger than hers, because the hypothesis is written nowhere at all.

**P1 (Recommended).** Her coaching and feedback material lands on a record that already fixed feedback as records and refused the room, and on ten sessions that fenced the check-in as the one place a person is spoken to about their work. What this page adds is the inside of that check-in, the coaching model that runs there, the rule for what is said live on a floor, and the feedback culture as the set of directions candor moves in and the surface each direction lands on. Her forms are refused where they assume a manager who writes about a person; her method survives nearly whole as a way of talking.

**P2 (Recommended).** The session task's direct-transfer premise is answered as two transfers: the live call about the work transfers whole under a rule that keeps it about the object and never about the person; her hard-feedback conversation transfers as a way of talking and is placed at the check-in, brought forward by the stack when it cannot wait. What is native to a kitchen and refused here is the correction of a person in the roster's hearing.

Where her material here reaches into them (the low performer at p.395, the manager of managers at p.388, the documented case at Workbook pp. 114 to 116) this page designs the coaching side and routes the rest.

**The extreme coach and the forgot-to coach, answered by construction.** Her continuum is a description of a manager's temperament, and a system that depends on temperament has already failed the designer's test. At Sŏn neither end can exist as a standing state, because the check-in's form bounds both. The forgot-to coach is refused by the stack: the check-in is placed when the interval elapses, not when a lead remembers, and "no later session may make it unpaid, optional, or dependent on a lead remembering to call it" (ledger, S4). A lead who has a hypothesis and does not say it has, by the next review, a page with nothing on it to open with, and a review that contains something not on the page is a candor failure read at S13's row and on the lead's entry, not a diligence lapse. The extreme coach is refused by the fifteen minutes and the interval: a lead may bring a check-in forward once per interval (section 6.4), and the count of brought-forward check-ins per lead per month is a load reading at the leads' review, so a lead "providing endless feedback" (Book p.382) shows on a count before it shows on a person. Between check-ins the lead has the live call about the work, the placed line (section 6.5), and nothing else that writes.

**"Feedback is a gift," refused as a posture and kept as an obligation.** She concedes it "feels cloying to say" (Book p.382). Canon's register is "present without performing" (Canon 1.0, section 01), and the program's standing rule refuses performed conviction. No lead at Sŏn says that feedback is a gift, that they are here to support the person, or that they believe in them; the workbook's "As your manager, I'm here to support you" and "It's great to see you're back on track!" (Workbook pp. 113, 115) are the register canon forbids and the exclamation canon forbids "in any written brand voice context" (Canon 1.0, section 09). What her phrase is reaching for, "the manager's attitude needs to be one of service" (Book p.382), the record states as mechanics: "Employees are the users" (WP p.17), the fourth read is scored on the building (S6 P33), and every check-in's second question asks what is in the way and hears it as a system question (S4 4.3). **Her two exhibits.** "Think harder" (Book p.383) is the forgot-to coach's note and is refused: at Sŏn a read with no named row is the tell of an untrained reader, in hiring (S5 P52) and here. "What do you think Teresa was thinking?" (Book p.383) is her best case and it survives whole as the open-ended question's form (section 5): it asked the person to read the room they had just run, it named no trait, it waited, and it delivered the observation only after the person could not find it. Her deputy's lesson, "I'd come in with a problem but not done one bit of work to propose some solutions" (Book p.384), is the hold's joint half-page: the problem stated as the outcome for the customer or the house, the options considered (S11 P29).

**"You really have talent."** Her one argument for the formal review is that "sometimes, especially when the work is unending and hard, you need to have someone tell you that as part of a formal process" (Book p.384). Session 13 answers the review. This page places the informal half of it: the strength said aloud lives in the check-in's first question, what is working, which is asked every time and is the person's account first; and in the recognition a peer writes about an act (S11 P11), which the record ranks above management praise (WP p.19). A lead's praise of a person is said at the check-in and written only if the person writes it as their line; it is never a class on the platform and never a line on a page in the lead's words.

**P3 (Recommended).** The chapter's frame is answered by construction and not by temperament: the forgot-to coach is refused by the stack placing the check-in and by the review that can contain nothing the page does not; the extreme coach is refused by the fifteen minutes, the interval, and the count of brought-forward check-ins as a load reading. ## 3\. The coaching model: hypothesis-based coaching, translated

Her three steps survive as method: gather data, form a hypothesis, test it (Book p.387). What changes is the answer to three questions she never has to ask, because her manager's notebook answers them by default: from which records, on which surface, and with what written residue.

### 3.1 Gather data: from which records, and from which never

"You have more than you realize and need less than you think" (Book p.387). Her Anika example counts "10 or 15 data points rather than three" by adding the manager's own context on each person, their "work style, preferences, strengths, and weaknesses" (Book p.388). At Sŏn the count is real and the sources are different, because the house refuses every source that is a person's impression of a person.

The sources a lead may read before a check-in, all of which the person can already read:

| Source | What it holds | Where | The person can read it |
| ---| ---| ---| --- |
| The person page's last three check-in lines | The person's own words, dated, read back before saving (S4 P18) | The person page | Yes, at any time |
| The plan block, while a plan runs | States and milestones with dates, the fourth-read baseline, the supplied lines (S7 I2) | The person page | Yes |
| The readiness form's rows, and the four-week load read | Ready or not yet, per row, scored by an assessor who is not the mentor; a milestone, never a scorecard (S7 P24, I7) | The person page's milestone | Yes |
| The close's captures for the tables or stations the person held | Incidents with range status, in the writer's own words, naming a process and never a person as cause (S4 P12; S11 5) | The capture record | Yes, their own |
| The range-used count | Whether the person used the range, on what, and whether they escalated on a trigger, read as a count (S10 3.3; S7 7.4) | The stack | Yes |
| The domain's dated fixes | The evidence lines and process updates on the leads' review record, which name no person (S9 P8, P24) | The leads' review record on the team home | Yes |
| The schedule's facts | A shift worked, a paired service missed, a check-in moved; facts the stack holds (S7 13.4) | The scheduling surface | Yes |
| The fluency record and the path line | Connections demonstrated; the one track in the person's words (S8 P35; S9 P43) | The person page | Yes |
| The lead's own observation, unwritten | What the room's owner saw from the coverage position (Canon 1.0, section 08) | Nowhere | It is said, or it does not exist |

The sources a lead may never read as data about a person: a recognition on the platform, because it is a peer's act about an act and "never a read of a person" (S11 P11); a channel item, because "nothing about a person is read from it" (S11 P30); the diagnostic's evidence lines, scorecard sections, and reset records, because "a person's name is on no line, memo, or record it produces" (S9 P42); a hiring scorecard or any interview evidence, because hiring assessments never become a person's record (S5, `86akh7rb0`); a mentor's account, because the mentor "never becomes a sponsor, a coach, or a channel for the lead" (S10 P32); a peer's account of a peer, because the environment names no person and a lead who asks one peer about another has built the informal relationship bus failure names (WP p.13); a work style placement, because the grid "never enters a room as a read of anyone" (S10 P30); and a lead's own note written anywhere, because the lead writes on the person page at the check-in only and on no other record about the person (S4 P20).

Her question, whether "the data you're collecting suggests conflicting conclusions" so that one should "spend more time collecting data before you form a hypothesis" (Book p.389), is answered by the table's last row: the lead's observation is the one source that is not on a record, and it is the weakest, because it is the one the person cannot read. A hypothesis that rests on it alone waits for a record to join it, or is said as the lead's perception and nothing more.

### 3.2 Form a hypothesis: what it may be, what it may not be, and where it lives

"An intuition is just a hypothesis" (Book p.385). At Sŏn a hypothesis about a person is a sentence of one shape: a behavior in a service, and its impact on the work. "On the two Saturdays the captures show a re-fire on your section, the sequence call came after the plate left the pass" is a hypothesis. It names a service, a behavior, and an object. Her own example is the same shape: "I have a theory that you're not effectively getting important information across to the right stakeholders. For example, when we came out of that recent business review, I sensed that the CMO was unsure about your plans" (Book p.390).

What a hypothesis may not be. It may not be about who the person is: her "bad communicator" (Book p.390) is her own counter-example and canon's rule against a sentence that could belong to anyone (Canon 1.0, section 09, test five) is the same rule from the other side. It may not be a verdict, because the check-in refuses verdicts (S4 P15). It may not be a placement on the skill-will matrix or an answer to her motivation questions, "Is it new, episodic, or persistent? Could the person be experiencing something in their personal life?" (Book p.286), because the matrix is refused as a read of anyone (S9 P40) and a lead asking about a person's life is the intrusion the non-negotiables refuse (S9 P44). It may not be a dimension mark, because nothing is added at review that was not read at hiring (S6 P33) and a hypothesis carried to a review by any door is a fifth dimension. It may not be a diagnostic read of a person, because the diagnostic opens at a mode and never at a person (S9 P1). It may not be a "self-awareness gap" (Book p.390), which is a trait word for a person who has heard a hypothesis and not agreed, and which section 3.5 answers as a count. It may not be about Nunchi or Jeong as qualities the person has or lacks (section 13).

Where it lives: nowhere. This is the model's one structural departure from hers and it does most of the work. Her manager files the hypothesis away ("File that away and bring it back up when the moment is right," Book p.386). At Sŏn the lead may not write on the person page outside a check-in (S4 P20), may write no free text about the person on it at any time (S7 P25), and may carry no note about a person on any other record (section 3.1). A hypothesis exists in the lead's head between the records that prompted it and the check-in at which it is said. If the lead needs to hold it longer than that, the stack can bring the check-in forward (section 6.4). What the lead may write down is what the person can already read: the capture's date, the row's number, the line's date. A hypothesis with no record behind it and a lead who cannot hold it until the next shared shift is a hypothesis that was not ready.

### 3.3 Test a hypothesis: at the check-in, as a perception, once

"The best way to test your hypothesis is to share it directly with your report... 'I'm testing a hypothesis. Let me know whether it feels right to you'" (Book p.389). This survives whole as the form, and section 5 gives the two openings. What this page fixes is the conditions: at the check-in and nowhere else that writes (section 4); as an observation of behavior and impact, owned as the lead's perception, "not asking them to confirm your criticism" (Book p.394); said once per check-in, with the person's answer heard before any solution; in the person's chosen language (section 13); and with the person's answer as the only thing that can be written, in their words, read back.

Her test has three outcomes and each has a residue here. The person agrees, and adds what they see: the line written is theirs, about what they will change and what the building must supply for it, which is the range card re-read or a paired service placed or a module in their language, and that half goes to the training-infrastructure line at the leads' review (S7 P29). The person disagrees, and the records are thin: "Sometimes you'll be wrong, which is in itself a data point" (Book p.386), and at Sŏn a wrong hypothesis writes nothing; the line written is whatever the person says about what is working or in the way, and the lead reads more records before the next check-in or drops it. The person disagrees, and the records are not thin: the lead says so, as records the person can read ("the captures on the fourteenth and the twenty-first say the sequence call came late; what do you see in them"), and the check-in ends with the person's line either way. Her "it may take a few rounds before you arrive at a common understanding" (Book p.395) is the interval doing its work: rounds are check-ins, and a hypothesis said at three consecutive check-ins with the records unchanged and no agreement is the shape Session 9 handed forward and section 7.4 places.

### 3.4 What a hypothesis may never become

A verdict at the check-in (S4 P15). A word on the person page in the lead's voice (S7 P25). A dimension, a rating, or evidence at the review (S6 P33; S13). A diagnostic read of a person, an evidence line, or a line on any record the diagnostic produces (S9 P42). A task, because nothing is assigned at a check-in (S9 P38). A pay line or a designation withheld (S10 P33). A reason in a change entry, because a change with a person as its reason is refused (S9 P26). A cause on a capture (S4 P12). A citation in any managing-out decision, because no coaching record exists to cite, and the one thing the diagnostic may write after recurrence is about the seat's load, never the person (S9 P41). The model holds because it has no page: what cannot be written cannot be carried.

### 3.5 Her self-awareness gap, answered as a count

She names the person whose "self-perception is consistently misaligned with what you and others observe" (Book p.390) and says such a person "often become\[s\] low performers" (Book p.395). The label is refused: it is a trait word, it rests on "what you and others observe" as a source, which section 3.1 refuses, and it is a verdict about coachability. What survives is the thing she is pointing at, stated as records: a hypothesis about a behavior, said at consecutive check-ins, with the captures, rows, or range count unchanged after the building's fix, and no line from the person that names it. That is a pattern the check-ins have recorded and the person has heard at each, which is exactly the shape Session 9 hands to Session 14 "only if the pattern the check-ins have recorded, and the person has heard at each, becomes a performance conversation, which Session 14 designs" (S9 14.1). Her "you may need to make a change" (Book p.395) is Session 14's question and not this page's, and this page hands it there with what the check-in has: the person's own lines, dated, and the records they were read against.

**P4 (Recommended).** A lead gathers data for a hypothesis from records the person can already read: the person's own lines, the plan block, the readiness rows and load read, the captures for the tables or stations they held, the range-used count, the domain's dated fixes, the schedule's facts, and the fluency and path lines; and from the lead's own unwritten observation, which is the weakest source because it is the one the person cannot read. Never from a recognition, a channel item, the diagnostic's records, a scorecard, a mentor, a peer's account of a peer, a work style placement, or a lead's note written anywhere.

**P5 (Recommended).** A hypothesis about a person is one shape: a behavior in a service and its impact on the work. It is never about who the person is, never a verdict, never a matrix placement or a motivation question, never a dimension, never a diagnostic read, never a self-awareness label, and never a read of Nunchi or Jeong. It lives nowhere: the lead holds it between the records that prompted it and the check-in at which it is said, and writes nothing.

**P6 (Recommended).** A hypothesis is tested by saying it at the check-in, once, as an observation of behavior and impact owned as the lead's perception, in the person's chosen language, after an open-ended question and before any solution. Its residue is the person's answer in their words, read back; a wrong hypothesis writes nothing; a hypothesis the person declines is said again only against a record that has changed.

**P7 (Recommended).** A hypothesis may never become a verdict, a word on the page in the lead's voice, a dimension or evidence at the review, a diagnostic read, a task, a pay line, a designation withheld, a reason in a change entry, a cause on a capture, or a citation in a managing-out decision.

**P8 (Recommended).** Her self-awareness gap is refused as a label and answered as a count: a hypothesis said at consecutive check-ins with the records unchanged after the building's fix and no line from the person naming it is the pattern Session 9 defined, handed to Session 14 with the person's own lines and the records they were read against.

## 4\. The inside of the check-in: the fifteen minutes, designed

Session 4 fixed the form and this page does not touch it: fifteen minutes as the first fifteen minutes of a scheduled shift in the pre-service window, placed by the stack, between the person and the lead of their domain, three questions in a fixed order, the upward question, one line written on the person page and read back before saving, the next date (S4 P15). It refuses a verdict, task assignment, a pay conversation, discipline, and a post-mortem (S4 4.3), and a mentor in the room (S11 6.3). What this page adds is what happens inside those minutes when a lead holds a hypothesis, and what happens when the lead holds none, which is most check-ins.

### 4.1 Before the check-in: the lead's pre-read

The lead opens the person page and reads what the person can read: the last three lines, the plan block if one runs and which read this check-in is (S7 7.4), the next milestone the stack shows, and the captures for the person's tables or stations since the last check-in. The lead reads the domain's dated fixes for anything that touched the person's section or station. That is the whole pre-read and it takes the minutes before the shift the stack already placed. The lead writes nothing. A lead who arrives at a check-in with a written list about the person has written outside a check-in, which is the trigger S4 set (S4 P20).

### 4.2 The first question: what is working

Asked first, every time, and heard as the person's account. This is where a strength is named and it is the person who names it first. If the lead has seen something work, in a capture the person wrote or in a service the lead covered, the lead says it here, as a specific service and a specific act, in the register canon sets for everything the house says: specific, ending at its last load-bearing word, no adjective performing warmth (Canon 1.0, section 09). "Saturday's eight-top, the re-seat when the pyeong-sang ran late" is the form; "you're great with big tables" is not. A lead's praise is said and not written unless the person writes it as their line. It never goes to the platform, which carries a peer's words only (S11 P13).

### 4.3 The second question: what is in the way

Heard as a system question, "the first question is not who failed. It is what allowed this to happen" (WP p.17; S4 4.3). The person answers first and the lead listens for the building: a module in the wrong language, a mise depth, a pass that called late, a schedule shape. Frictions go to the channel by the lead with the person's knowledge (S4 4.4; S11 6.8).

This is where a hypothesis enters, after the person's answer and never before it. If the person's account of what is in the way already names the thing the lead's hypothesis is about, the lead has her best case, "the person's own hypothesis about a development area" (Book p.386), and the line written is the person's. If it does not, the lead asks the open-ended question first (section 5.1) and then, if needed, says the observation (section 5.2). Both are about a service the records show and neither is about the person. The lead says it once. The person answers. Then, and only then, the two look for what would change it: something the person will do differently in a service, and something the building must supply. The first is the person's line if they choose it. The second is the training-infrastructure line at the leads' review (S7 P29), or a range card re-read here and now, or a paired service the stack places.

Three refusals inside the second question. The lead does not offer a solution before the person has answered the question, which is her rule ("if you offer solutions before you both agree on the problem, there's a good chance that you'll alienate your report," Book p.392) and is the check-in's rule against task assignment read at its smallest scale. The lead does not name a second hypothesis in the same check-in; one behavior, one service pattern, one conversation. The lead does not say anything about the person's manner, pace as a trait, attitude, or life.

### 4.4 The third question: what changed

The person's one goal and its state, the progression state as the stack shows it, schedule needs, and "anything they want the lead to know" (S4 4.3). Inside a plan, the plan block tells the lead which read this is (S7 P27). Here the check-in reads what the records changed since the last: a readiness form (section 7.1), a range used (S10 3.3), an unlock the stack scheduled, a designation held for the first time. A hypothesis that concerns a change the records show, a first solo that ran short or a range used inside its limit and wrong, enters here rather than under the second question, and in the same form.

### 4.5 The upward question

"The person's read on the lead and the house" (S4 P15), in the person's words, written only if they want it written (S11 P30). The lead asks it in her form, as a request for something to change and not as a yes-or-no: her own contrast, "What could I have done differently" rather than "How do you think that meeting went" (Book p.397), is the difference between a question and a prompt. The lead hears it as she says to: "repeat the feedback to ensure you've heard the other person accurately, then thank them" (Book p.398), and does not explain. Her "Follow up on the feedback" (Book p.398) is structural: the friction half goes to the channel with the person's knowledge and the loop window closes it in thirty days with an owner (S4 P30), and at the next check-in the lead reads the channel item's state back to the person under the third question. The half that is about the lead has no destination the lead does not hold until `86akhb2jg` closes (S10 P15), and the lead may not write it unless the person wants it written. A lead who explains, defends, or answers an upward line with a hypothesis about the person has left the question; that is the tight edge of the fourth question and it is the one place the check-in can become her manager's room in reverse.

### 4.6 The one line, and the read-back

One line, in the person's words, read back aloud before it is saved, and the next date (S4 P15). Whose words: the person's, always. What it may hold: what the person will do differently, what the building must supply, what is working, what is in the way, their read of the lead if they want it written, or nothing about a hypothesis at all. What it may not hold: the lead's hypothesis, the lead's observation, the lead's summary of the conversation, a verdict, a row mark, a plan state (the stack writes those), or a word the person did not say. The read-back is the check: a line the person hears as not theirs is re-written before it is saved. The line the person writes is the only residue of coaching, and it is the evidence base the review opens on (S4 P18), which is how a review that "never contains new information" (WP p.19) is built rather than hoped for.

### 4.7 The check-in with no hypothesis

Most check-ins. The lead asks the three questions, the upward question, and writes the line. Nothing on this page makes a lead manufacture an observation to fill fifteen minutes; her extreme coach is the lead who does. A check-in that runs short is a check-in that ran; a check-in extended past fifteen minutes "when the pre-service window has the slack, at the lead's own read" (S4 4.6) is where a hard conversation that needs the rounds she describes gets them.

### 4.8 The kitchen's check-in

Chef-gated with every kitchen entry (S4 P16). The form above is the floor's; the chef partner rules whether the kitchen's check-in holder is the chef seat or a Station Lead and whether the inward strand's spine changes. The coaching model's rules (sources, the shape of a hypothesis, no page, one line in the person's words) are the record's and canon's and not the floor's alone, and the team recommends they hold in the kitchen unchanged; the chef ratifies.

**P9 (Recommended).** The inside of the check-in: the lead pre-reads the person page, the plan block, the person's captures, and the domain's dated fixes, and writes nothing; asks what is working and names a strength as a specific act in a specific service only after the person's account; asks what is in the way and hears it as a system question, and only after the person's answer says one hypothesis as an open-ended question and then an observation of behavior and impact; asks what changed against the records; asks the upward question as a request for something to change and does not explain; and writes one line in the person's words, read back. Solutions come after the shared problem, one hypothesis per check-in, nothing about the person's manner or life.

**P10 (Recommended).** The one line is the person's only: it may hold what they will do differently, what the building must supply, what is working or in the way, or their read of the lead if they want it written; it may never hold the lead's hypothesis, observation, summary, a verdict, or a word the person did not say. It is the only residue of coaching and the evidence base the review opens on.

**P11 (Chef-gated).** The kitchen's check-in holder and spine (S4 P16, extended and not filed twice), with the team's recommendation that the coaching model's rules hold in the kitchen unchanged.

## 5\. Hard feedback: her conversation, kept as a way of talking

"Be an explorer, not a lecturer" (Book p.392) survives whole, as does nearly every sentence of her section, because it describes how two people talk and not what a manager writes. This section fixes where it is said, by whom, in which language, with what record, and what the workbook's recap becomes.

### 5.1 The first opening: the open-ended question

"How do you think that presentation went yesterday?" (Book p.393) becomes "how did the Saturday turn go for you," "what happened on the eight-top's second course," "how did the sequence feel on the fourteenth." The rule is hers and canon's at once: neutral, open, about a specific service, and not a closed question that "signal\[s\] that you've already made up your mind" (Book p.393). Her worst case, "Do you think you're bad at presenting?" (Book p.393), fails her test and fails canon's test one, a sentence that names its own effect. The question is about a service the records show, so that the person can answer from the same record the lead read. If the person's answer is an acknowledgment, her "I wish I had been more prepared" (Book p.393), the conversation is already at the shared problem and moves to what would change it. If it is "All's well" (Book p.393), the second opening.

### 5.2 The second opening: the observation

Situation, behavior, impact. The workbook's form for every example, "the situation, the observable behaviors, and the impact they had" (Workbook p.113), is the observation's spoken form and it is also canon's: a specific service, a specific act, and what it did to the work. "On the fourteenth and the twenty-first the captures show a re-fire on your section; both times the sequence call came after the plate left the pass, and the table waited." Her rules bind each part: "supportive, objective, and specific instead of emotional and generic" (Book p.394); owned as the speaker's perception, "you're not trying to assume their reality; rather, you're trying to share your reality with them" (Book p.394); and never "I feel like you've gotten a lot worse" (Book p.394), which is generic, about a trend in the person, and unreadable from any record. The observation ends with her question: "What did you think?" (Book p.394), and where the person asks for help, "Is there something I can help with? Something I should know?" (Book p.394) is the second question of the check-in asked again.

Her cycling rule survives: "If going directly to an empathetic observation isn't working, try a few cycles of reverting to Option 1" (Book p.394), and her last resort, "It may be time to give your own answer: 'Huh. My impression was that it was not that great. For example …'" (Book p.394), is permitted exactly as she frames it: the lead's impression, said as an impression, with the record as the example. What is not permitted is the sentence after that one, the verdict, and the check-in's form ends the conversation at the person's line whether or not agreement was reached.

### 5.3 Where, who, language, record

Where: the check-in, and a placed check-in when it cannot wait (section 6.4). Not the brief, the close, the leads' review, the gathering, or any room the roster is in (S10 P15); not the floor during service (section 6.1); not a message, a note, or a call off the clock (S11 5.1); not the mentor's paired service (S10 P32). Who: the lead who holds the person's check-in, the Maitre d for every floor seat (S8 P21), the kitchen's chef-gated; never a designation holder, who "holds nothing else the Maitre d's seat holds: no check-in" (S8 8.5); never a peer, a mentor, or a founder passing through. Language: the person's chosen language, the interpreter case founder-gated (S11 P20; section 7.7). Record: the person's one line and nothing else; the lead's observation is spoken and unwritten; the records the observation cited exist already and are cited by date.

### 5.4 The recap: none exists, and no one writes it

The workbook's first template is a letter from the manager recapping "what we discussed" with "examples that we discussed," "what you will need to work on," and a weekly check-in schedule "for the next \[X\] weeks" (Workbook p.113). At Sŏn no such letter exists and no one writes it. The person page is the recap: the person's own line, read back and saved, readable by them at any time (S4 P18). A manager's written summary of a conversation is free text about the person in the lead's voice, which the person page refuses (S7 P25), and a summary the person receives is a note the lead wrote outside a check-in, which the lead may not do (S4 P20). Her reason for the recap is the record: "so that the person always knows what they are learning" (WP p.18) and "If something is wrong, the person knows before the review" (WP p.19). The line does both, because the person wrote it. Where the person wants more than one line of their own, the line may be as long as the person makes it; the form limits the lead, not the person. Her weekly check-ins "to make sure you're continuing to improve" (Workbook p.113) are the placed check-in, brought forward once per interval and counted (section 6.4), not a lead's weekly schedule.

The three follow-on letters, the progress update, back on track, and insufficient progress (Workbook pp. 114 to 116), are performance-improvement documentation and are Session 14's; this page routes them there whole and states the constraint they arrive under: every check-in inside whatever Session 14 designs still writes only the person's words, so a written arc of a person's progress in a lead's voice does not exist on any page of this program unless Session 14 argues one into existence against S7 P25 and this page.

**P12 (Recommended).** Her hard-feedback conversation is kept as a way of talking: the open-ended question about a specific service first, then the observation as situation, behavior, and impact, owned as the lead's perception, cycled as she describes, with her last resort permitted as an impression and the verdict after it refused. Solutions after the shared problem.

**P13 (Recommended).** Hard feedback is said at the check-in or a placed check-in, by the lead who holds the person's check-in, in the person's chosen language, with the person's one line as its only record; never at the brief, the close, a review, a room, the floor during service, a message, or through a mentor, a peer, or a designation holder.

**P14 (Recommended).** No written recap of a hard-feedback conversation exists and no one writes one; the person's own line on the person page is the recap and may be as long as the person makes it. The workbook's initial documentation template is refused; the three follow-on letters are routed to Session 14 under the constraint that every check-in writes only the person's words.

## 6\. In the moment, during service

This is the session task's central problem and the ledger's. The ledger holds three things: the private correction is the check-in's (S10 P15); no room the roster is in carries a correction of a person (S10 P14); the lead may not write on the person page outside a check-in (S4 P20). Her chapter holds that "the sooner you and your report can identify areas for improvement, the sooner you can start making changes" (Book p.386). The task holds that a kitchen does this in the moment. Session 4 added a fourth fact the ledger did not carry into its summary: "Anything the lead wants to say between check-ins is said in person and written at the next one" (S4 P18). This section works out what is said live, what waits, how the wait is shortened, and where the pages disagree.

### 6.1 Two kinds of speech: the operating instruction and feedback about a person

During a live service, a range's holder speaks to the people doing the work all service long. Fire the four-top. Hold the sequence on twelve. That plate goes back. Re-set nine before the eight-top walks. Take the pyeong-sang's water. Walk them, do not point. Every one of those is a call inside a range on the object the range holds: the table, the plate, the sequence, the section, the threshold. The record permits them by construction: "the server owns the table relationship and resolves it using their authorized judgment" (WP p.13); the room's owner "owns the room" with coverage and pattern recognition and "backs up without intercepting" (Canon 1.0, section 08); the sequence designation "own\[s\] the floor's side of the pass for that service" and "the call to hold or fire the floor's timing" (S8 P22); the pass owner is command-and-control (S8 P23). None of these is feedback about a person. Their subject is the work and their verb is a present act.

The test that separates the two is grammatical and it holds at tempo because it needs no thought: an operating instruction has the object of the work as its subject and a present act as its verb, and it is complete when the act is done. Feedback about a person has the person as its subject and a pattern or a quality as its predicate, and it is not complete when any act is done, because it asks for a change in the person. "Hold twelve" is the first. "You're always late on the call" is the second. "Re-fire it" is the first. "You need to watch your sequence" is the second. The first is native to a floor and the house keeps it whole. The second is a correction of a person in the roster's hearing, and the environment refuses it (S10 P14), canon refuses it as spectacle (Canon 1.0, section 06), and the record refuses it as blame in the moment the system should have absorbed (WP p.17).

The rule has a second half that the Realist's read supplies. A live call that has to be repeated, at the same person, on the same object, inside one service, is no longer an instruction; it is a sequence failure or a readiness question, and the record's own discipline names it before anyone names the person: "Before intervening at the people level, identify which failure mode is actually present" (WP p.14). The range's holder acts inside the range (takes the table, re-sections, calls the room designation to back up), the service finishes, and the capture at the close writes the incident as a process. What was wrong with the sequence, the mise, the section shape, or the readiness read is fixed nightly. Whether anything is said to the person about it waits for section 6.4.

### 6.2 What the range's holder may say live on the floor and at the pass

By range holder, the floor's entries complete and the kitchen's chef-gated:

The server, at the table: everything inside the table's range, to a runner or a host at that table, about that table, now. "Water on nine." "Hold the second course, they are not ready." Never to a peer about the peer's section.

The room's owner (the Maitre d, or the room designation on services the Maitre d does not work): everything inside the room's range, to anyone on the floor, about the room, now: coverage, a re-seat, a re-section, backing up a table, the transition trigger, the escalation destination above threshold (S8 8.5). "Backs up without intercepting" (Canon 1.0, section 08) is the boundary: the room's owner may take a table's next act and may not take the table's account. What the room's owner may not say live: anything whose subject is a person. That constraint is the same seat's constraint at the brief ("takes no item about a person," S4 13.1) applied to the service.

The sequence designation, at the floor's side of the pass: the call to hold or fire, a station called down, the first answer to a kitchen-side disturbance (S8 P22). To runners and servers, about the sequence, now.

The door: the threshold's calls (S8 P24). To hosts and the room, about the arrival, now.

The pass owner: chef-gated (S8 P23). The team's reading, to the ceiling: every call at the pass is an operating instruction by construction, about the plate, the station, the fire, the hands; the pass owner's range is the widest live range in the house because the pass is where the sequence converges (Realist: "the pass is command-and-control"); and the one thing the pass owner may not say is the thing kitchens are known for, the correction of a cook as a person in the line's hearing, which the non-negotiables bound ("No toxicity ever," Deck 3.0, p.4) and which is not fast feedback but blame. The chef partner ratifies the pass owner's range and this reading of it.

What no range holder may say live: a person's manner, pace as a trait, attitude, or pattern; a comparison to another person; a reference to a prior service; a threat, a consequence, or a designation withheld ("you will not hold the room again"), because a designation is never a sanction (S10 P33); a customer's complaint relayed as a person's fault, because blame absorption runs toward the customer and the person both (WP pp. 17, 26). And no one may say any of it in a customer's hearing, which canon's "No spectacle during dinner service" (Canon 1.0, section 06) already forbids and which the record's "The way the phone is answered and the way the final course is plated" (WP p.20) places on the same footing as every other detail the house cares about.

### 6.3 What waits

Everything whose subject is a person waits for a check-in. That includes the good: the Realist's floor has a habit of the in-service "nice" that is a manager's praise in a room, and the platform is where a peer writes it (S11 P11); a lead's spoken praise of a person during service is permitted as a word and refused as a mechanism, because a lead who praises publicly and corrects privately has built a room where being unpraised is the correction. What waits also includes the hypothesis, the observation, the pattern across services, the question about what is in the way, and the person's own account, which the capture takes that night in the person's words and which the check-in opens on.

### 6.4 How the stack brings the check-in forward, and what "fast" means here

Session 4 placed the check-in "when the person's interval elapses rather than by a lead's memory" (S4 P15) and gave the lead a range to move one later "when the pre-service window is short-crewed or the brief needs the whole window" (S4 4.6). It gave no range to bring one forward. This page adds one, and surfaces it as an extension to be ratified beside S4 P15 and P20 rather than as a quiet revision.

The placed check-in. A lead may ask the stack to place a check-in ahead of the interval, once per interval, for a named person. The stack places it as it places every check-in: the first fifteen minutes of the person's next scheduled shift that the lead also works, in the pre-service window, paid as shift, on the schedule where the person sees it (S4 4.3). The interval resets from it. The schedule carries no reason and no reason is written anywhere; the schedule shows "placed" where it would show the interval's date. The lead may not cancel the standing check-in it replaces; the placed one is the standing one, brought forward. It may not be placed mid-service, at the close, or off the clock, and it may not be placed for a non-negotiable breach, which has its own path (section 11). The count of placed check-ins per lead per month is a line at the leads' review, read as a load reading on the lead's windows and never as a read of any person; a count above a parameter set at the reset goes to the reset as the extreme coach's tell, and a placed check-in the schedule cannot fit inside a stated number of services is a window fault on the schedule, not a person's fault.

Why this holds Session 4 rather than breaking it. The check-in stays on the clock, in the window, on the schedule, placed by the stack, with the same spine and the same refusals. It adds a second trigger for placement beside the interval. It does not make the check-in "dependent on a lead remembering to call it," because the standing interval still runs whether or not any lead places one; it gives a lead a way to shorten the wait, counted. The alternative, a lead who may not shorten the wait, is her forgot-to coach mandated by the schedule, and it loses the one thing she gets right.

What "fast" means at Sŏn, stated as a sequence. The work is corrected in the second, by the range's live call. The process is corrected that night, by the capture and the lead's review of it (WP p.13). The person's own account is on the record that night, in the capture the person wrote, before any lead's memory of the service has become a hypothesis. The conversation about the person's work happens at the next shift the two share, which the lead can make the next one. The house gives up one thing: a lead's correction of a person inside the service, which is the thing the record's blame rule, canon's no-spectacle rule, and the non-negotiables refuse anyway. What she loses by comparison is worth naming: her manager's data is the manager's memory, and the person's account arrives at the 1:1 after the manager's hypothesis has formed. At Sŏn the person's account is written first.

### 6.5 What the lead does between check-ins, without writing on the page

Three things and no fourth. The lead makes live calls inside the range, about the work (6.1). The lead reviews the capture and updates the process, off the floor, "the appropriate lead reviews, the process that allowed the failure gets updated" (WP p.13), and holds a private post-mortem that is process-focused and names no person (WP p.17; S4 P12). And the lead may say the placed line: in person, on the clock, off the floor, one sentence, that there is something about the work to talk about and that a check-in is placed for the next shift they share. The placed line carries no hypothesis, no observation, and no correction; it says a conversation is placed. It exists so that a person does not learn from the schedule alone that their lead wants to talk, which would be the surface saying what a person should have said (S11 P10's logic, in reverse). It is not written anywhere. It is not a message; a lead's note to a phone off shift obeys the rule that nothing is owed off the clock (S11 5.1).

What the lead may not do between check-ins: say the hypothesis or the observation in person and "write it at the next one" (section 6.7 surfaces this against S4 P18); write a note about the person anywhere; say anything about the person to a peer, a mentor, the other lead, or a founder except through the paths the register names (a non-negotiable flag; a Class B entry that names a mechanism); carry a capture's content to the person as a lead's finding, because the person wrote the capture and reads their own; or use the channel to write about a person, which the channel refuses (S11 P31).

### 6.6 What is lost if the house waits, honestly

The Realist's read: a floor learns fastest in the service, and a green server who is told at 8:20 that the sequence call is coming late will fix it by 8:40 if the telling is a call ("call it before the plate leaves") and will not fix it if the telling is a correction of them. The house keeps the first and loses the second, and the loss is small because the second was never the fast part. What is lost in fact is the lead's ability to say "here is what I saw tonight" while it is fresh, in a corner, after the last table. That is the close, and the close is allocated: the server's close is the thank-you note and the capture (WP p.26; S4 P12), and a lead's word in that window takes it from the customer. The team judges the loss worth taking, because the alternative reintroduces the manager's memory as the record and the roster's hearing as the room, and states it as a loss rather than pretending the placed check-in is the same as the moment.

### 6.7 The conflict surfaced: Session 4's "said in person" and Session 10's "the check-in's"

Session 4 wrote: "Anything the lead wants to say between check-ins is said in person and written at the next one, or goes through the feedback channel with the person's knowledge" (S4 P18). Session 10 wrote: "the private correction is the check-in's" and "criticize privately is the check-in and nowhere else" (S10 6.4, P15). Read together they permit a lead to say a correction in person between check-ins and forbid it. Both are recommended and neither ratified.

This page does not pick silently. It reads Session 4's sentence as narrowed by Session 10's: what a lead says in person between check-ins is the live call about the work and the placed line, and the correction itself is the check-in's, brought forward by the placed check-in. The narrowing is surfaced for the founders to ratify beside S4 P18 and S10 P15 together. The argument for the narrowing: a correction said in a corner during a service week and written at the next check-in is a correction with a lag between the saying and the record, in which the lead's memory holds it and the person cannot read it; that lag is the manager's notebook by another name. The argument against, which the team states and does not adopt: a house that forbids a lead to say a hard thing in a corner is a house that has made candor a scheduled act, and her whole chapter is a warning that scheduled candor is the ambush. The placed check-in is the team's answer to the second argument; if the founders find it insufficient, the alternative is to ratify S4 P18 as written and to let the correction be said between check-ins in person, off the floor, and written at the next one in the person's words, with the placed line as its minimum. The team recommends the narrowing.

### 6.8 The kitchen's version, chef-gated, developed to the ceiling

The kitchen's live speech is louder, shorter, and more continuous than the floor's, because the pass converges every station's timing into one call and the call is the coordination organ (Realist). Everything at the pass is an operating instruction by construction, and the team's reading is that the two-kind rule holds in the kitchen unchanged: the pass owner and a Station Lead may say anything about the plate, the fire, the station, the hands, and the sequence, now, to anyone on the line; and no one on the line may be corrected as a person in the line's hearing. The kitchen's "fast" is the same sequence: the call in the second, the kitchen's capture at its close (S4 P13, chef-gated), the person's own account on it, the check-in at the next shared shift. ## 7\. Feedback in each context the carryovers name

Seven contexts, each an inherited constraint, each answered in the check-in's form. None adds a surface and none writes anything but the person's line.

### 7.1 Inside the ninety-day plan: the not-yet form, and no verdict

Session 7 built "onboarding's hardest conversation": the third check-in reading a readiness form that reads not yet, "the rows named, the modules to retake, the next read's date, and the infrastructure line, with no verdict on the person" (`86akhb2x5`; S7 7.4). Her "get that feedback to the person quickly and strongly" was refused as a manager's form (S7 P47). This page inherits that everything said to a person on a plan is a state, a row, or the person's own words (S7 P25), and designs how a lead says a hard row aloud.

The form: the lead and the person read the form together, row by row, "whichever way it read" (S7 7.4). A row that reads not yet is read as the row: what the row asks, what the assessor marked, in the assessor's words on the form, which the stack wrote from the assessor's submission (S7 16.8). The lead adds nothing to the row. The lead's contribution is the open-ended question first ("what happened on that row, from where you stood") and then, if the person's account and the row do not meet, the observation as a specific service the row was read in. What follows is the plan's own mechanics: the modules to retake, the next read's date, and the infrastructure line if it is a second not yet (S7 7.4). The line written is the person's: what they will do before the next read and what the building must supply. What the lead never says: that the person is behind, that the plan is at risk, that a solo is unlikely, that the person may not make it, or any sentence whose subject is the person and whose predicate is a state the plan does not hold (S7 P28). A not-yet row is not a hypothesis, because the assessor already read it; the lead's hypothesis, if any, is about what in the service produced the row, and it is said as such.

The tension the carryover names, hard feedback without a verdict, is resolved by the form's own construction: a row is a fact the person can read, and a verdict is a sentence the lead would have to add. The rule is that the lead adds none. What "strongly" means at Sŏn is that the row is read whole and not softened, in the assessor's words, and that the next read's date is on the schedule before the check-in ends.

**P21 (Recommended).** A not-yet readiness form is read at the check-in row by row in the assessor's words, with the lead adding nothing to the row; the lead asks what happened on the row before saying anything, says an observation only as a specific service the row was read in, and the plan's mechanics follow (modules, the next read's date, the infrastructure line). The line is the person's. The lead never says a sentence whose subject is the person and whose predicate is a state the plan does not hold. "Strongly" means the row is read whole and the next date is on the schedule before the check-in ends.

### 7.2 To a designation's holder: about the range and the service

Session 8 wrote that a room designation who acted outside its range goes to the Maitre d "as a team-health entry the next day," and that feedback about a service a person held a designation on "is feedback about the range and the service, delivered inside the check-in's form," with a designation withheld never a verdict (`86akhcz71`; S8 8.5; S10 P33). This page adds the form.

The range is the object. A designation's holder is a person holding a range for one service, and the range card states what the holder may and may not do (S8 8.5; S7 I-range card). Feedback to the holder is about acts inside and outside that range in that service: the escalation not made at the threshold, the transition triggered early, the table intercepted rather than backed up. It is said at the person's check-in, not the next day at the brief and not by the Maitre d in the moment the seat is handed back, in the same form as any hypothesis: the service, the act, the impact, owned as the lead's perception, after the person's own account. The record cited is the team-health entry the Maitre d wrote, which names the act and the range and not the person's fitness, and the capture if there was one. The line is the person's. What is never said: that the person will not hold the designation again, because "it is never named as a reward or withheld as a sanction" (S10 14.3); and the count of times the person has held it, because the count is read only at the reset and "never as the holder's performance" (S9 P32).

The rotation floor and the eligibility rule do the rest without a word to anyone: eligibility is a complete readiness form and range card and no non-negotiable line (S8 P25), and the brief names from the eligible. A lead who stops naming a person to a designation after a service that went badly has made a sanction of a designation, which is a refused act, and the stack's count at the reset is where it shows.

**P22 (Recommended).** Feedback to a designation's holder is about acts inside and outside the range in that service, said at the person's check-in in the hypothesis form, citing the team-health entry and the capture, with the person's line as its record. A designation is never withheld as its consequence and its count is never spoken; eligibility and the rotation floor do the work.

### 7.3 Recruiting's two events

Session 5 fixed two feedback events to people in the house and not how they are given (`86akh7r6z`). Both are delivered here in the check-in's form, and the constraint holds that neither is a check-in and the lead writes on the page at a check-in only.

The untrained interviewer. "A scorecard returned without a named read goes to the Maitre d as a re-training trigger, and the interviewer does not run another stage until the re-training is done" (S5 P52; S5 12.2). The stack enforces the second half: the interviewer's pool status changes to "re-training" and the stack draws no stage to them (S5 P52). What the person hears: the trigger is a state on their training record on the person page, written by the stack from the scorecard's missing field, and it is read with them at their next check-in, or a placed one, under "what changed": the scorecard's field was empty, the read it should have named, the module to retake, the date the pool re-opens. The lead's hypothesis, if any, is about the scorecard as a record and not about the person's judgment of candidates: "the read wasn't named; what did you see in the practical that the rows didn't give you a place for" is the form, because an unnamed read is as often a rubric's silence as an interviewer's, and the calibration read reads the rubric (S6 P41). The line is the person's. Where the interviewer is a lead, the same trigger reaches the leads' review as a line on the calibration record and whoever holds a lead's check-in reads it with them (`86akhb2jg`); until that closes, the Maitre d's own re-training trigger is read at the partners' review as a load line and no one reads it with the Maitre d, which this page states as a gap and does not fill.

The contradicting decline. "A decline at the packet that contradicts every stage's scorecard goes to the leads' review with the lead's reason, not as an appeal but as a calibration read" (S5 12.3). This is one lead's judgment placed in front of the other lead, and the carryover asks how it is delivered without becoming a verdict. The answer is that it is not delivered to a person at all; it is read as a record at a meeting-shaped mechanism between two leads, and the record is the calibration read's line on the packet, which names the reads and the reason and not the deciding lead's competence. The leads' review reads it as the diagnostic reads everything: which mechanism the pattern touches (the anchor set, the rubric's rows, the interviewer note), and a change goes to the mechanism by version (S6 P41; S9 P24). A lead is not coached by the other lead, because neither holds the other's check-in (S3; `86akhb2jg`); what the lead hears is the line read at the review and the mechanism change, if any, with its reason. When the pattern repeats, three consecutive leads' reviews on the same object go to the partners' review by the recurrence rule (S9 P17), which is the record's own path and not a coaching one.

**P23 (Recommended).** The untrained-interviewer trigger is a state on the training record written by the stack, read with the person at their next or a placed check-in under what changed, with the lead's hypothesis about the scorecard as a record and not the person's judgment, and the pool re-opened on the module's completion; a lead's trigger is a calibration line at the leads' review read with them by whoever holds a lead's check-in. The contradicting decline is delivered to no person: it is a calibration read's line at the leads' review, acted on by a mechanism change with its reason, and its recurrence follows the recurrence rule to the partners' review.

### 7.4 The tension-slack hand-off

Session 9 built the one path a diagnostic read reaches a person: the seat and adjacent seats named, the domain fix made and read back, the person's own check-in line as the only person-side source, one permitted dated statement after recurrence ("that the seat remains occupied and its load remains carried by named adjacent seats after the domain's fix, with the dates"), then hand-off here (`86akhpvkk`; S9 P41). The coaching content "works only from the person's own lines and the domain's dated fixes" (S9, the carryover's first constraint); the skill-will motivation questions are refused as diagnostic reads and routed here and to Session 14; the career conversation is not a coaching surface.

What the check-in does with the hand-off. The lead reads the domain's dated fixes with the person under what changed: the re-sectioning, the designations rotated, the range card re-read, the schedule's shape, each a change the person already heard by the check-in path (S9 14.1 step 2; S11 P25). The lead asks what is in the way, and hears it as the record hears it, as the building; the person's answer is the only person-side source the diagnostic took and it is the check-in's own second question. The lead's hypothesis, where the lead has one, is bounded harder here than anywhere: it may be about a behavior in a service the captures show; it may not be "disengaged," which is the record's word (WP p.14) and the one word this page keeps off every lead's mouth, because it is a state of the person and the diagnostic itself may not write it (S9 14.1 step 4); it may not be a motivation question, whether the thing is new, episodic, or persistent, or whether something in the person's life is the cause (Book p.286), because both are refused (S9 P40) and the second is the intrusion the non-negotiables forbid (S9 P44). What the lead may say is the one permitted statement, in its own words, as a record the person can read: the seat remains occupied and its load is carried by named seats after the fix, with the dates. That is a fact about the domain and not about the person, and the person's line is their answer to it.

Where it goes if it does not change. The check-ins record the person's lines against that statement across the interval, and "only if the pattern the check-ins have recorded, and the person has heard at each, becomes a performance conversation" does it reach Session 14, by Session 14's design (S9 14.1 step 5), never Session 15 (S9 P41). This page adds the count that hands it: the permitted statement read at consecutive check-ins across the recurrence rule's thresholds, with the person's lines beside it and the domain's fixes unchanged, and nothing else, because nothing else was written.

**P24 (Recommended).** The check-in works the tension-slack hand-off from the domain's dated fixes and the person's own lines only: the fixes read under what changed, the second question heard as the building, the one permitted statement said as a fact about the seat's load with dates, and the person's line as the record. "Disengaged" is never said; the motivation questions are never asked. The hand-off to Session 14 is the permitted statement read at consecutive check-ins across the recurrence thresholds with the person's lines beside it.

### 7.5 A disagreement about a person, routed from the half-page

Session 11 fixed that a half-page whose subject is a person "is routed to the check-in and never decided as a hold" (S11 P29; `17tn048qc2b`), and that her collaboration conflict between two people is a coaching act whose work content takes the half-page and whose person content is this page's.

The routing. The acknowledging owner of a half-page (the range's owner inside a domain; the owning domain's lead across domains; a founder between the leads) reads its problem line. Across domains, where the two accounts reach two leads, neither lead says anything about the other's person to the other lead except as a mechanism line at the leads' review, because "nothing is assigned, reviewed, or escalated by a person standing above another person" (ledger) and no lead stands beside another's person either.

**P25 (Recommended).** A half-page whose subject is a person is declined as a hold with "routed" recorded and no content; each party's account goes to their own check-in in their own words with their own lead, the work between them goes back onto a half-page as the work problem, and no three-body conversation, no question to either about the other, and no line on either page about the other exists. Across domains a lead speaks of the other's person only as a mechanism line at the leads' review.

### 7.6 The recognition decline, said in person

The room's owner who declines to read a recognition that is "a correction dressed as praise, a read of a person's state, or a comparison" tells the peer "in person, off the floor," and what that conversation is, and how it stays a correction of the mechanism and not of the peer, is this page's (S11 P11; `17tn048qc2b`).

**P26 (Recommended).** The recognition decline is one sentence about the platform's rule and one about what would have met it, said by the room's owner in person, off the floor, on the clock, in the peer's language, never at the brief; it is not a check-in, writes nothing about the peer, and its count per period is read at the leads' review as a count on the mechanism. ## 8\. The response by type of failure

Session 10 handed this page the decision that "decides whether a capture is met with a process fix or something else" (S10 6.2; `86akht34y`). The designer's frame is the three types: basic, complex, and intelligent (profile, Edmondson). The record's frame is one question: "what allowed this to happen" (WP p.17). They are one rule, because the type is an answer to the record's question.

### 8.1 The three types, in the house's terms

A basic failure: a known process, on the sheet or the range card or the module, not followed in a service. The 86 not read before the table was told the dish was available; the allergy on the book not carried to the pass; the station opened without its checklist. The record's response is the capture, the lead's review, and the process fix (WP p.13), and the process fix for a basic failure is usually the process's placement (was it on the readiness block, was the module in the person's language, was the checklist on the surface the person opens) before it is anything about the person. Where the same basic failure recurs on the same seat across the recurrence rule's thresholds with the process's placement fixed (S9 P17), it is the shape section 3.5 named and it reaches the check-in as a hypothesis about a behavior in a service, with the captures as the record. That is the one path a basic failure reaches a person, and it runs through the check-in's form and no other.

A complex failure: a combination the system produced, no one step of which was wrong. The eight-top and the pyeong-sang seated inside the same fire window; a runner short and the sequence designation on a table; a re-fire that met a station's par at its floor. The record's response is the process fix and only the process fix; there is no person in a complex failure, and a lead who finds one has stopped diagnosing early. The capture names the combination and the process update names the mechanism (the pacing at the door, the par, the section shape, the designation's rule). Its read is the diagnostic's (S9 P1).

An intelligent failure: an act inside a range, taken on judgment, that did not work. The server's comp that did not land; the re-seat that made the room worse; the pyeong-sang's silence held a beat too long. The record's whole architecture exists to produce these: "pre-authorizing it signals trust to staff" (WP p.26), the Playground's "open-ended results" (Canon 1.0, section 01), and "training architecture that progresses from procedure mastery to autonomous judgment" (Canon 1.0, section 01). The response is that the system learns and the person is not corrected: the capture writes what was tried and what happened, the slot may read it as "what the system learned from the last close as process" (S10 P20), a peer may recognize it, and nothing reaches the check-in as a hypothesis, because a range used inside its limit and wrong is exactly what a range is for. Session 10 asked what a check-in does "with a range used inside its limit and wrong" (`86akht34y`): the answer is that the check-in hears it under what is working or what changed if the person brings it, and the lead says nothing about it that the person did not, because judgment after the readiness form is read only by range use as a count (S10 3.3) and an intelligent failure corrected as an error is the tight edge closing, the Deck's "Rules so tight the result is foreclosed" (Deck 3.0, p.14).

### 8.2 Who decides the type, and where it is written

The reviewing lead, at the capture review off the floor: the room's owner for the floor's captures, the Operations Lead for the inward strand's outputs read as a record (S8 P21), the kitchen's chef-gated. The type is a field on the process update, not on the capture and not on any person's page; the capture is the writer's own words and is not edited (S11 5). The type's count per period joins item 1 of the leads' review beside the capture rate (S4 P26): so many basic, complex, intelligent. A count of any type by person is refused; it is a ranking by another name (S9 P32). A capture with a person named as cause is re-written by the reviewing lead as process and the naming is a team-health entry on the writer's page only in the sense S4 gave it, a line written outside a check-in (S4 P20), which is to say: a capture that blames is a capture written wrong and the fix is the capture's form.

The one failure that is not typed is the non-negotiable breach, which is the flag's and not the capture's (S7 P45; section 11).

### 8.3 Blame absorption, held

"Management absorbs blame from staff" (WP p.17). In this design the sentence is mechanical: the process fix carries the reviewing lead's seat as its owner, and the evidence line names the mechanism (S9 P24); the person who wrote the capture appears on it as its writer and nowhere as its cause; the customer who saw the failure was met with the recovery the record specifies (WP p.26), which absorbed the blame at the table before the capture was written. What a lead absorbs, exactly, is the ownership of the fix. What a lead may not do is absorb it in words at the brief ("that was on me") because the brief carries no item about a person, the lead included (S4 13.1). The founders' version is Session 1's principle 4, blame the process and the founders' processes first, and it is the founders' to ratify (`86akh2qbc`).

**P28 (Recommended).** Failure is typed at the capture review by the reviewing lead, as a field on the process update and never on a person: a basic failure is met with the process's placement fixed, and reaches a person only as a hypothesis at the check-in after recurrence on the same seat with the placement already fixed; a complex failure is met with the process fix only and has no person in it; an intelligent failure, a range used inside its limit and wrong, is met with the system learning and the person not corrected, and reaches the check-in only if the person brings it. The type's count per period is read at the leads' review; a count by person is refused. The non-negotiable breach is not typed.

**P29 (Recommended).** Blame absorption is mechanical: the process fix carries the reviewing lead's seat as owner, the capture's writer appears as writer and never as cause, the customer was met with the recovery before the capture was written, and no lead absorbs blame in words at the brief.

## 9\. The feedback culture: informal feedback in every direction

Her phrase is "the one-off pieces of advice or observations that people offer to help their colleagues and reports in their day-to-day work" (Book p.395), and her diagnosis of Stripe is that it does well at commenting on work product and less well at "bidirectional, informal feedback" where "people frequently speak up and say something constructive without necessarily being asked" (Book p.396). This section says, for each direction candor can move in the house, what its surface is, what its record is, and what it may never become, given what the program already fixed: no person named in a room (S10 P14), nothing reaching the house that is not on a record (S11 P10), recognition as a peer's words about an act (S11 P11), upward feedback on the channel and the fourth question (S11 P30), and a concern about a lead with no destination until `86akhb2jg` closes.

### 9.1 Lead to person

The check-in, as sections 3 to 5 design it; the placed check-in when it cannot wait; the live call about the work; the placed line. What it may never become: a room, a message, a note, a manager's praise class, a designation as reward or sanction, a review input outside the person's own line. Its record: the person's line.

### 9.2 Person to lead

The fourth question, in the person's words, written only if they want it (S11 P30), asked at every check-in so that no person has to model the asking; and the channel for the house, a mechanism, a tool, a space, a process (S11 P30). A concern about the lead who holds the check-in has no destination the lead does not hold until `86akhb2jg` closes (S10 P15), and this page does not fill it; it states that the coaching model makes the gap larger, not smaller, because the lead now holds a hypothesis about the person and the person holds none the house will read about the lead. Her "solicit and welcome feedback, especially when you're still setting the tone of a working relationship" (Book p.397) is the fourth question at the first check-in of every plan, asked as she says to ask it, and the lead's working-with-me document (S7 P49) is the vocabulary a domain may use upward about the lead (S10 P30).

### 9.3 Peer to peer: recognition, and whether a peer corrects a peer

Recognition is designed (S11 P11): a peer's own words about an act in one service, on the platform, read at the brief. That is the whole of the house's mechanism for a peer's positive feedback and it needs nothing added.

Corrective feedback between peers is the harder question and the carryover's hardest case (section 7.6). The house's answer has three parts.

During service, a peer speaks to a peer about the work inside a range: the sequence designation to a runner, the server to the runner at their table, the door to a host. Those are operating instructions (section 6.1) and they are the only correction one peer gives another on the floor. A server who corrects another server's section is outside their range and it is captured as an interface question, not carried as a correction.

Between services, on the clock, a peer may say to a peer, in person, anything about the work: "the call on twelve came late for me" is a thing one person may say to another in a house that has no rule against people talking. The house builds no surface for it, requires none of it, records none of it, and refuses only three things: that it be said in a room the roster is in (the brief, the close, the gathering, a station huddle), because a correction of a person in the roster's hearing is refused whoever says it (S10 P14); that it be written anywhere, on the platform, the channel, a message, or a page, because a peer's words about a peer's work are the peer's own words only when spoken to that peer and become a read of a person the moment they are on a record; and that it be asked for by the house, because a norm that peers correct peers is the "informal relationships between individuals" the record names as bus failure (WP p.13) dressed as culture, and a person who does not want to correct a peer has done nothing wrong. Her Stripe diagnosis, that people do not "speak up and say something constructive without necessarily being asked" (Book p.396), is not a failure the house corrects by norm; every constructive thing a person could say has a destination that is not a peer's ear (the capture for a failure, the channel for a friction, the half-page for a disagreement about the work), and a peer who speaks to a peer instead has chosen the one destination with no record, which is theirs to choose and the house's to leave alone.

The mentor is the exception that proves it: the one peer the house names to a person, who "answers questions" (WP p.18) and hands the person the section in the paired services, and who "never scores and never writes on the person page" (S7 P8). The mentor's feedback is the paired service itself: what the mentor hands over and the person carries, in the person's account at the second check-in (S7 7.4). After the plan the mentor "never becomes a sponsor, a coach, or a channel for the lead" (S10 P32).

### 9.4 To and from the room's designation holders

A designation holder speaks to the roster inside the designation's range and only there (section 6.2). Feedback to the holder is section 7.2's. Feedback from the roster about the holder's service is a capture about the room's sequence or a channel item about the designation's rule (S11 P30), never an item about the holder; and the room designation's team-health entry to the Maitre d "the next day" (S8 8.5) is the Maitre d's own note about an act and a range, which the Maitre d reads with the holder at their check-in and writes on no page.

### 9.5 Her "asking for feedback," translated

Her four pointers (Book pp. 397 to 398), each placed. "Ask on different occasions and through different forums": at Sŏn the house asks, at every check-in's fourth question, and a lead does not also ask at the brief, after a service, or by message, because the fourth question exists so that asking is not a lead's initiative and because every other occasion is a room or off the clock. Her phrasing survives exactly ("What could I have done differently," not "How do you think that went"). Whether a lead asks a peer lead for feedback is a leads' review question and Session 15's. "Normalize the practice of giving feedback" by mentioning received feedback at the next team meeting: refused; the brief carries no item about a person, the lead included; what the roster hears is the slot's "what the system learned from the last close as process" (S10 P20), which is the house normalizing the fix and not the feedback. "Let the feedback sit": absorbed as the fourth question's rule that the lead repeats it back and does not explain (section 4.5). "Follow up on the feedback": structural, the channel's loop window and the item's state read back at the next check-in (section 4.5).

Whether a person asks. A person may ask their lead anything at the check-in, and the first question invites it. A person may ask a peer or the mentor in a paired service or between services, on the clock; nothing is owed off the clock and no peer is obliged to answer (S11 5.1). A person may not be asked by the house to ask.

### 9.6 Public praise, private correction, and team feedback in public

"Praise publicly and criticize privately" (Book p.396) is the record's shape already: praise publicly is the recognition read at the brief, a peer's words and never a lead's (S11 P11); criticize privately is the check-in and nowhere else (S10 6.4). Her exception, that "team feedback... should be data-driven and can be presented as an honest assessment of how things are going" publicly (Book pp. 396 to 397), is the goal page's state and the one thing from the leads' review spoken at the brief in one sentence with the evidence line's words as its reason (S4 P10; S11 5.1); it is the unit's read and never a lead's assessment in the lead's framing, and an underperforming unit is "a unit whose goal page reads not met, read by the diagnostic and never asked in a room" (S11 P6). Her "focusing on what you all learned" (Book p.397) is the slot's process item.

### 9.7 The culture, stated

Candor at Sŏn is not a disposition the house asks of anyone. It is the property of a system in which every hard thing has a destination that is a record, no destination is a room, and the one conversation about a person's work is placed by the stack, held by one seat, said in the person's language, and written in the person's words. The house does not tell its people that feedback is a kindness. It builds the check-in so that the hard thing is said on the clock within a service or two of the records that prompted it, the capture so that the person's own account precedes anyone's hypothesis, the platform so that praise is a peer's, the channel so that a friction is answered by an owner within a window, and the review so that it cannot contain a surprise. Her Don Hall line, "to be brutally honest you can't have a culture that's brutal" (Book p.396), is the non-negotiables read as the condition of candor: No Toxicity Ever is what makes the observation safe to say, because the observation is the only form a correction can take.

### 13.3 Jeong and Nunchi: developed, never graded

Recruiting never selects for them (S5 3.4). Coaching may develop them and may never grade them. ### 13.4 No performed conviction, and the Korean terms

No lead says "I believe in you," "we're invested in your success," or "feedback is a gift." The observation ends at its last load-bearing word. ## 15\. Decision rights for coaching and feedback

Every entry in the ledger's form: owner; range as may and may not; triggers, each with a named destination. An entry the record cannot furnish is marked incomplete and never filled by analogy. Session 4's check-in entry (S4 P20) stands and these extend it.

### 15.1 Forming and testing a hypothesis at a check-in

Owner. The lead who holds the person's check-in: the Maitre d for every floor seat; the kitchen's chef-gated; a lead's own holder open (`86akhb2jg`).

Range. May read, before the check-in, the person page, the plan block, the readiness rows and load read, the captures for the person's tables or stations, the range-used count, the domain's dated fixes, the schedule's facts, and the fluency and path lines. May say one hypothesis per check-in, after the person's answer to what is in the way or what changed, as an open-ended question about a specific service and then an observation of behavior and impact owned as the lead's perception. May cite records by date. May extend the check-in when the window has slack (S4 4.6). May name a strength as a specific act.
