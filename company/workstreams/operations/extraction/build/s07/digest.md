# S07 digest: onboarding (book p.216-223) and hiring mistakes (book p.224-227)

## Part 1: What the old page holds that's worth keeping

### Onboarding: what the book asks for

- Book p.216: a new person is "on high alert for context," and a company that just has them show up and start working misses the chance to inculcate culture. Curriculum order: the "why" (mission, story), the "what" (business, priorities), the "how" (behaviors, operating principles).
- Book p.216: company leadership should take part in onboarding; "stories, not bullet points" carry a message; the curriculum should be "the fastest possible ramp to success."
- A kitchen voice (Dominique Crenn, book p.216-217): "people just get thrown onto the job... it's not just about the kitchen," and training is "a continuation of things," never a one-time event.
- Book p.217: all prep work (paperwork, equipment) done asynchronously before onboarding starts. Nine outcomes of onboarding: a community of new hires; leadership's vision and principles; the business and products; the users' perspective; operating structures and goals; a picture of the org; key processes (compliance); where the team fits; ability to self-educate and find resources.
- Book p.217-218: internal mobility survey at every team change (5 questions, read over time for "bad mobility"); manager-to-manager transition guide.
- Book p.218-219: "Working with Claire" document, first meeting on mutual expectations across six headings (onboarding, operating approach, management style, communication preferences, initial priorities, career conversation); invites the new person to write their own.
- Book p.219: new leader onboarding standardizes then adds, New Leader Experience: welcome emails, prescheduled meetings/pre-reads, leadership assessment, coach, first-month actions, ninety-day 360. Table 5's six objectives (book p.220). Daily/every-other-day standup for critical roles (book p.221), balance between listening and acting. Direct report's sidebar in nine parts, "Commit to their success" through "Share stories and histories" (book p.221-224).

### Hiring mistakes: what the book asks for (3.4)

- Book p.224-225: most hiring mistakes surface months into tenure as a performance issue; some surface right away or before start date. Four signals: confidential information posted, misrepresented background, poor treatment of people pre-start or during onboarding, arrogance/bad behavior toward new teammates in week one. Anything violating the employee handbook. Minor issues: give feedback "quickly and strongly." Rule: "the main thing is to do something", rescind or terminate swiftly, with a short note to the team.
- Book p.225-226: closing the loop, a retrospective by the hiring manager; ATS data (time in process, funnel, interviewer stats); engagement/pulse survey prompts; marrying hiring data with performance data at milestones; surveying rejected candidates.
- Book p.227: "Hiring is expensive, but talent is everything."
- Chapter 3 exercises appendix (book p.228-259) reproduces workbook templates. Four already answered in a different chunk's hiring-and-training work (interview framework/rubric, sample questions, written exercise, candidate review framework). Four belong here: Manager Transitions Guide (book p.252-253), Working with Claire (book p.253-257), Working with Me template (book p.257), New Leader Experience with schedule (book p.257-259).

### White-paper commitments (source of default assumptions)

- WP p.18: onboarding begins before the first shift, information delivered, expectations set, peer mentor connected, locker assigned, uniform/tools ready before first clock-in. "Why comes before how": orientation to mission/values/behavioral standard precedes operational training. Every new person paired with a mentor, "a proven cultural steward rather than simply the strongest performer", who answers questions and reflects the standard. Progression documented from the start with milestones, skill assessments, feedback. A ninety-day plan sets expectations and goals with regular check-ins and course correction, "so early success is planned rather than hoped for."
- WP p.18-19: industry's training model is "sink or swim", shadow two shifts, catch fragments, get thrown in before ready, fail, quit, repeat, described as "designed-in failure." A learning platform feeds a progression structure; any qualified team member can create training modules and get paid for teaching; more modules completed, more earned; competency validation means "no one touches a table until they are actually ready." Cross-training is career development, not just operational insurance, a server who learns the kitchen becomes more valuable everywhere; goal is internal succession.
- WP p.19: performance systems develop people rather than judge them; advancement tied to demonstrated mastery via a competency conversation with the appropriate lead; "no surprises", a formal review never contains new information; feedback runs both directions.
- WP p.19-20: the house pays for full uniforms (tops, bottoms, aprons, shoes), smart lockers for every team member, and a real employee area with secure lockers and break space, "not a perk... dignity is a precondition for presence."
- WP p.9-14: the operational structure onboarding delivers a person into, no top/bottom web, new people always enter at the outside edge (the most sensitive position, not the bottom); for each employee the web inverts and they're at the center of their own knowledge map; documented competency requirements visible to anyone; a skill set unlocks automatically after a set period in a role, self-paced; advancement requires preparation, not permission; a server "owns the table relationship" and resolves it with judgment and a generosity range. Failure modes onboarding guards against: memory failure (teaching the same things repeatedly to a rotating cast), skill invisibility (competencies not documented or accessible), cooling failure. "Employees are the users," "blame the process, fix the process." Sequencing: bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results.
- WP p.21-22: the stack philosophy, "heavily deterministic software over a thin layer of AI," bought where the capability is settled and built where it's the edge; the OS surface as the home where software agents live; a structured feedback channel syncing to the execution system.

### Prior sessions already decided (context, not re-litigated here)

- The fifteen-minute check-in: paid, on the first shift of a pre-service window, placed by the system, three questions plus an upward question, one line written and read back to the person, every two weeks during the ninety-day plan and monthly after; it never delivers a verdict, task, pay, or discipline decision.
- The person page: created at the offer with ten fixed entries (seat/sheet version, strand, domain lead, decision-rights range, service periods, start date, mentor, plan start, first unlock, and what the candidate said they need); no assessment scorecard ever travels to it.
- Start-date conditions already fixed: mentor connected, uniform/locker ready, training platform carrying first modules, first check-in on a pre-service window the lead works, decision-rights range filled, never a new period's first service, never coverage before readiness. A date that can't be met for training reasons is a training-infrastructure load, not a recruiting one.
- The cohort is hired against the seat and onboarded against the first unlock; cross-period fluency is a development marker; a "ready-now pool" exists from day one.
- Training form for interviewers (module read, live session, observed stage, paired stage, then solo, all on scheduled shift) is the model this chunk's operational-training sequence reuses.
- Hiring calendar: kitchen chain unskippable; leads in the building six weeks before the dinner service gate.
- The founding-partner working-with-me exchange (all three founders, one spine, exchanged among them, chef partner's before signature) was decided earlier and is diligence, not onboarding; it is never required of hourly staff.
- A failed "range and escalation triggers stated unprompted" check was already named an onboarding failure to be fixed here.

### The onboarding behavior test (what onboarding is for)

- Intended behavior stated so it's testable, at close of a ninety-day plan a person: holds their seat solo at service tempo without a mentor; states their decision-rights range and escalation triggers unprompted and has used the range at least once; uses "customer" consistently in speech and writing and can speak to a dish with felt knowledge of dishes that exist; has opened the learning platform and moved on it unprompted; has told the house at six check-ins what was in the way and can say what changed; knows the next seat in their path from their own account.
- Onboarding may conclude only: service-readiness per seat (via a readiness form read by an assessor who is not the mentor); each milestone's state and date; what the building supplied vs. what the person said they needed; what in the training infrastructure did not hold (read as a load on the infrastructure, never on the person).
- Onboarding refuses to conclude: any verdict on the person; a level, placement, or pay outcome; anything like intuitive social/cultural mastery (that takes longer than ninety days to develop); month-six performance, cross-domain fluency, or a person's ceiling; whether a hire was a mistake (routed elsewhere); whether the person "fits" (no such category exists at hiring or onboarding); anything about the person the person hasn't heard.

### Who runs onboarding (functions, not one manager)

- No one function does everything a manager would in the book. Roles: the domain lead (holds the check-in, reads/writes the person page there and only there, names the mentor, places paired services and first solo, reads training-infrastructure issues to the leads' review, but does not teach modules, run the readiness read, write a verdict, or assign tasks); the mentor (a proven peer, paid as shift, beside the person at the live session and paired services, writes nothing on the person page); the assessor (trained, not the mentor, scores the readiness read, does not decide); the founder for the "why" content pre-opening and through the first quarter (who carries this after is an open question); the software/systems layer (creates accounts at acceptance, delivers materials, places check-ins, records completions, refuses a start date or solo without its conditions met).
- Option: distribute the manager's onboarding functions across five separate runners with stated ranges, so no single role holds everything and nothing depends on one person's memory. Reasoning: matches the flat/no-manager structure already decided and avoids a single point of failure or overload.

### Pre-opening cohort vs. steady state (a real sequencing problem)

- Risk/dependency: most of the book's onboarding material assumes a running house (existing team, curriculum, manager with context, a menu). Sŏn's first cohort has none of that, they are the team, there's no menu until the kitchen chain reaches it, and there's no learning-platform content until someone builds it.
- Mechanism-by-mechanism split needed between "steady state" and "pre-opening" form so pre-opening improvisation doesn't quietly become the permanent practice: person page at offer (no difference); start-date conditions (first modules must be built before any start date is set, by leads/founders); mentor connection (real proven mentors don't exist yet, see cohort-in-waves below); orientation/why block (run as one block per cohort wave, not individually); module set (interim, built before the cohort, on the training platform); observed/paired services (training services stand in for real services and are marked as reading procedure without load, the first four dinner weeks become the real load test); readiness read and first solo (form completed in a training service; first solos happen before the first live service so no one's first solo is opening night); felt-knowledge block (cannot exist until the chef's menu is fixed); onboarding read (a monthly rollup metric, first runs at a later "mechanism reset" milestone, not monthly from day one).
- Key sequencing/dependency: "training service" count needed before opening is a hiring-calendar item, not set on this page. Risk if under-set: floor and kitchen aren't actually ready by the dinner opening.
- Option: cohort arrives in two waves, a first wave small enough for leads (and the kitchen's owners) to mentor directly through training services, and a second wave mentored by the first wave once training-service records show the procedures were reproduced with no policy violations; first wave's mentor status is provisional until reviewed after opening. Reasoning: the book's mentor definition ("proven cultural steward") can't be met literally by a from-scratch team, but a single wave mentored only by leads risks overloading them right when they're also standing up their own domains, and hiring outside "trainer" staff isn't supported by the planned team size. This decision interacts with and should be made together with the pre-opening hiring burst/calendar decision.
- Option: no cohort member's first solo shift is the opening dinner service itself, every section/station on opening night should already have been soloed at least once in a training service beforehand. Reasoning: the book's "no one touches a table until ready" standard, applied literally to a from-scratch opening night, would otherwise put every single station's first solo under maximum pressure simultaneously.
- Legal/counsel-adjacent gap (silence, not resolved): who builds the first training modules before any team exists to author them ("any qualified team member" has no candidates yet), proposed answer: the leads for their domains, the kitchen's owners for kitchen content, the founders for the "why" and systems content; approved by two readers since the sole author can't approve their own; module set is version one until revised after the first training services.

### Before the first shift

- At acceptance: the person page is created; legal/payroll onboarding opens (content and compliance owned by whoever runs HR/legal, with counsel, not specified here); accounts are created for the person's other tools, in the person's chosen language.
- Two things the ten person-page entries need that recruiting alone can't supply: (1) the mentor entry needs an actual name, which requires a mentor pool and rules for naming (section on mentor, below); (2) the ninety-day plan's start date is the actual start date, not the acceptance date, so the first check-in lands within the paid period, not before it.
- Start-date conditions previously fixed apply unchanged: mentor connected, uniform/locker ready, training modules ready, first check-in falls on a pre-service window the lead works, decision-rights range filled, not a new period's first shift, not coverage before readiness.
- One additional condition surfaced (not yet decided): whether the foundational mission/values/principles documents must be finalized before any cohort member's start date, given the book's rule that orientation to mission/values must precede operational training. Option: make this a condition for the cohort's start dates but not for the two initial leads (who arrive early specifically to help finish that foundational work). Cost if adopted: cohort start dates (and the opening date) could slide if the foundational documents aren't ready in time; the alternative is to accept an incomplete "why" block on day one.
- "Mentor connected before arrival" defined as: mentor named from the pool at acceptance; the system introduces the mentor by name/role/contact method in the person's language; the mentor sends one message on shift introducing themselves and confirming they'll be present at the first live session; the mentor's shifts for the person's first two weeks are scheduled to overlap. Nothing is required of the incoming person in return, and the mentor never sees the candidate's application/interview file, they meet the person only through the seat description and the (limited) person-page entries.
- What arrives before the first shift, and how: legal/payroll paperwork and account provisioning through whatever HR system is in use; an "arrival note" generated automatically with the start date and hours (framed as a paid orientation block, not a working shift), where to arrive, the mentor's name/seat, the seat description and pay-transparency sheet, a plain-language rendering of the person's decision-rights range (to be read aloud together on day one), the first two weeks' schedule with the check-in visible, a link to (but not an assignment of) the first training modules, the person page itself, and one line stating nothing in the note is required before the first shift; the uniform, fitted at a paid scheduled block before the start date; a locker, assigned and named; tools ready at the person's station. No handbook exists yet, the (three) non-negotiables stand in for one until it exists, and the arrival note says so.
- Rule, stated as a default: nothing, no module, no reading, no call, no form beyond legally required paperwork, is required of a person before their first paid hour. What arrives before the start date is delivered to the person, not assigned as homework. (The book's line about "all prep work done asynchronously" is read as the house's prep, not the incoming person's unpaid labor.)

### Why before how: the "why" block

- The book's commitment: orientation to mission, values, and behavioral standard happens before operational training, delivered by leadership, using stories rather than bullet points.
- Risk/dependency: this block cannot be delivered in full until the company's mission statement and core principles are finalized and approved, a foundational-documents dependency this chunk does not own or resolve; the team's reading is to treat that document as the actual blocking dependency rather than filling the gap with placeholder language.
- Content and order proposed for the "why" block, once the founding documents are ready: (1) the mission statement, said once, and what it was chosen against; (2) the core principles, each illustrated with one real moment/story once the house has run, or a founding story pre-opening; (3) the behavioral standard, the three non-negotiables, the stated priority order (bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results), and the language rule that staff always say "customer"; (4) the operating structure the person has entered (the web from their own position outward, why the newest hires sit at the most information-sensitive point rather than the "bottom," what a domain lead is and isn't, "blame the process not the person"); (5) real stories that make structural points rather than founder hero-narratives.
- Who delivers it: the book asks for leadership to take part. Option: pre-opening and through the first quarter after opening, a founder delivers this block in person to every wave and every new hire; after that, whoever is later designated to carry the company's public voice internally would take over (an open question this page does not resolve); the fourth part (structure) can be delivered by the relevant domain lead once a domain exists; the mentor is present but delivers none of it.
- Cost of making foundational-document readiness a start-date gate: if the documents aren't finalized in time, cohort start dates (and by extension the opening date) slide. The alternative recommendation is to finalize documents ahead of the calendar need rather than let the gate slide, mirroring the same trade-off made for compensation-architecture readiness in an earlier chunk.

### The first shift, first week, first ninety days

- The proposed sequence for all operational onboarding mirrors the interviewer-training sequence already adopted elsewhere: modules read, a live walkthrough (no service), an observed service (person present, does nothing), paired services (working alongside someone, with the paired count matching what the specific role's complexity requires, not a fixed constant), then solo. Two differences from the interviewer version: the paired count is role-specific, and the transition to solo is gated by a readiness form scored by a trained assessor rather than by two people jointly scoring an interviewer.
- Book p.216-217's kitchen critique (Crenn) is read as describing "two shifts of shadowing", i.e., only the "observed" step, with the other four missing, which is exactly what this sequence is designed to correct.
- First shift for an hourly frontline role: one paid orientation block, no table/station, building tour, uniform (already fitted), the "why" block, initial account setup and app orientation, the decision-rights range read aloud and repeated back by the person, meeting the mentor, and being told what may be raised as a concern and to whom.
- First week: first modules, live walkthrough with mentor, one observed service, first paired service if the role's schedule allows. First check-in is not in week one, it's two weeks after the start date.
- Weeks two to six: paired services at the role-specific count, first check-in at week two, felt-knowledge tastings once dishes exist, readiness read at the end of paired services, first solo once the form passes, second check-in at week four.
- Weeks six to thirteen: solo service; a four-week "load read" (one check-in line per week) reads whether the person can actually hold the role under real service pressure, distinct from and after the readiness form (which only reads procedure, not load); range use and first escalation reviewed at week eight; development path opened at week ten; plan formally closes at the sixth check-in (week twelve/thirteen).
- Variant notes: runner role follows the same steps with runner-specific procedures (smaller decision-rights range); the bar role's domain lead and felt-knowledge content is beverage-specific; the host role's very existence and reporting structure is an open structural question this page doesn't resolve; kitchen content in full is deferred to the chef partner, though the five-step sequence, an assessor distinct from the mentor, and delivering the "why" block to kitchen hires the same as everyone else are fixed regardless.
- Kitchen-specific: no kitchen module content, live session, paired count, readiness rows, or felt-knowledge content can exist until the chef partner and the menu exist, a real sequencing/dependency risk, since kitchen hiring and training necessarily starts later on the calendar than the floor's but must still finish in time for opening.
- Lead variant: the first two leads arrive at least six weeks before the dinner-service opening gate, into a building with no live service yet; their "onboarding" consists of extensive pre-reading (all prior decisions, seat descriptions, founders' own working-with-me documents), delivery of the "why" block by a founder, and being present in the recurring leadership review meetings from day one; a later, externally-hired lead instead gets a peer co-lead as mentor and reads the domain's history/notes. A lead's plan goal is framed as a state of their domain running well, not a personal unlock, since the book gives no concept of "level" for a manager here. What the variant explicitly refuses: any formal leadership personality/psychometric assessment (same reasoning as excluding a similar tool from hiring, it reads a person for a role rather than for demonstrated competency); a paid coach for the leader's first months (framed as a spend this page won't price, left to the founders); a formal 360-degree review (replaced by the ongoing upward-feedback question already built into every check-in); welcome emails/an onboarding point person/checklist (handled by the automated arrival note and plan instead); "loaning social capital" from a manager above (rejected because leads' authority is meant to live directly in their domain, not be borrowed).
- Founder-gated: whether a coach is engaged for a lead's first months (option: none, since it's an unpriced discretionary spend and the leads' checked-in relationship with a founder/partner already covers the书 function).
- The first solo and readiness read: gated by a readiness form, scored during (or right after) a paired service by a trained assessor who is not the person's mentor and didn't run their paired services; the form scores role procedures reproduced correctly, several universal behavioral rows, a "people" evidence row, correct customer-language usage, the person's own stated decision-rights range and triggers (asked, not just recited from memory), and, once dishes exist, the felt-knowledge row. Outcome is binary: "ready" or "not yet" (never a composite score, never a verdict on presence/speed/fit). A domain lead can never self-certify someone or run the readiness read on their own reports, the same separation-of-duties principle used in hiring. "Not yet" names specific rows to redo and schedules another attempt; a second "not yet" on the same rows escalates as a training-infrastructure question (about the module, mentor load, or paired-service count) before it's treated as anything about the individual. Pre-opening, the readiness read happens in a training service and explicitly reads procedure only, not load, that's what the four-week load read after the first month of real dinner service is for.
- "Service-ready" is defined once, narrowly: a passed readiness form for that specific role and time period, nothing about cross-role fluency, judgment under pressure over time, or being "fully trained" in any broader sense.

### The ninety-day plan

- The plan is described as a set of automatically-tracked milestones on the person page (populated by the system where visible, and by the lead only during check-ins) rather than a document a manager writes freeftext. It's the same shape for everyone in the same role, varying only by dates and by what the person said at hire they'd need.
- The plan runs a fixed ninety days from the start date (not shortened by an early solo, not lengthened by a late one, except for the one allowed extension). A generic 30/60/90-day deliverables framework (as used for knowledge workers) is rejected as a form, a cook's first thirty days don't produce a "deliverable," they produce a station reliably held.
- Its form: one plain-language goal stated as an end-state (e.g., "holds the seat solo, with the range stated, by the plan's close", binary: readiness form passed and a solo has actually run); thirteen tracked milestones (orientation held, modules complete, live session held, observed service held, paired services complete, felt-knowledge passed, readiness form outcome, first solo held, four-week load read, range used, platform use beyond required modules, development path discussed, plan closed) each written by whichever party (system or lead) can actually see that fact; the person's stated needs from hire, with one line per check-in on whether the house met them; the six check-in dates. Explicitly excludes: any rating, scorecard field, free-text assessment of the person, a milestone about intuitive social/cultural fit, wage/rate info, or anything resembling a "probation" label.
- What the plan is explicitly not allowed to introduce as a new expectation: everything in it should trace back to what was already disclosed at hire (the role's requirements, the decision-rights range, core behavioral standards, and the customer-language rule). Two exceptions, explicitly flagged as new: the felt-knowledge block (impossible to test at hiring because the menu didn't exist yet) and familiarity with the internal tools/systems themselves.
- The six check-ins, each with a defined focus so the plan doesn't rely on a lead's memory: week 2, did the house deliver what the person said they'd need; week 4, how paired services are going, plus a channel for reporting where the training material doesn't match floor reality (feeding back to whoever owns that training content); week 6, the readiness form results, read together line by line; week 8, the four-week load read and whether the person has actually used their decision-rights range; week 10, the person's development path and what they want to learn next; week 12/13, formally closing the plan (goal met or not) and moving check-in frequency to monthly going forward.
- What no check-in may ever contain: a verdict on the plan, a pay conversation, a probation decision, or anything about the person's "manner."
- What the plan's close may never be treated as: a performance review, a probation outcome, a hiring verdict, or a signal about whether the person will stay. If a plan's goal is not met even after the one allowed extension, what happens next is explicitly routed elsewhere (to whichever future chunk handles underperformance and eventual departure) rather than decided here.
- Extension rule: the plan can be extended exactly once, by the lead together with the person at a check-in, to a specific new date, and only when a milestone was blocked by something the house failed to provide (a missing-language training module, an absent/overloaded mentor, paired services that ran short, dishes not yet tasted, a readiness read that couldn't be scheduled), never for the person simply not meeting the bar without a named organizational fix. A second extension escalates to a broader organizational review rather than being the lead's call again.
- Shorter plans exist for people already employed who add a new service period or move between departments, reusing existing modules/sequence at the receiving department's pace, inside the person's existing (now-monthly) check-in cadence, with no new ninety-day plan and no repeat of the "why" block.

### Training infrastructure and the learning platform

- The book commits to a platform that's engaging, personalized, and constantly expanding, authored by "any qualified team member" who gets paid for it, gated by competency validation. It doesn't specify what a module actually is, who approves one, what languages it needs to exist in, how completion gets tracked back to the person's record, or what stands in before the platform exists.
- Bought-tool notes (kept as facts, not endorsements of any brand system): one platform tool is being used as the interim/first form of the learning platform, with completion tracked per person per module and content organized as one path per role; a screen-recording tool is used for real people demonstrating real procedures in the actual space (never recording an actual customer or a service with customers present); the payroll/HR system handles legal onboarding, payroll, and account provisioning with no training content of its own; a workspace/productivity suite is the likely interim home for shared documents until a dedicated internal system exists; a scheduling tool needs to be able to refuse to schedule a solo shift without a passed readiness form (a capability to confirm, not assumed).
- An AI-generated-video tool was purchased for the training-production stack, but whether an AI-synthesized on-screen presenter is appropriate for any training content is an open policy question this page does not resolve (does synthesized media that isn't clearly disclosed as such cross a line the company wants to hold generally, given the company's broader "nothing should conceal what it is" stance). Until resolved, no module uses a synthesized presenter; if it's later allowed, the position is that it should be limited to narrating procedures only, never used for the "why" block or anything describing the customer, and always carry a clear disclosure line.
- What a training module should contain: the procedure as a sequence of concrete steps; which role and situation it serves; the underlying reason/decision the procedure exists for (not just the mechanical outcome); a real-person recording where the procedure is physical; any decision-rights range it touches, with the escalation trigger named; language, author, approver, and version info; and how mastery is demonstrated (i.e., which readiness-form row it feeds).
- What a module should do: exist in every language a role's incumbents actually need; take no longer than the standard check-in time unit to complete on a paid shift; be readable on a phone; get revised through a formal versioned-decision process when floor reality contradicts it; be understandable and use consistent terminology.
- What a module should refuse: a rigid script for customer interactions (beyond one or two fixed courtesy lines), the goal is judgment, not memorized delivery; teaching a procedure with no decision inside it, when the role actually holds a decision there; treating a skill that only develops with experience (like reading a room) as something to "execute" from a script; a scoring quiz that grades the person (readiness reads do that job); teaching a competency as "required at entry" when it's actually meant to develop over time.
- Authorship/approval: "qualified" to write a module means the person has demonstrated (validated) the competency themselves; each domain's own lead approves modules for their area, the kitchen owner approves kitchen content, and anyone handling compliance/food-safety content needs sign-off with counsel input. Approval is against the written standard, not personal taste, and an author can't approve their own module.
- Who builds the very first module set, before any team exists to draw from "any qualified team member": the leads for their own domains, the kitchen's domain owners (or chef partner) for kitchen content, and the founders for the "why" block and systems-related content; the set covers only what's needed to reach the readiness read (not later development content); it's approved by two readers side by side since a sole author can't approve their own; it's explicitly marked "version one," expected to be revised after the first real training services surface what's wrong, via the week-four check-in feedback line.
- Payment: the book describes two distinct payments, one to authors for approved modules, one to learners for completions, but neither has a mechanism defined; that belongs to a later compensation-design chunk. Three constraints carried forward regardless of that later pricing decision: authors are paid for a module that's actually approved against the standard (not merely written, to avoid incentivizing filler content); learners are paid via the normal completion-tracking mechanism, not a bonus for opening a module; mentoring counts as paid teaching time, not a volunteer favor.
- A monthly "onboarding read" is proposed to replace the book's survey-based closing loop (onboarding-effectiveness pulse questions, a milestone survey to managers): instead, one computed summary drawn automatically from everyone's plan data, time from start date to readiness, share of people who pass their readiness form on the first try, count of "not yet" outcomes by category, count of extensions/infrastructure issues with their named fixes, and whether the house delivered what people said they needed at hire. No target number, no name attached to any line, first run at a later company-wide "mechanism reset" milestone rather than from day one.

### Felt knowledge and the menu (a real sequencing dependency)

- The concept of "felt knowledge" (staff speaking about a dish from real experience of it, not a memorized description) can't be taught or assessed until an actual finalized menu exists, which itself depends on the chef partner being hired and the menu being finalized, a separate dependency chain this chunk doesn't control but must sequence against.
- Operational reading proposed: felt knowledge means the person has actually tasted every current dish, can describe what it is and why it's made that way in their own words when a customer asks (and say nothing when a customer doesn't ask), and never resorts to generic filler description words. The readiness-form row for this should test only those three things, nothing about intuition or timing of when to volunteer information (which isn't scorable).
- Sequencing dependency stated plainly, without inventing a number of days: no floor readiness form can pass its felt-knowledge row before the cohort has tasted every dish on the finalized menu, with the chef's explanation given, in an actual training service. The number of training services needed before opening depends on menu length, kitchen readiness, and cohort size, none of which is set here, and it needs the chef partner's input plus the founders holding the calendar. A menu that changes between the tastings and opening re-opens the row for the changed dishes. A menu finalized too late to allow proper tastings before opening is named explicitly as a sequencing failure that should delay opening rather than being worked around.
- Ongoing (steady-state) rule once open: any menu change adds a tasting requirement for the new dish before anyone serves it alone.

### Cross-training and the first "unlock"

- Two independent timers run on a new hire from day one: the ninety-day plan's clock, and a separate, longer, role-specific "unlock" timer for when a person can begin developing the next skill set in their own role, set per role in advance and running regardless of onboarding-plan status.
- Every skill track and its requirements should be visible to everyone from day one, and any person may start any module on any track at any time. What's gated is the actual practical assessment for a second domain, proposed rule: no cross-domain competency assessment is scheduled for someone until their own first plan has closed with its goal met, since being assessed in a second domain before finishing the first spreads a person too thin.
- Open structural question, not resolved here: for a competency spanning two domains, which domain's lead has authority to sign off on it. Working assumption used elsewhere in this material: the receiving domain's lead signs it, with the person's original domain lead simply informed.

### The mentor

- Eligibility to be a mentor: has closed their own ninety-day plan successfully (or, pre-opening, has a passed training-service readiness form); has no unresolved conduct issue on record; has completed a mentor-training module; is in the same or an adjacent role/domain as the person they'd mentor; and has agreed to do it. This is a maintained roster per domain, visible to the leads, and does not rank people against each other.
- Naming: the relevant domain lead names the mentor from that roster at the time of offer, so recruiting can record the name on the person's file and connect them before the person arrives. The lead cannot name themselves, the person's future assessor, someone off-roster, or someone already mentoring another open plan (named explicitly as a risk of overloading one person). Matching should consider language and schedule overlap, never personality/rapport, which is called out as reproducing whatever bias already exists in who happens to be in the room.
- If no eligible mentor exists for a role, the start date can't be set, that becomes a training-infrastructure issue to resolve, not a recruiting failure.
- Pay: mentoring hours are paid as regular shift time, scheduled explicitly as a "teaching body" during live sessions and paired services; whether mentoring earns anything extra (beyond hourly pay) is left to a later compensation-design chunk, with the constraint that it should be recognized as teaching, not as an unpaid favor.
- What a mentor does: teaches practical building/section/procedure knowledge not fully captured in modules; is present at the live walkthrough; answers questions in the first week and after; is the "teaching body" during paired services; models the behavioral/customer-language standard by example; shows the new person where to route "what's in the way" type concerns.
- What a mentor explicitly never does: score the readiness read (conflict of interest, same reasoning as never having a candidate's interviewer also be their sole hiring decision-maker); write anything on the person's page; give any verbal verdict on the person to the lead or anyone else; assign tasks, schedule, or discipline; deliver the "why" block or the decision-rights range explanation; sit in on the person's check-in; see the person's original application/interview materials.
- The relationship continues past the formal plan only if and how the person themselves chooses to continue it (asked explicitly at week 10); either party can end the pairing by telling the lead, with no note held against either person's record.
- Flagged tension, surfaced rather than resolved: the book calls for a "proven" mentor, but a from-scratch opening cohort has no one who's proven anything yet in this specific building, hence the two-wave proposal above, explicitly marked as a founder decision because it's a hiring-calendar and headcount question.

### Decision-rights range, the org "web," and standards, delivered, not just written

- The commitment that every frontline/floor person can state their decision-making range and escalation triggers unprompted (already an existing readiness-check requirement) is treated as something onboarding must actively produce, via five checkpoints: the range stated aloud at the job offer itself; read aloud together and repeated back on day one; taught with two written worked examples in a module; observed and then practiced (handed off) during paired services; and finally tested unprompted at the readiness read and again referenced at week eight's check-in. Not just servers/floor managers, every role with any decision-making range goes through all five checkpoints.
- Practical detail carried over from an earlier decision: any not-yet-finalized numeric thresholds within a person's range (e.g., a dollar limit) should be explicitly presented to the person as "not yet set" rather than the training program inventing a placeholder number.
- Explicit refusals in how range is taught: never present it as permission a manager grants case-by-case on the night; never imply that using it less is "safer" (the source material explicitly frames under-use of a decision-making range as a failure mode, not a virtue); never teach an escalation trigger without naming exactly who/where it escalates to; never teach a specific dollar figure as the answer if it hasn't actually been set yet.
- The org structure ("the web," with new hires entering at the most-sensitive edge position rather than the "bottom") should be drawn out for the person at orientation from their own position outward, and again in miniature every time a training module names who has decision authority over something, "escalate" alone is never treated as a sufficient answer; it must name a specific destination.
- Core behavioral standards (three non-negotiables) are delivered twice: once inside the "why" block as commitments the house is making to the new person, and again at the end of day one framed as what the person themselves is entitled to raise a concern about, to whom, and that raising a concern is never held against them or noted on their file. What happens after a concern is raised is explicitly left to a different, later chunk (departure/conduct handling) rather than invented here.

### Hiring mistakes, worked against onboarding (book 3.4)

- The book's framing: most hiring mistakes only surface months in as a performance problem; a minority surface immediately (four named signals: leaking confidential info, a misrepresented background, mistreating people pre-start or during onboarding, arrogance toward new teammates in week one). Immediate-signal cases should trigger swift action (rescind or terminate) with only a short, low-detail note to the team.
- How the ninety-day plan should read "a hire that isn't holding": strictly as a factual state, never a verdict, a specific missed milestone with its cause, a stated need that went unmet, or a repeated infrastructure problem (e.g., a module that was never available in the person's language, a mentor carrying two open plans at once, paired services that consistently ran short, a readiness read scheduled during a flagged/overloaded shift, a solo scheduled as the only coverage for a section). The philosophy carried over: "when something goes wrong, the first question is what allowed it to happen, not who failed."
- Proposed order of investigation when a hire isn't holding: check the organizational/training causes first (module quality/language/version, mentor's workload, paired-service count and whether services ran short, readiness-read conditions, scheduling, whether tastings/menu content was actually available, whether the house delivered what the person said at hire they'd need) before any question is asked about the person themselves. Only after ruling those out does it become a question about whether the original hiring decision itself was sound (routed to whichever chunk audits hiring-calibration accuracy).
- What the plan can never conclude on its own: that the person should leave, that the hire was a mistake, that the plan should be cut short, or that a probationary period has "failed", none of those states exist in this design. If the plan's goal still isn't met after the one allowed extension, what happens next (up to and including departure) is explicitly handed to two other, later chunks: one covering "how a not-holding hire gets addressed" generally, and one covering the actual mechanics of managing someone out.
- Specific scenarios worked through: a serious policy violation discovered between offer acceptance and the first shift should be handled through the same conduct-flag mechanism used at any hiring stage, with an accepted-offer withdrawal handled by whoever manages employment paperwork, with counsel; a serious violation in week one goes to both leads jointly, with next steps left to the later chunks; a minor, recurring issue (e.g., chronic lateness) should surface as a factual check-in line about what's getting in the way, not as a manager's "quick, strong" verbal correction, only a pattern that's been documented across multiple check-ins (and that the person has already heard about) becomes anything more serious; departures are communicated to the rest of the team with minimal detail, consistent with the plan brief never naming an individual's personal situation.
- The book's proposed "closing the loop" mechanisms (ATS funnel/interviewer-quality metrics, engagement/pulse survey questions, marrying hiring data to performance data, surveying rejected candidates, sentiment analysis on interview notes) are mostly already covered by mechanisms decided in the hiring chunk (a pipeline read, a calibration read, a candidate pulse survey at every hire/decline decision) plus the employee engagement survey and the onboarding read described above. Explicitly rejected as separate new mechanisms: a standalone manager milestone survey (asks for a manager's impression rather than a hard fact), and sentiment/text-mining analysis of interview notes.

### Working-with-me documents, the "New Leader Experience," transitions, mobility survey, and the personality-grid tool

- Working-with-me documents for later, externally-hired leads (distinct from the founder/chef-partner diligence version already decided elsewhere, which this chunk does not re-open): proposed that a lead reads the founders' own issued versions before their first shift, then writes their own between weeks four and six of their own plan (once they've actually seen the operation), reviewed at plan close, and readable by their own team, never required of, or suggested to, hourly staff.
- The book's New Leader Experience program is broken into its individual objectives and mapped one-by-one onto existing mechanisms rather than adopted as a standalone program: manufacturing team excitement about a new leader is rejected outright as inauthentic; giving quick context is handled by pre-reading materials; forging connections is handled by existing standing meetings; balancing listening vs. acting is handled by an existing "quiet period" convention for new leaders; reinforcing alignment with principles is handled by the working-with-me document itself rather than a formal personality assessment (rejected, reads a person for a role, same objection as elsewhere); course-correcting on friction is handled by the leader's own check-in with a partner/founder. A formal leadership assessment tool, a full 360-review process, welcome emails, and a dedicated "onboarding point person" role are all explicitly rejected as separate mechanisms, each mapped instead to something already built (pre-reads, the automated arrival note, the plan, existing check-ins).
- The book's manager-transitions guide/meeting (for when a report changes managers) is rejected as a standalone meeting; instead, when a person's domain lead changes, the new lead simply reads the person's existing page/history before the next scheduled check-in, and the person and new lead review it together at that check-in, nothing is passed between leads about the person outside of that shared, person-visible record.
- The book's internal-mobility survey (asked at every internal move) is rejected; instead, internal moves are already captured by other existing records (the automatic skill "unlock," an internal-transfer/pool-hire record, or a period/domain move record), and the existing upward-feedback check-in question already captures the book's underlying "is this a healthy move or an escape" concern on an ongoing basis for everyone, not just movers.
- The "work style" personality-grid tool (an Analyzer/Director/Collaborator/Promoter-style framework from the book) was already excluded from hiring in an earlier chunk, on the reasoning that service-industry hiring panels structurally over-select for assertive, extroverted people, and letting that same bias grade candidates would just reproduce it; and that development should instead run through open, equally-rigorous role requirements for every position rather than a personality read. Where it can return: only as a voluntary self-awareness exercise inside a leader's own working-with-me document (something a person says about themselves), never as an instrument used to evaluate or place anyone, and never surfaced on any person's formal record.
