# S5 digest: Recruiting (Book p.167 to 192; Workbook p.42 to 66)

## Part 1: What the old page holds that's worth keeping

### The book's demands for this chunk

- Book p.168: talent is "destiny"; hiring must involve nearly everyone and hold rigor at every level, not only leadership; leaders who "waltz in" lose credibility even with strong résumés.
- Book p.169: talent acquisition reframed as marketing and sales, a funnel from traffic to conversion to onboarding; conversion is not the end state, successful onboarding is.
- Book p.170: a hiring pyramid, volume hiring at the base with a set interview committee, becoming "more focused and customized" toward the top; a bespoke executive process.
- Book p.171: founder interviews every finalist while under "100 employees or less per founder"; founders model the no on a candidate who is "just fine," and that no should eventually be built into process rather than left as a founder-only instinct.
- Book p.174: careers page and job description should be clear about environment and expectations without being "overly verbose or prescriptive"; watch for language that "transmits bias."
- Book p.175: recruiter (and possibly coordinator) once past the initial team (roughly 10 to 20 people) and hiring consistently; a full recruiting team once hiring multiple people a week; "a recruiter should never be a substitute for a hiring manager."
- Book p.175-178: hiring as everyone's job; a mutual-commitments structure between recruiting and everyone else.
- Book p.178-181: role definition before opening a search ("Build insights on talent needs and candidate success"); the three-circle employee (good at the work, has impact, loves it); honest descriptions that let candidates self-select; referrals valued but capped (diversity risk); screening practices.
- Book p.184: her three questions for any role - what is the work, what does great look like, how do you assess against that benchmark - answered before sourcing, never mid-interview ("do not use your interview process to calibrate your understanding and expectations of a role").
- Book p.189: the experience trap and the "playbook thinker"; look instead for "raw curiosity and signs of pure learning aptitude"; caution that experienced candidates are often just better at being interviewed.
- Book p.190: promotion-from-within guidance, roughly one-third internal, one-third external, one-third a toss-up, offered for fast-growing companies without enough internal bench; warning not to assume the people who got you here will get you there.
- Book p.190-191: mixed results from search firms; better to lean on personal network, board, advisers first; "you are ultimately the recruiter."
- Workbook p.43-45: interview framework structure - competencies assessed, stages, who may run each, length, a rubric per stage - is the object recruiting must produce before a seat opens.
- Workbook p.43, 47: her "Recruiting for Recruiters" loop defines a recruiter's core competency as full life-cycle ownership, sourcing, closing (pitching against competitors, pushing back on candidate asks).
- Workbook p.49-50: a recruiter is scored partly on diversity of pipeline; her screening step includes résumé review and an optional written/coding assessment for some roles.

### Research and how the book's ideas land in a restaurant

- A funnel narrows to one decision-maker and treats a decline as a loss; a decentralized structure with no single hiring bottleneck and a formal "return to pool" state doesn't fit that model cleanly. The useful residue of "funnel" thinking is measurement (counts, stage timing, source), not the posture of advertising/closing.
- A pyramid that scales rigor by altitude doesn't fit an organization that classes seats by the shape of the work rather than seniority; a phone screen for a high-volume role is not "lower rigor," it's a different process for different work.
- Distinguishing seats by shape of work rather than level: high-volume roles (phone screen), physical-skill roles (paid practical replacing the industry's unpaid tryout shift), leadership/salaried roles (a working meal/dinner as final round). A given seat can sit in more than one class.
- Unpaid tryout shifts are named explicitly as "frequently degrading, legally exposed, and a poor predictor of actual performance" - the paid, real-environment practical is the intended replacement.
- A "years of experience" requirement is flagged as one of the weakest predictors of performance; competency, not credential or tenure, is the intended read.
- Candidate format choice (phone/video/in-person) as a "qualification filter" is in tension with the same source's claim that format choice "protects dignity" - reading format as a seriousness signal risks penalizing candidates with real constraints (e.g., transportation). Old team's reading: format is the candidate's own signal, never a score or basis for decline.
- Search-firm value is questioned for leadership seats that don't map to standard market titles/roles; a firm may not be able to position an unconventional split of responsibilities.
- Self-directed mastery (something a candidate taught themselves without being asked) is used as evidence of growth potential/curiosity, echoing the book's guidance on raw curiosity as a signal.
- A "hiring committee" model is replaced by independent, structured scorecards plus a single decision-owner; disagreement is captured by naming a specific, structured read rather than open discussion - this preserves the book's own point that disagreement should be specific and capability-tied, without a standing committee.
- One labor pool serving multiple service periods (not separate hiring per daypart) means recruiting has to think about scheduling/period fit as a candidate attribute captured on the seat's record, not a separate hiring track.
- A follower-candidate (someone who applied because they'd been following the business publicly) already has a deep, largely accurate picture of the operation; treating their eventual "no" carelessly risks losing them as a customer/community member too, not just as an applicant.
- Publicity/content around a hiring search: showing the search process, the seat, and the reasoning is treated as valuable, low-risk content; showing an identifiable candidate (image, voice, application, scorecard, anything from an interview/practical) is treated as high-risk and reserved for leadership-level review with written consent obtained only after a decision, never as a condition of being considered. Never invite an audience to weigh in on a hiring decision, never stream or film an assessment stage, never use a candidate's rejection as content while they're in process.

### Risks, failure modes, sequencing and dependencies

- Founding-seat gate: recruitment design for a founding culinary partner/chef seat cannot proceed until it's settled whether that seat is a co-founding partner (negotiated) or an executive hire (recruited through the standard leadership process). Whichever way it resolves, it gates: the seat's own hiring process design, every kitchen-side seat/hiring material, and (in this house's structure) the kitchen pipeline overall, because kitchen practicals, plates, and rubrics can't be built until that seat is filled and a menu exists.
- Interviewer training must exist before any interview is run, but pre-launch there is no training platform and no trained pool yet; the founders (then early leaders) are the only available interviewers and are themselves untrained by the source material's own definition. Needs an interim answer for how founders are certified/trained before a real training system exists, including in more than one language if the first hires speak languages the founders don't.
- A "final round dinner/meal" for leadership and specialized seats logically requires a functioning dining room, but leadership seats (the first couple of operating leads, in this design) must be hired before any room exists. Needs a pre-opening substitute (e.g., a meal at another establishment) with equivalent read but at real cost, and awareness that reading a candidate in someone else's environment tests against that environment's own standards, not the house's.
- A "single sheet given at the first interview with everything needed to decide," including compensation mechanics, can't be delivered as designed until compensation architecture is finalized - yet early leadership hires' first interviews may need to happen months before that architecture is ready. This is a sequencing conflict, not just a missing number.
- The practical assessment (a paid, real-environment tryout) has known costs to a running shift: reduced slack, an assessor's attention, a slightly slower section/table. Should not run on a shift already flagged short-staffed, on the first shift of a newly opened service period, on any shift being filmed/streamed for content, or on a schedule set to hit a hiring deadline rather than the shift's own needs.
- Payment/employment classification of a paid practical (payroll vs. some other engagement form) is unresolved and carries legal exposure either way; needs a definitive answer with counsel, and an interim answer for the first hiring cohort in the meantime. No practical should run without a settled interim form.
- A pipeline that depends on someone remembering to check it is treated as a system failure; unanswered applications and "ghosting" a declined candidate are treated as failures to design against, not individual lapses.
- If pool candidates (previously declined, invited to reapply) aren't scored to the same standard as new candidates for critical-path roles, "readiness" becomes theoretical rather than tested; a pool candidate for a critical operational seat should still take a scored, real-environment practical before holding it.
- A "one-third internal / one-third external / one-third toss-up" promotion ratio is written for a fast-growing company outrunning its own bench; it doesn't map cleanly onto a single-location business whose growth is in additional service periods rather than headcount scaling, and there's no internal bench at all on day one (every early hire is external by definition).
- Referral-based sourcing and founder/network-based sourcing both tend to reproduce the existing team's makeup; if diversity of the team is ever addressed as a goal, it needs a monitoring mechanism (which sources dominate a pipeline, which stage disproportionately declines a group) built in regardless of whether a target is ever set, so the data exists if a policy position is later taken.
- A temperament/work-style typology (introvert/extrovert x task/people orientation) used as a hiring or team-composition tool is flagged as a structural hazard in a service environment: floor/service pressure tends to reward assertive, expressive people, so any hiring or interviewer pool skewed toward "the room's read" will systematically underweight quieter, detail-oriented candidates, and nobody will notice because the same profile ends up doing the noticing. The structural fix proposed is not "try to value the other type" but rather: give the detail-oriented type of work (kitchen prep, operations/back-of-house) its own seats with their own rigor and never treat them as a stepping stone to guest-facing seats; keep measurement/reporting in systems rather than dependent on any one person's disposition; and score the practical assessment by a fixed rubric administered by someone who does not also make the hire decision, so the interview room's "feel" about a candidate never substitutes for a structured read.
- One labor pool serving multiple times of day (not just one long dinner service) means a person hired for one period may eventually also work another; recruiting needs a place on each seat's record for period/schedule fit, and succession/promotion planning needs to account for movement between periods, not just up a ladder.

### Legal and counsel questions (verbatim in substance)

- The employment/pay form for a paid practical assessment is unresolved: is a candidate on a paid practical classified as an employee for that day (on payroll) or engaged some other way? Each option carries consequences that aren't yet named. This needs to be closed with counsel; until then, an interim form is needed for the first hiring cohort, and no practical should run without one.
- Consent from a candidate to be shown publicly (identity, image, voice, application materials, or any content from an interview/practical/final-round meal) should only ever be requested and given after a hiring decision is made, never as a condition of being considered or advanced - because a candidate asked to consent while a decision is still pending cannot meaningfully refuse.

### Positions the old team argued (rewritten as options, no verdict)

**On whether a "funnel" model fits:**
Option: keep "funnel" only as a measurement structure (stage counts, time-in-stage, source tracking), not as a philosophy. Reasoning: a funnel implies a single narrowing path to one decision-maker and treats a decline as attrition; this structure has no single hiring bottleneck, and a well-handled "no" is designed to route a candidate back into a future pool rather than being lost, so the funnel's "conversion" endpoint and its "discard" behavior don't match, but its counting utility does.

**On classing seats:**
Option: class every seat by the shape of the work (high-volume, physical-skill, leadership/salaried, plus a founding-partner class held separately) rather than by seniority, and let the seat's class determine which pipeline stages it includes, not how "seriously" it's run. Reasoning: the source material itself splits treatment by role type, not by rank, and treating a phone-screen-eligible role as lower-rigor than a leadership role misreads why the stages differ.

**On what interviews should assess ("fit"):**
Option: score only four things per stage - a candidate's competency for the role's specific type of work, evidence of self-directed growth/curiosity, alignment with baseline conduct standards and comfort operating with real autonomy (rather than needing to be told exactly what to do), and what the business itself would need to provide for this person to succeed - and treat "fit," "attitude," or "culture fit" as invalid, unscoreable categories. Reasoning: vague fit-based scoring is where bias and inconsistency creep in; naming four specific reads makes every score defensible and comparable across interviewers.

**On the temperament/work-style typology:**
Option: do not use any temperament/personality typology (like an introvert/extrovert, task/people-orientation grid) anywhere in hiring - not as a scorecard item, not as an interview question, not as a team-composition target. Reasoning: service-floor pressure predictably rewards assertive, expressive personalities; letting that show up in hiring will entrench a skew where quieter, detail-oriented people are systematically screened out, and nobody notices because the people doing the noticing share the same skew. The tool could still be useful later purely as a self-awareness exercise once someone is already on the team.

**On the "org chart" item required on the candidate transparency sheet:**
Option: replace a traditional org chart with a rendering of the person's position and its immediate connections within the non-hierarchical structure (their role, its neighbors, who they'd turn to), drawn by hand if no formal visual exists yet, rather than ever drawing a conventional chart with the candidate at the bottom. Reasoning: the source material explicitly argues an org chart misrepresents how the organization works and who's replaceable; handing a candidate a conventional chart at their first interview would contradict that argument before their first day.

**On referral pay:**
Option: do not pay a cash bonus for referrals. Reasoning: referrals are treated as valuable information from people who know the local labor market, and it's suggested that peer recognition (through whatever internal recognition system exists) already provides acknowledgment; introducing a cash incentive risks turning a trust-based signal into a transactional one, and it's suggested that watching pipeline data over time will show whether referrals are flowing without a bonus anyway.

**On whether the business takes any position on team diversity/composition:**
Option: don't set a target or state a position on the makeup of the team, but do build a habit of periodically reviewing pipeline data for whether any single source dominates a role's applicant pool or whether any stage disproportionately declines a particular group, purely as a monitoring habit with no target attached. Reasoning: the underlying source material states inclusion-adjacent mechanics (like letting a candidate choose their interview language) but never states an actual position on team composition, so setting one wasn't something the prior team felt authorized to do on its own; but not tracking the data at all would make it impossible to ever act on a position if one is later taken.

**On search firms for leadership hires:**
Option: don't use a search firm for the first couple of leadership hires. Reasoning: those particular leadership roles are structured unusually (a split, peer-level version of a general-manager role) and don't map onto standard market job titles a firm would search against; the publicly documented build-out process is argued to already reach the same audience a firm would; using a search firm is also a real cost, which recruiting design itself isn't positioned to weigh.

**On staffing a dedicated recruiting/HR seat:**
Option: don't create a standalone recruiting seat at all, even as the team grows past the thresholds the book names. Reasoning: the book's recruiter role exists mainly to run scheduling/logistics (arguably automatable through the hiring software already chosen) and to "close" candidates by selling the role against competing offers - but a fully transparent offer (one sheet with everything a candidate needs to decide) is designed specifically to remove the need for a "closer." What's left - candidate experience standards, compliance/record-keeping, and each hiring manager's own domain - already has an owner elsewhere in the structure.

**On the founder's interview role pre-launch:**
Option: give founders one specific, narrow stage in the process (checking baseline conduct standards and whether the business can actually support what this candidate needs) rather than a broad, instinct-based "final say" veto over every hire. Reasoning: a broad founder veto risks functioning as a review from above a decision-maker who's supposed to own that decision, and the book's own "just fine, but something feels off" no is explicitly flagged as something to eventually build structure around rather than keep running on gut feel - this treats that as a starting design rather than an end state to grow into.

**On when the founder-interview stage ends:**
Option: retire the founder-level interview stage for frontline/non-leadership roles once the business has been open and running for roughly a quarter, but keep it in place indefinitely for leadership-level hires. Reasoning: leadership hires are closer in kind to the founders' own level of responsibility and are argued to warrant a founder's continued involvement, while a frontline role's process should be able to stand on its own once it has a track record.

**On the founding chef/culinary-partner seat (argued both ways without resolving):**
The record disagrees with itself, describing the seat both as an equal founding partner (with an equal vote on things like whether a new service period is ever built) and, in other internal documents, as a seat still to be filled by an executive hire against named criteria. Two full paths were laid out without picking one:
- Option A (partner): treat this as a partnership negotiation, not a recruiting process - it wouldn't run through the standard hiring pipeline at all, existing evaluation criteria would function as founders' own due diligence questions rather than a scorecard, and a final step would be the candidate cooking for the founders in a kitchen of the candidate's choosing. Reasoning: this reflects the source material's more developed argument that this seat is a founding one, freeing the culinary vision to be led by someone who isn't also carrying the rest of the business.
- Option B (hire): run the full leadership hiring process, treat the existing evaluation criteria as a literal scored rubric, and treat the final "cooking" round as a formal practical assessment like any other leadership hire. Reasoning: other parts of the record use hire-oriented language for this seat and lay out specific hiring criteria as if for a candidate being assessed, not a partner being courted.
Whichever way it resolves, both paths agree that: the kitchen-hiring pipeline (kitchen practicals, plates, rubrics) can't proceed until this seat is filled and a menu exists; any public content about this search should be framed as the founders' search process, not about candidates; and the ownership of the food's identity as a brand rests with the business regardless of who is executing it.

**On the "final round dinner" before a dining room exists:**
Option: for the small number of leadership hires made before the restaurant is open, substitute a comparable meal at an existing local restaurant the founders choose, applying the same rules around cost, no filming, and no scoring of the outside restaurant's own staff. Reasoning: the intended final-round read (how a candidate behaves as a customer, how they treat service staff, whether they can hold a table conversation without performing) can't happen in a room that doesn't exist yet, but the read itself is considered valuable enough to preserve in substitute form.

**On the internal-promotion ratio:**
Option: don't adopt any fixed ratio for internal-versus-external hiring. Reasoning: the book's "roughly one-third each" guidance is written for a fast-growing company that's adding headcount faster than it can develop people internally; this business's growth model is different (new service periods, not fast headcount scaling), it starts with zero internal bench on day one by definition, and a ratio would be a lagging measure of what already happened rather than something that shapes decisions going forward - a simple internal-promotion-rate metric, tracked over time, was suggested as more useful.

**On when the internal-succession commitment starts "counting":**
Option: treat the internal-succession commitment as starting to apply from the first departure/opening after the business is running (not from day one, since there's no internal bench yet), with a rule that pool candidates get checked before any outside search begins, and require any leadership-level outside hire, after enough time has passed, to be accompanied by a documented note explaining that no one internal was ready and how close the nearest internal candidate was. Reasoning: this makes the internal-succession commitment testable and self-honest rather than an aspiration that's never actually checked against what happens.

**On "cross-period fluency" (working more than one time-of-day shift/service):**
Option: name working across more than one service period as its own recognized kind of professional growth, parallel to the source material's named "cross-strand" fluency (someone who's skilled in more than one kind of role), and track it on a person's record. Reasoning: because one labor pool is expected to eventually staff multiple times of day, the ability to work well across periods is a real, valuable skill the existing structure doesn't have a name for yet; whether it should affect pay is left to a compensation-design process, and where it fits structurally is left to a team-structure design process.
