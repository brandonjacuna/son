# S11 digest: team-building complexities, diversity and inclusion, team communication

## Part 1: What the old page holds that's worth keeping

### 4.6 Team-building complexities

**Distributed/remote-team equivalent.** Son has no remote team in the book's sense; the real condition is non-co-presence by shift-hour, not headcount: whoever worked the morning may be gone by the time a later shift starts, and every shift is worked by a different set of people. Risk worth carrying forward: information can fail to move between shifts if it depends only on people telling each other things ("verbal handoffs, tribal knowledge, and informal relationships between individuals. When the relationship breaks, the information stops. This is not a culture problem. It is a systems problem," WP p.13); the fix is framed as "system maintenance, not people management" (WP p.14).
- Book's Table 9 treats "a team split across two or three different offices" (book pp.336-337) as the closest analogue, with coordination (making sure people are aware of and feel involved in decisions that affect them) as the central risk.
- Book's async-work mitigations worth keeping as reference: strong documentation to support asynchronous work (book p.333); balance workload so no one waits idle on a colleague (book p.333); map processes so no one waits hours for someone to become available (book p.333), illustrated by an engineer waiting overnight for an owner's approval and the lesson that most systems need "a self-service interface" (book p.334) rather than an approval queue.
- Book's rule that the moment an informal conversation becomes a work discussion or decision, it should move to a format everyone can access (book p.335) is worth keeping in substance: a decision made only verbally isn't safely a decision until it exists somewhere written.
- Book's caution that chat tools have retention limits, so anything needing permanent record should live elsewhere (book p.336).
- The one genuinely distributed relationship at Son pre-opening is the founders (and a future chef partner or other remote hire); book's advice to "document communication norms in more detail than you think you need to" (book p.338) applies most directly there.

**Cohesion and participation.** Book's answer is consistent norms that put everyone on a level playing field (book p.335); her example of a remote employee who can't hear the room and stays silent (book p.338) maps to a floor equivalent: a kitchen runner, someone whose English is a third language, or someone who missed the pre-shift briefing. Book's specific inclusive-meeting practices (video links, acoustics, active facilitation, notes, book p.337) don't transfer directly since there's no meeting during service, but the underlying goal, no one's ability to be heard depends on being physically in a room, is worth preserving. Book's airtime rule, giving someone who hasn't spoken in a team meeting a chance to (book p.358), generalizes to: every person needs some way for their voice to reach someone, that doesn't depend on a room and doesn't rely on a manager happening to notice silence.

**Going global / cultural differences.** Book has a manager learn national cultural dimensions (Hofstede) and map the company against them (book pp.341-342); flagged as a real risk if applied to people rather than markets, since scoring individual team members on a cultural dimension amounts to reading them by a demographic proxy. Book's more durable point, worth keeping: participation in any voluntary social or business-adjacent gathering is the person's choice, never a condition of anything (book p.342).
- Book's "adding remote workers" factors, maturity of the operating system, the nature of the role, manager support, and the person's experience level (book pp.342-344), are most relevant if Son ever adds a genuinely remote role (e.g., a distributed chef partner); a mature, well-documented operating system is what book says makes adding a remote member safer (book pp.342-343).
- Book recommends an engagement survey specifically to check how connected remote employees feel (book pp.344-345); flagged as a question this project needs to weigh against a general skepticism of survey-based reads elsewhere in the operating system.

**Underperforming teams.** Book's diagnostic sequence: probe the root cause in one-on-ones, then hold an open team conversation with four questions, why are we behind, what's in or out of our control, what can we do now, how much do external factors matter (book pp.345-346), then branch into three causes: the goal itself was wrong, a skill or collaboration problem, or a fractious team, the last diagnosed with a round of individual interviews "like a 360° review process but for a team" (book p.347).
- Risk worth flagging: an open team conversation framed around "why are we behind" can read as management judging the group publicly; worth deciding whether that conversation should happen as a group at all versus through individual check-ins plus a leadership-level read of the metrics.
- Book's fix for a wrong goal: reset it, and "be transparent internally about the fact that you're resetting your goals and why" (book p.346).
- Book's fix for a two-person collaboration conflict: meet the two people "first separately and then together" (book p.347); her explicit option to "consider shifting one or both people to another team" (book p.347) is a real lever worth naming, alongside the risk that a transfer can look like (or function as) quiet punishment if not handled carefully.
- Book's team 360 and her suggestion to "return to your personality and work style assessments" (book p.347) are named as tools other operators use; caution worth carrying forward is that team-level personality/style assessments can drift into typing or labeling individual people.

**Working with other teams / dependencies.** Book's guide: identify dependencies during planning, set up a semi-regular check-in by email, embed a person on the other team, form a working group with a clearly designated decision-owner, and escalate constructively to whoever is the shared decision-maker for both teams if a disagreement can't be resolved (book pp.348-349). Book's closing point, worth keeping regardless of mechanism chosen: a better structural approach sometimes removes the dependency entirely rather than managing it forever (book p.350).
- Option: treat cross-department dependencies (e.g., kitchen and floor) as documented interfaces, reviewed on a fixed cadence, rather than standing meetings or an embedded liaison. Reasoning: fewer recurring meetings, but requires real discipline to keep the documentation current or it silently goes stale.
- Option: keep a lightweight recurring check-in between department leads instead of (or alongside) written interfaces. Reasoning: simpler to start and more familiar, but can drift into meeting bloat and doesn't scale as the organization grows.

**Managing through uncertainty and crisis.** Book's three moves: be transparent up to a point, restate the vision, bias toward action (book pp.350-351). Acceptable script worth keeping as a model: "We don't have the details of a plan in place, and it may take us a while to get there, but I'm working on it" (book p.351). Book's Covid-era example: a company that "methodically communicated each decision and kept the entire company informed with emails and a resource page updated daily" (book p.352). Book's Reid Hoffman sidebar (pp.352-354): an existential threat was handled by naming it explicitly, running a bounded eight-week test, and getting a written answer, and it turned out not to be a crisis after all; his lesson, worth keeping directly: "we needed to acknowledge that we didn't have all the answers, and we needed to have a plan to start getting them" (book p.354).
- White paper's own standard for this: "Crisis communication is transparency first... the response is honesty about what happened, the real impact, and the fix" (WP p.31).

### 4.7 Diversity and inclusion

Book frames three areas, hiring; performance assessment, reward, and recognition; and running teams (book p.356), reasoning that "a great team and company are not only built by hiring well but also by building an environment that fosters and retains talent" and that "without equitable processes and an inclusive environment, the team will not be successful" (book pp.354, 357).

White paper commitments relevant here, kept as citations: candidates can choose their interview language and are interviewed by someone who speaks it, described as protecting dignity (WP p.17); every interview asks whether the building itself is equipped to create the environment a given candidate needs to succeed (WP p.18); engagement is measured across psychological safety and values alignment (WP p.23).

**The central gap.** Nothing in the white paper states a goal, a read, or a position on what the composition of the team should be, the book's diversity chapter assumes an employer has taken a position on this; Son's founding materials have not.

Book's concrete tools, kept as reference with a caution on each:
- "Assess your team portfolio. What are the backgrounds and viewpoints you're lacking?" (book p.356): reading a team's composition this way requires reading who people are, which cuts against a hiring approach built to record only job-relevant facts (like language) and nothing about identity.
- "Don't start interviewing until you have a strong, diverse pipeline" (book p.356): raises whether a seat is ever held open to wait on pipeline composition, trading off against simply needing the seat filled; a held-open seat becomes a load on the people covering that gap.
- "Add someone to your interviewing team who has a very different background from yours" (book p.356): raises whether interviewers should ever be selected for their own personal diversity as a deliberate practice, versus selected only for job-relevant reasons.
- "Develop and retain a diverse leadership team," with internal promotion rate as the leading measure (book p.357): the white paper's own ambition, "the building rarely hires a manager from outside at all" (WP p.19), already implies an internal-promotion-rate metric that could serve this without adding a new measurement.
- "Establish a common, agreed-upon way of evaluating the best person to promote" (book p.358): a documented, consistent promotion process is the general fix regardless of the diversity framing.
- "One of the ways I come to understand whether my team contains less visible elements of diversity is in the work we do to get to know each other, our backgrounds" (book p.355): flagged as a genuine tension, asking about people's backgrounds can build real connection, but a general policy against asking about someone's origins or family (for both legal and dignity reasons) cuts against it. Legal/counsel question, verbatim in substance: what can lawfully be asked about a candidate's or employee's background or family in this context.
- Book's equity-check examples, calibration for performance management, and pay parity analysis for compensation (book p.358), a pay-parity read has not been designed and would need real mechanics plus counsel's involvement; no financial figures or mechanics are detailed here, per project scope.
- Book's closing idea about teams uniting around shared qualities and a "collective vision," and Valdary's three tenets (book pp.359-360), are relevant only if a mission/values statement is separately being drafted; not detailed further here.
- "How you collect agenda items, plan an offsite, run a meeting" (book p.358): whatever this project decides about meetings generally should be checked against inclusion goals.

**The composition position (whether Son states any goal about team makeup, and whether it collects voluntary self-identification data).** Legal/counsel questions, verbatim in substance: what protected-characteristic data may lawfully be asked, recorded, retained, or acted on; whether any hiring-pattern read that could be construed as acting on such data is lawful; and what language requirements a given seat can lawfully carry. These are counsel's questions, not this project's to resolve.
- Option: state nothing beyond a conduct-only position, the house applies a consistent, identity-blind hiring process, and separately monitors its own hiring "reach" (where candidates come from, whether any interview stage declines candidates from a given source or language at a different rate) with no targets and no demographic inference. Reasoning: consistent with a hiring approach built to never read or record who a person is beyond job-relevant facts like language; requires no new data collection.
- Option: state a broader aspiration about hiring reach (e.g., that the candidate pool should be as broad as the customer base) without collecting self-identification data. Reasoning: makes a values statement about effort without new data collection.
- Option: state a diversity aspiration in the book's own terms (that the team should reflect some target composition). Reasoning: matches conventional practice and outside expectations, but is not measurable without collecting self-identification data (see legal question above), and commits Son to tracking something its hiring process is currently built not to record.
- Underlying data question: should candidates ever be asked to voluntarily self-identify (protected characteristics)? Book's own bias-control method relies on reviewing outcomes by self-identified group (book p.205). Option: don't collect it, reach monitoring runs on source/stage/language data alone. Option: collect it voluntarily, after a hiring decision point, restricted to aggregate use only and invisible to anyone involved in deciding, this still needs counsel's sign-off on form, wording, storage, and retention.

**Hiring-reach monitoring, as background research (not itself a decision).** A hiring pipeline can be checked for whether a single source (referral, job board, etc.) dominates a given seat's candidates, and whether any interview stage declines candidates from one source or one chosen language at a noticeably different rate, read only as a pattern, with no target, no name, and no inference about any individual candidate, used only to broaden recruiting reach (post elsewhere, activate other sources) or flag a stage/interviewer for coaching. This is one concrete mechanism a hiring-diversity commitment could run on without collecting demographic data.

### 4.8 Team communication

Book's ask, in short: run a team that isn't co-located by documenting more, moving work conversations into a shared, accessible format, leveling the playing field between whoever is in the room and whoever isn't, and meeting in person on a cadence; build and run an inclusive team; and communicate constantly, share the same information with everyone, pass decisions down with framing, keep a snippets document, and think each day about what to tell people. Quote worth keeping: "Communicate, communicate, and by the way, communicate" (book p.360).

Underlying premise worth naming plainly: the book assumes a desk-based, co-present team led by a manager who communicates by meeting, email, and chat, with a layer of leadership above passing information down to that manager. That does not describe a restaurant team, and every recommendation below should be read against that mismatch.

**Structural facts worth carrying forward as research, independent of any particular design choice:**
- The restaurant's version of "distributed" is time, not geography: whoever worked one shift may be gone before the next one starts, and every shift is worked by a different group of people.
- A shift team exists only from the pre-shift meeting to closing and then dissolves, unlike an office team that persists week to week.
- Staff will likely work in multiple languages; the white paper commits to letting a candidate choose their interview language (WP p.17), which becomes an ongoing fact the workplace has to keep serving after hire.
- Most staff won't be at a desk or reading messages between shifts; anything meant to reach the team should assume it's read in short bursts, probably on a phone, before or after a shift.
- Practical/legal flag: sending information or requests to an employee's personal device outside their paid shift shouldn't create any expectation of a response, since off-the-clock engagement raises wage-and-hour questions; whatever system is chosen should distinguish "available to read" from "owed a response."

**White paper commitments relevant to communication design, kept as citations:**
- A peer-recognition mechanism that lets colleagues acknowledge each other's work "in the moment," described as landing differently than management praise "because it comes from the people who understand the work" (WP p.19).
- A structured feedback channel "that syncs to the execution system," so staff can raise issues and see follow-through, reasoned as "feedback without follow-through breeds cynicism... the loop gets closed" (WP p.22).
- The pre-shift briefing described as equal in importance to the signature dish (WP p.20); the shift close described as what the next shift's server needs to inherit (WP p.13).
- "Information cannot move between domains because the communication infrastructure is verbal handoffs, tribal knowledge, and informal relationships between individuals. When the relationship breaks, the information stops. This is not a culture problem. It is a systems problem." (WP p.13)
- "the system is not surfacing preferences" is named as a symptom of information not reaching people before they need it (WP p.14).
- Feedback runs both directions: "Team members weigh in on management effectiveness, operational improvements, and culture, because the best ideas and the earliest warnings both come from the people doing the work" (WP p.19).
- An employee-NPS-style question, "would you recommend working here", tracked regularly rather than annually (WP p.22).
- Crisis/change communication standard: "honesty about what happened, the real impact, and the fix" (WP p.31); crisis response is named among the most closely reviewed categories of public communication (WP p.31).
- "Language shapes culture" is given as the reasoning for a consistent internal vocabulary (WP p.25).

**Decisions the book calls for, framed as options rather than a stated answer:**

1. *How does information reach a team that never all works the same shift?*
   - Option: a fixed, repeating pre-shift briefing as the main spoken channel, backed by a written record everyone can check afterward. Reasoning: guarantees everyone eventually gets the same information despite non-overlapping shifts, and creates a record for anyone who missed it.
   - Option: rely primarily on a written or digital system and treat verbal briefings as a supplement. Reasoning: less dependent on someone remembering to say something out loud; risk is staff may not reliably check a written system between shifts.
   - Option: rely on shift leads verbally passing information down the line. Reasoning: simplest to run, but the white paper names exactly this pattern ("verbal handoffs, tribal knowledge") as a failure mode (WP p.13).

2. *How is a decision or change communicated so no shift hears about it differently or later than another?*
   - Option: write the change down first (dated) and only speak about it after it exists in writing, so no one's verbal account becomes the record of it.
   - Option: allow a lead to announce a change verbally when it happens and document it afterward. Reasoning: faster, but risks different shifts hearing different versions and no clean source of truth if the change is later disputed.

3. *What is the shared internal vocabulary, and does it need translation?*
   - Option: maintain a running list of the languages the team works in (drawn from what languages positions require and what languages staff are hired in) and put important written materials in every language on that list. Reasoning: matches the interview-language-choice commitment (WP p.17) and keeps materials genuinely accessible.
   - Option: default to English with informal translation help from bilingual staff as needed. Reasoning: cheaper and simpler, but risks putting unpaid burden on bilingual staff and some staff getting information later or in less detail than others.
   - Either way: if bilingual staff are expected to do translation work, it should be treated and paid as work, not a favor.

4. *What can staff say to each other and to leadership, and where does it go?*
   - Peer recognition: option is a dedicated way for staff to publicly thank each other for something specific, read aloud periodically. Risk: recognition mechanisms can drift into rankings, leaderboards, or manager-authored praise if not deliberately kept peer-driven and action-specific rather than a judgment of the person.
   - Staff feedback about the operation: option is a dedicated channel with a guaranteed response window (acknowledged, then resolved, scheduled, or declined with a stated reason, within a set number of days), reasoned on "feedback without follow-through breeds cynicism" (WP p.22). Risk: without a clear rule against naming a specific coworker or manager in it, it can become a place where people are discussed without recourse.
   - Open structural question: what's the process when someone has a concern specifically about their own manager? The book assumes an escalation path up a management chain; a flatter structure may not have an obvious answer, and this needs its own decision.
   - One-on-one check-ins between an employee and their manager are a reasonable, private venue for a person's own concerns and feedback about their manager.

5. *How does the workplace handle uncertainty, crises, and departures?*
   - Crisis communication: book's three-move pattern (be honest as far as possible, restate the plan, keep moving) is a reasonable template, with the model script above (book p.351).
   - Worth deciding explicitly what threshold makes something the whole team's business versus leadership-only; not every business risk changes what staff do on a given shift.
   - Departures: book doesn't address this directly for a service business; worth deciding what (if anything) the team is told when someone leaves and who decides, since silence tends to get filled with rumor.
   - Exit interviews and post-departure contact: the white paper mentions exit interviews and tracking "how people grow beyond the building" as a long-run culture measure (WP p.23), needs a decision on who conducts an exit interview, whether it's mandatory or optional, what's done with what's said, and whether/how the business stays in touch with a former employee afterward (a privacy/consent question for counsel).

6. *Should there be a day-to-day workplace chat tool?*
   - Book's practice is yes, with a caution that chat retention rules can lose things needing a permanent record (book p.336), the point worth keeping regardless of the specific tool: a chat tool should never be the only place a decision or important information lives.
   - Open question: whether Son wants a chat tool at all, given most staff aren't at a computer during a shift.

**Unblocking / disagreement-resolution process**, drawn from the book's workbook exercise (book pp.372-377): when two people or two departments disagree on a decision affecting both and can't resolve it themselves, the book's practice is a short written brief (the problem stated as an outcome, not a position; the options considered; the unresolved tradeoffs) that goes to whoever actually owns the decision, decided within a set window (the book uses five business days), with a rule against hearing only one side, if one party won't participate in writing the joint brief, that refusal is simply noted and the decision-maker proceeds anyway. Book explicitly rejects escalating up successive layers of management "until a decision is reached (or the stack overflows)" (book pp.373-374) as unworkable in most small organizations. Worth deciding: who plays "the person who owns cross-team decisions" in Son's structure, since the book assumes a manager above both parties.

**Transparency policy.** Book's frame, worth keeping directly since it argues for structure rather than a blanket answer: "Transparency doesn't mean sharing everything. It means that everyone has clarity about what will be shared, when, and with whom" (book p.131). This is itself a decision Son needs to make: what categories of information go to the whole team, on what timeline, versus staying with leadership only (financial figures, individual performance-review content, and financial/strategic planning are common categories other operators keep back, but this is Son's choice, not assumed here).
