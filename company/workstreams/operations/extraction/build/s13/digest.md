# S13 digest: the review system and the compensation architecture

## Part 1: What the old page holds that's worth keeping

### The review, generally

- Her ask: a talent strategy; rubrics tied to levels/ladders; agreement on assessing results; a program capturing feedback and promotion nominations via written/numeric assessment; a calibration process; a compensation philosophy connecting performance and promotion to pay outcomes (Book pp. 399-400). The written review: manager drafts after reading 3-5 peers' feedback and self-assessment, carries a promotion nomination and a designation on a 5- or 3-point scale, goes to calibration, is formalized in an HR tool, delivered in a conversation weighted toward the future (Book pp. 402-407). Calibration: a roll-up by level, facilitated by a division leader, checking outliers/distributions/bias, ending in a spreadsheet checked against comp budgets (Book pp. 407-413).
- WP p.19: "Performance systems develop people rather than judge them." Reviews happen every 3-6 months, focus on development/growth/path forward rather than a verdict; governing rule is no surprises (continuous communication means a formal review never contains new information); feedback runs both directions.
- WP p.19: advancement is not the review's, a person schedules a competency conversation with the appropriate lead when ready and is signed off; "No one's advancement waits on a manager's attention or judgment. The system is the advocate" (WP p.11).
- WP p.19: peer recognition "lands differently than management praise." WP p.11: "Every position has documented competency requirements visible to anyone."
- Book absorptions kept as function, not form: a formal moment on a rhythm that reads a period whole and looks forward; the person's own account read first; "if you've managed well, there are no surprises" (Book p.401) as a governing rule built as mechanism, not diligence; little time on the past, most on the path forward (Book p.406); the two-way conversation as "a great chance for me to receive open feedback" (Book p.406); "people need to know how they're going to be measured, that the measurement will be considered fairly, and how results will lead to recognition and reward" (Book pp. 399-400); "instill trust in your systems" and "educate yourself" as a requirement that any person or lead can explain the pay/review mechanics; her two questions "Am I being treated fairly? Am I being recognized for my performance?" (Book p.414); "perfect can be the enemy of good" (Book p.402).
- Refused as forms: any performance designation/scale; promotion nomination; manager-written review narrative; peer reviews as review input; pre-written self-assessment as a document owed ahead of time; the calibration committee/roll-up/outlier-check/expected-distribution/comp-budget-check; the talent review on a comprehensive rubric (nine-box); the job ladder (including one describing outcomes rather than skills); the review season and two-week delivery window; the review visible in an HR tool post-conversation; "pay for performance" as a stated philosophy; fixed/variable pay as salary/bonus; market data, salary bands, equity targets, equity refresh; a raise or bonus as the review's reward; the compensation conversation as a scheduled event sharing a raise or its absence; the disappointed-person script tying no raise to insufficient contribution; a performance tool generating a calibration packet.
- Risk/sequencing: every prior chapter of work built something the review would read (evidence lines, connections, path lines) or fenced something it may not read (readiness forms, coaching records, counts, recognition), every one deferred writing the review itself here. Likewise every chapter touching pay deferred designing the compensation architecture to here. Two genuine gaps existed before this session: the review had an interval/focus/rule but no holder, placement, length, form, record, or definition of what it reads/writes; the compensation architecture had a settled principle and form but no definition of what the pool is a share of, how points are set, what a day is, how the daily calculation works or is shown, what base pay is, how a salaried seat fits a day-weighted system, or what any named paid act (teaching, mentoring, training, translation) is actually paid.
- Option (tested against the premise that "almost none of her compensation machinery transfers, but the underlying principle does, pay must be legible, defensible, and connected to a review people trust"): the premise holds for two of three principles (legible, defensible) but not the third. Reasoning: at Sŏn pay is deliberately disconnected from the review, the review produces development-and-path-forward language, never a verdict, and no feedback act touches pay; the trust a person needs in what pays them is instead built by giving them a system with no judgment in it (seat, horizon, markers, days), each on a record they can read themselves.
- Finding: the task material that generated this chapter's brief carried a specific percentage figure and named a data source outside the allowed sources; the team did not use the figure or reason from it, since financials are not a source for this program and the white paper itself states the percentage is still being finalized (WP p.20, p.22). Stated once as a finding about the source material, not as a position.

### The review's placement: interval, calendar, block, holder, language

- The record gives only a range, "every three to six months" (WP p.19), with no owner.
- Option: pick six months. Reasoning offered for it: a monthly check-in already carries the running conversation, so a review at three months would just be a longer-form repeat of the fourth check-in; at six months the review reads five check-in lines, roughly two capture cycles, at least one unlock window on most seats, and a fluency window's currency, enough new material for the four dimensions to say something the last check-in didn't. Second, the labor-arithmetic argument: at six months the review adds one scheduled block per person twice a year on the same holder's windows, versus four times a year at three months, on top of check-ins and placed check-ins the holder already carries. Third, "every three to six months" keeps its meaning as a formal moment only if the review doesn't run as often as the check-in.
- Option: the interval could shorten toward three months (never below) on a single read, reviews that keep surfacing something new the check-ins hadn't already carried, treated as a signal that the check-in, not the review interval, is failing.
- Option: the review runs on the person's own clock (first review six months after first paid hour, every review six months after the last) rather than a company-wide review season. Reasoning: a season concentrates the workload into one window for whichever role holds every review, turns the review into a house event rather than a person's, and the only reason her season exists (a company-wide calibration event) is itself refused as a form here.
- Sequencing: the review's placement must not collide with two other scheduled moments, the one-time career conversation held roughly two months after the plan closes, and the ninety-day plan's own check-in cadence.
- Option: the review is a paid, scheduled block (thirty minutes to start, adjustable by the same holder discretion as a check-in) placed in a pre-service window with slack or a closed hour, never mid-service or at close, never off the clock; movable to cover a short-staffed window but never cancelled. Nothing is owed off the clock, so a self-review written at home is refused; the person's half is written during the block itself.
- Option: the review is held by whoever holds the person's check-in (not a separate reviewer), because the review opens on the check-in's own lines and a different reader would be reading a relationship record they weren't part of; never with a third person present (interpreting excepted); in the person's own chosen language.
- Open/unresolved: who holds a lead's own review is unresolved pending a separate decision about who holds a lead's check-in; until then, founders hold leads' reviews by default.
- Chef-partner-dependent: the kitchen's review holder, block placement against the prep window, and whether the review changes the kitchen's own rhythm are choices reserved to whoever leads the kitchen; the team's reasoning is that the interval, dimensions, no-surprises mechanism, and pay separation could hold in the kitchen unchanged regardless of who decides those specifics.

### What the review opens on

- The review should open on the person's full page since the last review (or since the plan's close, for a first review) and nothing else: every check-in line since the last review; the plan's close state at the first review only; the path line and chosen track from the career conversation; the progression state (horizon held, next unlock's date, unlocks taken, modules completed/authored); the fluency/connections record with dates; the discretionary-range-used count and any escalations; the person's own written captures; whether a "non-negotiable line" exists as a bare fact; the state of any infrastructure item the person raised; and the status of any channel items their lines generated.
- Explicitly excluded: a readiness form's rows or a "not yet"; any coaching record (none exists by design); any count of placed/extended/moved check-ins (load readings, never a read of the person); any failure-type count; a recognition given/received or its decline; a diagnostic process's evidence lines; a hiring scorecard; interview evidence; a calibration record; a peer's or mentor's account; a designation's count; a work-style placement; a lead's note written anywhere; the anonymous pulse; any lagging or pay figure.
- Rationale: the list is meant to be closed by design, the assembling system pulls from named records only, with no free-text field, so no fifth thing can quietly become a review input later.
- Open finding: the white paper separately names three things hiring assesses (WP p.18) and two things reviews focus on (WP p.19) without saying these are the same list; a proposed mapping (cultural alignment to conduct, growth potential to self-directed mastery, path forward to the re-read career track) answers it as a mechanism, leaving the wording itself for founders to reconcile.

### The four dimensions, read as states not marks

- The four review dimensions (competency for the seat; self-directed mastery; conduct under the non-negotiables and inside a discretionary range; what the building supplies) were fixed earlier as the same ones used at hiring, with the rule that a review adds nothing not already read at hiring.
- Option for how each is read, without a rating scale: competency as a statement of the horizon held and the next unlock's date, no "does not meet," since a person lacking a held horizon would already be a different process, not a review; self-directed mastery as a dated list from the progression record rather than a lead's impression; conduct as two facts, the discretionary-range-used count's own states (used, exceeded, unused, escalated) and whether a non-negotiable-line entry exists, with a breach never discussed at a review; what the building supplies scored on the building, item by item (supplied / not supplied / not yet), feeding a systemic infrastructure record rather than the person's page.
- Risk noted: an under-used discretionary range should be read first as a question about the workplace, not a fact about the person.
- Rationale for refusing a rating: WP p.19's "development, growth, and the path forward rather than a verdict"; a rating is a verdict regardless of label; and since a separate assessor, not the lead, scores competency, the one dimension a rating could honestly grade is one a lead isn't allowed to grade anyway.

### "No surprises" as a mechanism, and the candor line

- WP p.19's governing rule ("a formal review never contains new information") and Book p.401's manager-side version ("If you've managed well, there are no surprises") are both present in the record; the design question is how to make that true structurally rather than aspirationally.
- Option: build it in three parts. First, the review's content is entirely pre-assembled from readable records before the block starts, and the holder adds nothing of their own. Second, an observation not on any readable record is never raised at the review itself; instead the holder asks for a regular check-in afterward and raises it there, once, after the person's own account. Third, the review ends with a read-back and one direct question: was anything said that wasn't already on a record you could read. The answer becomes a permanent mark.
- Option: treat a review marked "something new" as a signal about the holder's own candor/diligence at check-ins, tracked as a count per holder per period, never as a read of the person being reviewed, and never a reason on its own to shorten the review interval.
- Mechanical safeguard: every entry must cite the dated line or record it's drawn from; an uncited item cannot be entered.
- Also carried by "if something is excellent, they know before it too" (WP p.19): a strength not yet named at a check-in is named at the next one, not saved for the review; at the review a holder may read aloud what a record already shows, but may not add a new judgment.

### What the review writes

- Option: the review writes one record in two halves, a stack-assembled half (the four dimensions as dated states, confirmed rather than authored at the review) and a person's own half in their own words (what they hold from the period, what they want next as an updated path/track, what the building needs to supply for that, an optional read of the holder's own performance and the workplace generally, and the "nothing new" mark). The holder never writes a summary, assessment, strength/development note, rating, designation, nomination, or recommendation in their own voice; if the person doesn't want to write their own half, the holder may write it in the person's words and read it back to confirm, matching the same rule used at check-ins.
- Rationale for refusing any rating or designation outright, beyond the "not a verdict" framing above: the record states "there is no hierarchy of importance" (WP p.20), and any designation functions as a rank regardless of label; the reason her designations exist at all (to differentiate rewards by performance, Book p.405) doesn't apply here because no review output reaches pay; her further claim that ratings over time surface top talent is instead carried by the advancement/progression record itself (who's furthest along on documented, visible competencies) rather than by any lead's mark.
- Option for the "you really have talent... as part of a formal process" moment (Book p.384): treat it as the formal reading of the record itself, specific, dated, cited acts (connections earned, unlocks taken, modules authored, a moment a capture shows a service problem being absorbed without anyone noticing) read aloud in specific, concrete language, with the holder describing what the person did rather than pronouncing a verdict on what the person is.
- Note on record boundaries: the review record, the one-line-per-month check-in record, and the ninety-day plan record are meant to stay in three distinct forms and never blur, the check-in never writes a state, the plan never holds a running line, the review never writes a hypothesis, milestone, or plan state.

### Her self-assessment, peer reviews, manager review, calibration, and the upward half

- Option: absorb her self-assessment as the person's own half of the review, written during the paid block itself rather than as homework beforehand, with her three prompts translated: "top contributions" becomes what the person held against their own captures/connections; "one capability you excel at" is folded into the check-in's existing "what's working" question; "one development area, with thoughts on how your manager could support you" becomes "what you want next" plus "what the building must supply."
- Option: refuse peer reviews as a review input entirely. Reasoning: a peer's account of a peer is an informal-network risk; positive peer feedback already has its own full mechanism in the in-the-moment recognition system, itself never a review input; a peer's written capture is read only if the reviewed person chose to write it into their own captures.
- Option: refuse the manager-written narrative and the promotion nomination. Reasoning: a narrative in the holder's own voice is refused the same way free text about a person is refused elsewhere; nominations are refused because advancement runs through a separate three-seat structure (system schedules, an independent assessor scores, the domain lead records without overriding) the review doesn't participate in.
- Option: refuse calibration as a committee, level roll-up, outlier check, expected distribution, or compensation-budget check. Reasoning: nothing to calibrate, no designations to compare, no levels to roll up by, facts assembled identically for everyone, a single review-holder role on the floor, and no review output reaches pay. What calibration is meant to accomplish (fairness across people in the same seat; catching a lenient or harsh holder; a bias check) is instead carried by: the single assembled form; an independent assessor pool; a periodic calibration read comparing the hiring bar and readiness bar; the candor-line count; and a separate anonymized pay-parity read.
- Option: absorb WP p.19's "team members weigh in on management effectiveness, operational improvements, and culture" as the review's upward half, an extended "what's in the way" question covering the holder's own performance, the workplace's mechanisms as improvement items, and the building-supplied dimension, asked as "what would you change," repeated back without being explained away, written into the record only if the person wants it.
- Open/unresolved: a concern about the review holder themselves has no destination yet, pending the unresolved question of who holds a lead's own check-in and review.

### What the review may never become

- Restated as a list of hard refusals, each with its own reasoning already given above: a verdict; a rating, designation, or rank; a pay input by any route; a promotion nomination or advancement input; a file used to justify managing someone out (her suggestion that "compensation conversations can be a helpful tool" in that situation, Book p.418, is explicitly refused, there is no compensation conversation to use as a lever); a room with a third person present; a coaching record, hypothesis log, or written recap; a probation label, plan state, or milestone; a promise of pay, a seat, a date, or a designation; an input to calibration or a talent-review process.

### The review in specific contexts

- A first review (about two months after the one-time career conversation, roughly three months after the ninety-day plan closes): reads the plan's close state and the baseline captured then, the career-conversation path/track, and everything logged since the plan closed; the readiness/onboarding form itself is not reopened.
- A person holding two service strands or two time periods: connections and their currency are read as plain facts; fluency itself is never scored, only whether its window is currently active; a lapsed fluency window is communicated to the person by the record itself, not announced by the holder at the review.
- A person who regularly holds a special designation (e.g., a room, door, or sequence designation): the review reads nothing about designations held, no count, no credit beyond whatever the person chooses to write about it themselves; a designation held every single service is treated as a staffing/scheduling question, not evidence about the holder.
- Load on whichever role holds every floor review: read as a workload count (reviews held, moved, extended per period) alongside check-in load counts, addressed by staffing/scheduling rather than by shortening, delegating, or skipping reviews.
- A lead's own review: proposed to use the same four dimensions, form, and refusals as anyone's, scored against the lead's own equivalent of a seat sheet, with the domain's own upward feedback rolled up by mechanism rather than by name; who actually holds a lead's review is left to a separate, later decision, with founders holding it by default until then.
- Kitchen: regardless of who ultimately leads the kitchen, the team's expectation is that every kitchen seat is reviewed on the same interval, in a paid block, on the same four dimensions read the same way, with no rating and no pay connection; who specifically holds it and where it sits in the kitchen's own schedule is reserved to kitchen leadership.

### The compensation architecture: what's fixed and what needed designing

- Fixed already and not re-argued here: compensation is built into menu price rather than surfaced as a tip or service charge and is never presented as one (WP p.20, p.22); it's distributed to the whole team including the kitchen; it runs on a point system weighted by days worked; it's calculated daily rather than quarterly; it's paid on "a reasonable rhythm"; benefits are planned from the start rather than deferred; cross-strand fluency is recognized in pay as meaningful; teaching is paid; the percentage itself is explicitly left unfinalized in the white paper (WP p.11, p.19, p.20, p.22).
- Left undesigned and addressed here: what the shared pool is actually a share of; exactly who is included in it, seat by seat; how an individual's points are set and what must never be allowed to set them; what counts as "a day"; how the daily calculation is performed and shown to people; who owns the payment rhythm; how base pay and the shared pool relate to each other; how a salaried role fits into a day-weighted system; and who owns every numeric parameter.
- Structural point: the architecture has two layers, but the record only describes one of them. Base pay (an hourly wage for most seats, or a salary for a small number of seats) is assumed throughout the white paper (a "paid practical," "the training plan with pay dates," anything described as paid "as shift") but never actually named or defined; the shared daily pool is the second layer, and it's the one the white paper actually describes.

### The pool mechanism

- The pool is a defined percentage (still unset) applied to a defined revenue base, computed once per closed day.
- Percentage: an explicitly open, unset parameter, to be set later against the business's financial model; no value or range is stated here, consistent with financials being outside this program's scope.
- Revenue base: which categories count toward the base is an open list; the one substantive constraint proposed is that the base must not shrink when a server uses a discretionary comp/generosity allowance, a comped item still counts at full menu price and recovery spend doesn't reduce the day's pool, so the team isn't effectively paying for its own generosity. Whether other categories (morning hours, private events, retail, tastings) count is left open.
- One pool per day, not one per time-of-day period: proposed as a single shared pool per calendar day across every service period, rather than separate pools per period. Reasoning: separate pools would make periods compete for the same people's labor and pay a slower period's team only from that period's own revenue, working against a single restaurant across times of day; it also makes a fluency marker for someone who works two periods in one day meaningful, under "no hierarchy of importance" (WP p.20). A per-period pool is stated as the alternative, not read as required by the record.
- Who's included: every working seat with a shift that day, across every domain and strand; someone in training is included from their first paid hour; a candidate on a still-unpaid trial shift is not (a separate legal/employment question); contracted outside labor is outside the pool by not being employed team; whether the founders (and a chef-partner, depending on kitchen structure) hold any points is a separate, still-open founder-level question.

### What sets a person's points, and what must never set them

- A person's daily points come from exactly four sources, each a record the person can read: (1) the pay-weight published for their currently-held seat and horizon, moving upward on the date a completed skill "unlock" is recorded; (2) a small set of on/off markers (cross-strand fluency, cross-period fluency, teaching) while currently active; (3) days actually worked; (4) for salaried seats, a day-weighted rule counting every open business day in the pay period regardless of shifts worked.
- Explicit list of things that must never move a person's points: any review, check-in, rating, designation, recognition, any lead's subjective judgment, any lagging business indicator beyond its role in setting the pool's base, a customer rating, sales attribution, hours worked (as distinct from days), which period was worked, tenure or history, or negotiation. No one, including the founders, moves points by hand; every point is a computed output of records.
- Reasoning: WP p.11's "No one's advancement waits on a manager's attention or judgment" is read to extend to pay, since pay progression follows the advancement mechanism; "one rule for everyone by construction" is the core defense against pay disputes.

### What counts as a day; the daily calculation; the pay page; the payment rhythm

- A day is proposed as any calendar day on which the person worked a scheduled shift of any length in any period, counted once regardless of how many periods worked (why a separate fluency marker exists for two periods in a day, rather than counting two days). Any paid block (check-in, review, training, paired service, module hour, orientation) counts as a worked shift, consistent with "no cost shifting to employees" (WP p.19). A day scheduled but not worked doesn't count. An unexpectedly closed day is left as an open wage question for whoever owns wage-law compliance.
- Daily calculation: at close, a system computes the day's pool from the base and percentage, sums every working person's points, and gives each a share proportional to their own points over the day's total; written to each person's pay page by the next morning in their language, accruing to the next pay date; no lead computes anyone's pay or sees anyone else's page.
- Legibility trade-off, not resolved: checking one's own share by arithmetic needs both the day's total points (not itself sensitive) and the day's actual pool amount, but the pool amount plus the known percentage lets anyone back-calculate daily revenue, a sensitive figure kept private elsewhere. Two options stated: show the full pool amount (verifiable, but revenue-derivable) or withhold the pool total while showing enough to check the share against total points and the rule (confidential, but the one number that decides the payout stays invisible).
- Payment rhythm: WP p.20 says "calculated daily... paid on a reasonable rhythm" without a cadence. Reasoning for paying the pool share on the same pay date as base pay, never later: a share arriving weeks after the nights that earned it doesn't feel like "the upside of a full night"; hourly workers in this market are typically paid weekly or biweekly, so a slower cadence risks undermining "reasonable" from the first pay date. Actual cycle length is bounded by wage/pay-date law and the payroll platform.
- Base pay vs. the pool: base pay is proposed as one rule per seat, same wage for everyone holding it, published like the point-weight, never something an offer states as contingent on the pool. Every wage/hour/overtime/tip-law/pay-transparency question the two-layer structure raises (including whether the share counts legally as a tip) belongs to counsel.
- Benefits: WP p.20 names a specific HR/payroll platform and "a fast path to full benefits as the business supports them"; the platform's actual features are unconfirmed, not asserted. Proposed: which benefits exist at hire is a plain fact on the transparency document; "fast path to full benefits" is a conditional gate rather than a promised date, and no surface states a benefit date the gate hasn't reached.

### The parameters register (every number the architecture needs, unset here)

A proposed list of every numeric/parameter decision the architecture needs, each set later by the founders (generally with the financial-model owner, and counsel where wage law applies), with no value stated anywhere in this program: the pool percentage (vs. the financial model); which revenue classes count in the base and the comp-counts-at-menu-price rule; the point-weight for every seat at every horizon (kitchen seats reserved to kitchen leadership); the value of each marker (cross-strand, cross-period, teaching); salaried seats' actual salary figures and their day-weighting rule; hourly wages for non-salaried seats (vs. wage law and the market); the actual payment cadence (vs. pay-date law and the payroll platform); the flat payment for an approved training module or translated page; the discretionary comp/generosity range's value and its escalation threshold; the benefits gate's specific conditions; and the pay rule for an unexpectedly closed day.

### Salaried seats, a lead's entry point, fluency markers, unlocks, and designations

- Structural test proposed for which seats are salaried: a seat that owns a domain or program across every open time period rather than a set of scheduled services; by that test a small number of roles (operations, floor leadership, kitchen leadership, beverage leadership) qualify, with kitchen-specific status reserved to kitchen leadership.
- Option: a salaried seat holds a salary plus pool points for every day the business is open in the pay period, by rule rather than counted shifts, since that seat's work spans every open day the domain runs; no conflict arises because no seat holder can move anyone's points by hand. Alternative stated but not favored: salary entirely outside the pool, simpler and avoids any appearance of a leader drawing from the same pool as people whose reviews they hold, but makes a full night worth nothing extra to the seats that hold it.
- A lead's own entry point: the seat's own published salary and point-weight at its entry horizon, deliberately named with no added rank; future pay moves only the same way anyone's does.
- Fluency markers: cross-strand fluency (two service roles in one domain, each demonstrated through a separate real-service evaluation) priced as an on/off marker while connections stay current, never a rank. Cross-period fluency (roles across two times of day) proposed recognized the same way, since a day counts once regardless of periods worked, so the second register would otherwise earn nothing extra. Alternative (no pay recognition, fluency visible only in succession ordering) stated, not favored.
- Unlocks vs. pay: each skill "unlock" moves a person's point-weight to the next horizon on the date the domain lead records it (never the lead's own initiative, only what an independent assessor already scored); a competency for a seat not currently held adds a connection and, if it meets the fluency definition, a marker, but moves no pay until the seat is actually held.
- Designations (holding the room, the door, a service sequence, interpreting) are proposed to never carry pay, marker, or day-credit; regular designation load is addressed by rotation or by adjusting the underlying seat's staffing, not a designation-based pay line, paying a designation directly would turn it into a reward and recreate a permanent title by another name.

### The routed pay lines: mentoring, authorship, completion, training, translation/interpreting, the discretionary range

- Mentoring: shift pay plus a teaching-type marker on the mentor's points for every day a mentored plan remains open, never tied to the mentee's outcome (avoids making the mentor a judge) and never stacked (one open plan per mentor).
- Module authorship (WP p.19: "any qualified team member can create training modules and get paid for teaching everyone else"): a flat payment per module approved against a quality standard, not per module written, paid again per approved revision, as base pay rather than a point. Whether an author also earns per completion by others: proposed no, since it would incentivize pushing modules and rank authors by reach.
- Completion (WP p.19: "the more modules completed, the more earned"): proposed as already satisfied by paid module time plus the resulting unlock that moves pay weight, without a separate per-module payment, since that would reward finishing rather than actual readiness and recreate a volume incentive. Alternative (a flat per-completion payment) stated for founders to weigh.
- Training pay: base pay for every scheduled training hour from the first paid hour, plus the seat's entry-horizon weight in the pool from that same hour, with no reduced "training weight," since a lower weight would function as an unstated probation label. Alternative (an actual reduced weight) stated, not favored. An unpaid trial shift stays a separate, counsel-defined employment question outside the pool.
- Translation/interpreting: translation paid like module authorship, a flat per-approved-version payment requiring both the document's owner and a second fluent reviewer, as base pay, never a point; a software-assisted draft is paid for the human review work, with a disclosure note. Live interpreting is an on-clock designation carrying only ordinary shift pay.
- Discretionary comp/generosity range: two previously unset dollar values (how much can be comped on judgment, and the escalation threshold) proposed to be set by whoever owns the financial model, written as actual numbers on the relevant reference card rather than left as "use your judgment," versioned, and reviewed periodically against how often the range was used or exceeded.

### Review and pay: kept fully separate

- Core position: no review output, not the dimension-states, the person's own half, the "nothing new" mark, the upward half, or anything said in the room, is ever allowed to reach pay; no check-in line reaches pay either; no feedback act of any kind is a pay input.
- Reasoning, several independent threads: (1) the record's own description of the review as "development, growth, and the path forward rather than a verdict" (WP p.19) is incompatible with also deciding pay, since a person hears the number before anything else in the room; (2) the check-in and coaching system already refuse to touch pay, so a pay-connected review becomes the one place work and wage sit in the same conversation; (3) the person's own page refuses ratings elsewhere, so there's nothing on it a pay mechanism could read; (4) no-surprises can't coexist with a pay-bearing review, since a raise (or its absence) is inherently news; (5) WP p.11's "no one's advancement waits on a manager's attention or judgment" extends downstream to pay, so a lead who could move pay would recreate the manager-discretion problem advancement is designed to remove; (6) legibility, a person can only compute their own pay from the seat, horizon, markers, and days because nothing in it is opinion, and a review input would break that.
- What's proposed to carry her "pay for performance" instead: the unlock system (pay for an assessed competency rather than an impression); the fluency markers (pay for demonstrated, current versatility); and the pool's day-weighting (pay for nights actually worked, kitchen included). Deliberately not carried: any differential reward for doing the same seat better than someone else in it.
- Comparisons ("so-and-so makes more than me for the same job," Book p.417): proposed answer is that pay is fully arithmetic and checkable, the same seat, horizon, markers, and days always produce the same share, any difference traces to one of those four things on the person's own record; a person still can't see someone else's page, but can see the rule. Whether every seat's weight should be published house-wide, not only to a candidate for that seat, is an open, favorable-leaning question.
- Compensation conversation: since there's no discrete pay "event," her chapter is proposed to be replaced by three things: a one-time explanation at the offer stage; a page a person can check daily; and a defined destination per category of pay question (arithmetic to the pay page and a correction process with response-time commitments; the rule itself to a general improvement channel; what's possible next to the regular check-in or career conversation; another person's pay has no destination).
- What a person delivering pay information may or may not say: may explain the mechanism, state what the published sheet says a seat's wage/weight is, confirm a recorded unlock's effective date, and say "I don't know, I'll find out" (Book p.111). May not: state a figure not on that person's own page or the seat sheet, discuss anyone else's pay, promise or predict a future amount, link any review/check-in/designation/recognition to pay, or imply pay could have been higher "if" beyond what the system supports.
- "Managing disappointment" is answered the same three ways rather than a scripted conversation: an error goes through the correction path; a path-forward question goes through the check-in or career conversation from the sheet, never a spoken figure; a complaint about the rule goes to the improvement channel.

### Legal and counsel questions surfaced

- Whether embedding compensation into menu price (as a substitute for tips) creates Texas/federal wage-law exposure, FLSA classification, whether a tip credit applies, how overtime's regular rate is calculated when pay is revenue-linked, and disclosure requirements, is explicitly unresolved and flagged as counsel's work, not something this program can treat as settled by designing around it.
- Whether the daily-distributed share is legally a wage, a tip, a bonus, or something else under Texas and federal law (relevant to final pay and to any paid-leave-as-if-worked policy) is an open question for counsel.
- Whether excluding the founding partners (and, depending on kitchen leadership, a chef-partner while working services) from the shared pool has any legal consequence for the pool's wage character, or for calling the distribution "the whole team," is an open question for counsel.
- Founder-specific open questions: whether the founders count as employees for any purpose; whether the standard flag/escalation process can run with a founder as its subject; what happens to records, authorities, and accounts if a founder departs, becomes incapacitated, or dies; whether internal records carry retention, discovery, disclosure, or fiduciary consequences; and whether founders' outside commitments raise any duty under the partnership agreement or the law. None of these has an answer here; all are logged as counsel questions.
- A parity/discrimination-adjacent question, designed to stay non-invasive: since pay reduces to three rates (unlock rate, marker rate, days scheduled), a proposed "parity read" would apply existing anonymized tags to those rates by seat class at a periodic review point, with no names, figures, inferences, or individual consequence, never a performance input. It can't run ("cannot read yet") until enough tagged data and time exist. Whether tags should include self-identified demographic data is an open question for founders with counsel.
- A related staffing question: handling an hours reduction or closed service period without it functioning as a disguised termination, a published, non-discretionary rule for who loses shifts, advance disclosure of seasonal patterns, and a genuine departure triggered only when a seat is fully eliminated with no alternate available within a defined window. The specific counts involved are left as founder-and-counsel-set parameters, never stated as figures.
