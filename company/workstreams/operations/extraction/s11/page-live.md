## 1\. Her ask against the record, and how much is already decided

### 1.1 What she asks

Her three sections ask a manager to do three things. Run a team whose members are not in the same place, by documenting more, moving every work conversation into a shared channel, leveling the playing field between the person in the room and the person on the call, and meeting in person on a cadence. Build a diverse team and run it inclusively, by assessing the team's portfolio of backgrounds, hiring against a diverse pipeline, running equitable processes for projects and promotions with calibration and pay parity checks, and paying attention to airtime. And communicate: "Communicate, communicate, and by the way, communicate" (Book p.360), share the same information with everyone, pass down what leadership decided with the manager's framing, keep a snippets document, and end each day thinking about what to tell people. The appendix restates four exercises the workbook already carries.

Every piece assumes a co-present, desk-based team with a manager who communicates by meeting, email, and chat, whose members read documents between meetings, and whose company has a leadership above the manager to pass things down from. None of that is Sŏn.

### 1.2 What the record already holds

The record's communication architecture is more decided than her chapter's, because the record built its structure to fix communication. Bus failure is named as one of four failure modes: "Information cannot move between domains because the communication infrastructure is verbal handoffs, tribal knowledge, and informal relationships between individuals. When the relationship breaks, the information stops. This is not a culture problem. It is a systems problem" (WP p.13). Sequence corruption is its symptom: "information is not flowing to where it is needed before it is needed. The shift brief is not happening, or the system is not surfacing preferences, or post-shift documentation is not captured" (WP p.14). The fix, in the record's words, is "system maintenance, not people management" (WP p.14). The record commits the surfaces: the pre-shift briefing as equal in importance to the signature dish (WP p.20); the shift close as the capture the next server inherits (WP p.13); the peer recognition platform "in the moment" (WP p.19); "a dedicated channel that syncs to the execution system" with the loop closed (WP p.22); feedback running both directions (WP p.19); the transparency sheet's "communication expectations" (WP p.18); the way the phone is answered (WP p.20) and "the phone in software" (WP p.22); crisis communication as "transparency first" (WP p.31); and the team home the record lists as an open item, the web rendered as an artifact (WP p.15).

Canon governs every word: the language constant applies "in any internal communication" (Canon 1.0, section 01; Deck 3.0, p.5), the forbidden lexicon applies "in all contexts, no exceptions" (Canon 1.0, section 09), the master register is "present without performing" (Canon 1.0, section 01; Deck 3.0, p.28), Korean terminology "stands without explanation in all brand contexts from day one" (Canon 1.0, section 04; Deck 3.0, p.45), and "A living document changes by versioned decision, never by drift" (Deck 3.0, p.94).

Sessions 3 to 10 built on that. Session 3 set the trigger as non-co-presence by hour, adopted her four tests with accessibility read across hour, strand, and language, set three channels (spoken at the brief of every open period, written on the team home, carried by the lead or mentor), set the writing culture as structured capture at the edge and short prose at the center, adopted the crisis rule, and specified the team home (S3 section 11). Session 4 put the brief and the close on fixed spines, placed the check-in on the clock, set the feedback loop-closure window and the pulse, and bound every cadence artifact to the three channels (S4 P10, P12, P15, P29, P30). Session 7 placed the bought stack and held one tool against canon (S7 P31, P32). Session 8 fixed how a structure change reaches the team, in four channels and never by announcement, and refused the Code Yellow for the live-risk line (S8 P42, P49). Session 9 narrowed what the brief says on a departure to coverage only and read the channels' order as a diagnostic (S9 P30). Session 10 fixed that every topic has a named destination and that the environment names no person in a room (S10 P14, P15, P20).

### 1.3 The gap

Two things are not held. First, those rules were written in five sessions and never read as one architecture. The brief carries recognitions (S4 P10) and a recognition names a person, while the environment names no person in a room (S10 P14); Session 3 wrote that a departure is "announced" with its tension map (S3 P11.6) and Session 8 wrote that nothing reaches the team by announcement (S8 P49); Session 4 named Google Workspace as the floor team's backbone and Session 7 placed the team home on it pending the OS surface, while Session 3 held where the team home lives as founder-gated (S3 P11.9). This page reads every rule as one system, and where two conflict it surfaces the conflict rather than picking silently (sections 5, 6, and 10).

Second, the record holds inclusion mechanics and no position on who the team is (S5 finding 15.6; `86akh7r2f`, `86akh7r8z`). Language choice "protects dignity" (WP p.17), every interview asks whether "the building is equipped to create the environment this person needs to succeed" (WP p.18), engagement is measured across psychological safety and values alignment (WP p.23), and canon's non-negotiables are conduct rules (Canon 1.0, section 01). Nothing in the record or either canon artifact states a goal, a read, or a position on the composition of the team, and each of the record's four sources narrows (S5 6.2). Her diversity section is the test of that silence. Section 15 develops a position to the ceiling the record allows and marks what the founders must close.

### 1.4 Where she is refused, and where absorbed

Refused as forms: the pass-down with the manager's framing; the manager as relay; the team meeting as the room where a struggling team is asked why it is behind; the team 360; the engagement survey for remote employees; the semi-regular check-in email between teams; the embedded representative and the working group; the leadership snippets document; the weekly leader update with anecdotes and photos; the offsite in every form (S10 P17); Hofstede's dimensions as a team exercise; the team-portfolio assessment of backgrounds; and the unblocking process as an escalation up two lines (S8 P50).

## 3\. Team-building complexities, each read against the three objects

The ledger's translation of "team" binds: the house for the collective, the domain for a manager's reports, the service roster for who is on tonight, and a domain, a strand, and a service period are not teams (S8 P6). Each of her complexities is read against those three, the web, and the mechanism map.

### 3.1 Distributed and remote teams: the house's version is the hour

Her three challenges are coordination, cohesion, and participation (Book p.332), and her Table 9 sorts them by what kind of remote team a manager has (Book pp. 336 to 337). Sŏn has no remote team in her sense. The house is one building. What it has is her third row, "a team split across two or three different offices" (Book p.336), with the offices replaced by hours: the person who ran the morning window is not in the building at late night, the roster dissolves at every close, and a decision made on one service is unknown on the next unless the operating system carries it (S3 P11.1). Table 9's verdict for that row, coordination, "making sure that teams are aware of decisions that impact them and that they feel involved in those decisions" (Book p.336), is the record's sequence corruption named from the other side. The mitigation is the three channels and the change-reach record (section 7).

Her mitigations, read one by one. "Put in place company norms and structures that foster asynchronous work, such as strong documentation" (Book p.333): the team home, the versioned entry, and the capture (S3 P11.5). "Balance the workload of distributed team members so that they have independent, 'local' work but are also connected to the rest of the company" (Book p.333): every service period is a period of one restaurant only when its dependent interfaces are non-empty (S4 section 8; S8 P7), so no period is an island by construction, and the interface read is where a period losing contact shows (S9 3.3). "Map your processes... to avoid team members waiting several hours for a colleague to wake up" (Book p.333): the register's ranges, set in advance so no one waits for a person, and the decision-rights rule that "escalate" is not a destination (S3 4.1). Her sidebar on time zones, an engineer waiting overnight for an owner's approval and learning that most systems need "a self-service interface" (Book p.334), is the record's own argument for the range: a server who has to wait for a lead's approval to resolve a table has the load-bearing model's delay (WP p.13).

Her "the minute an informal in-person conversation turns into a work discussion or a team decision, it needs to move to a format that allows all relevant parties to participate" (Book p.335) survives whole as a rule and changes its form: at Sŏn a decision has no room to be made in; it is a versioned entry, and a decision that exists only as something two people said on a service is not a decision until it is written (Deck 3.0, p.94). Her "hallway chats occur in Slack channels" (Book p.336) and "watch out for Slack retention rules... if it needs to be permanently documented, put it elsewhere" (Book p.336) is the distinction this page keeps between a channel and a record: a chat surface, if the house has one, is never the record of anything, and section 6 says which surface is.

The one remote team Sŏn does have is the pre-opening partnership and the third seat when it arrives: two founders in different domains, a build that runs on documents, and a chef partner who may join from elsewhere. Her advice for that case, "document communication norms in more detail than you think you need to" (Book p.338), is met by the partners' page as the decision log, the domain read in writing, and the working-with-me exchange before the chef signs (S4 P53; S7 P49; S1). Nothing here adds to the founder cadence.

**P3 (Recommended).** Sŏn has no remote or distributed team in her sense. Its version is the hour, and her Table 9 row for a team split across offices applies with the offices replaced by service periods: the primary challenge is coordination, the record's sequence corruption, and the mitigation is the three channels and the change-reach record. Her rule that a work conversation moves to a shared format is absorbed as the rule that nothing is a decision until it is a versioned entry. The pre-opening partnership is the house's one distributed team, and the founder cadence already carries it.

### 3.2 Cohesion, participation, and inclusive meeting practices

At Sŏn the playing field is leveled by construction, not by norm: the same one thing is spoken at every open period's brief in the same week (S4 P10); the same team home is read by every person in the languages they hold (S3 P11.8); the same readiness block is on the OS surface for a person who arrives late (S4 P10). Section 8 answers the language half and section 6 the surface half.

Her three inclusive practices. "Set structures and norms for inclusive meeting practices" (Book p.337) with video links, acoustics, active facilitation, and notes: Sŏn has no meeting during service, and the brief's inclusive practice is the fixed spine, the readiness block written where a person can read it, and the languages of tonight's roster (section 8.3). "Level the playing field" (Book p.338): stated above. "Make room for in-person time" (Book p.339): the house is in person every service, and Session 10 answered the rest, no offsite, the cohort's orientation as the one forming block, the gathering founder-gated (S10 P17, P22). Her "pay attention to airtime. If someone hasn't said anything in a team meeting, give them an opportunity" (Book p.358) has no meeting to run in. A person who has said nothing in the house for a quarter is read by the diagnostic as a check-in line pattern and never by a lead's impression (S9 P41).

**P4 (Recommended).** Cohesion and participation are produced by construction rather than by norm: the same one thing at every open period's brief, the same team home in every language the team speaks, the readiness block written for anyone who arrives late, and every person's voice given a destination that does not depend on a room. ### 3.3 Going global, and adding remote workers

**P5 (Recommended).** Going global is refused: the house maps no person on a cultural dimension, and its cultural difference is inside the positioning and the team's languages, carried by section 8. ### 3.4 Underperforming teams

Her first step, probing in 1:1s, is the check-in's "what is in the way," heard as a system question (S4 P15). Her second step is refused as a form. No room the roster is in carries a read of how the team is doing (S10 P14), and a goal read aloud to a roster as "why are we behind" is a read of the roster. Her four questions survive on a record: the leads' review reads the goal page's state and the evidence line, and "in or out of our control" is the evidence line's own question, which mechanism the fix touches (S9 P24). Her wrong-goal branch is Session 4's goal cycle: a goal is adjusted by versioned change with its reason and what it displaced (S4 P35), read back on the next scorecard (S9 P26), and her "Be transparent internally about the fact that you're resetting your goals and why" (Book p.346) is the change entry reaching the team by the four channels and the goal page's change record on the team home. **P6 (Recommended).** An underperforming team is a unit whose goal page reads not met, read by the diagnostic and never asked in a room. ## 4\. The communication architecture's premise

Five facts the architecture is built on, none of them her chapter's.

**Non-co-presence by hour.** The trigger is the hour, not headcount (S3 P11.1). Under any ratified set of periods the person who opened the day is not in the building when it closes, and the roster of one service does not meet the roster of the next. Every rule below is written so that no service period's team learns of a decision later, or differently, than another's.

**The roster dissolves at every close.** Her team persists between meetings; Sŏn's service roster exists from the brief to the close and is a different set tomorrow (S8 P6; S10 section 5). A spoken word at a brief reaches the people on that service and no one else. Nothing that must reach the house may exist only as something said.

**The house has languages.** The record lets a candidate "choose the interview language" and promises that someone "who speaks it" runs the interview (WP p.17); the seat sheet's field 14 names the languages the seat works in (S5 I2); the team home is read "in the languages the team speaks" (S3 P11.8). The list of those languages is a fact about the roster, and it is a constraint on every surface (section 8).

**No one sits at a desk or reads between services.** The writing culture is structured capture at the edge and short prose at the center (S3 P11.5). A server reads the team home on a phone in the pre-service window or off the clock by choice. Nothing on any surface may assume an hour at a laptop.

**Nothing is owed before the first paid hour, and nothing is owed off the clock.** "Information delivered before arrival is available, not owed" (S7 P15), the check-in is on the clock (S4 P15), and no gathering is attended unpaid (S10 row 121). Applied here: no message on a person's phone off shift requires an answer, no feedback prompt is due, no recognition is expected, no schedule change is effective by a message the person has not been on shift to read. Every surface is read on paid time, and what a surface may push to a personal device off shift is the schedule the person already agreed to and nothing that asks anything of them (section 6.10).

To these the Realist adds the floor condition: the architecture's floor half must survive a Friday at tempo run by the crew that is actually there, a server at the end of a double, and a thirty-second brief. The readiness block is "a checklist the rush cannot delete" (S4 P10). Every rule that reaches the floor is written in that light: it either sits in the pre-service window or the close, or it is on a record a person reads when they choose.

**P9 (Recommended).** The architecture's premise is non-co-presence by hour, a roster that dissolves at every close, a house that works in several languages, a team that does not read between services, and nothing owed before the first paid hour or off the clock. Every surface and every rule below is tested against those five and against a compressed Friday brief.

### 5.1 The hard rows, argued

**Recognition names a person; the environment names no person in a room.** The two rules meet at item 4 of the brief. The reconciliation is in what a recognition is and where it lives. Its destination is a record, the platform, which is the rule's requirement (S10 P14). What the brief does is read a record aloud, as it reads the readiness block; the room's owner is a reader and not a speaker, "not editorialized" (S4 P10). A recognition is a peer's own words about an act in a service, never the house's read of a person. The ledger's prohibition is on "a read or a correction of a person," and this page states the boundary as a rule the recognition platform enforces: a recognition names what a person did, in one service, and never what a person is; it carries no rating, no comparison to anyone, and no rank; and the room's owner declines to read one that is a correction dressed as praise, a read of a person's state, or a comparison. A recognition that fails those is not deleted; it is left on the platform unread and the peer is told why by the room's owner in person, off the floor. Read that way, item 4 is the one place the roster hears a person named, and it hears the person's peers, in their words, about an act. That is the record's reason for the platform: it "lands differently than management praise because it comes from the people who understand the work" (WP p.19). The finding underneath, that reading a peer's words in a lead's voice at a brief is itself a change of speaker, is section 20's.

**The structure change and the departure both use the brief's change line, and Session 3 called the departure an announcement.** The three positions (S3 P11.6, S7 P47, S8 P49) are consistent once Session 3's tension map is read as coverage, which Session 9 already did (S9 P30). This page narrows Session 3's wording once more and surfaces it: "announced" in S3 P11.6 and "in person before in writing" are read as the brief speaking coverage under its standing change line, which is spoken before the team home shows the inventory change only because the brief runs before the next roster reads. Nothing is said about the person, and the founders ratify the narrowing beside S3 P11.6 rather than this page overwriting it.

**The one thing carries no framing.** Her pass-down comes with the manager's frame, "I think the right way to think about this is..." (Book p.361). At Sŏn the one thing is one sentence, the same sentence at every brief that week, spoken by the room's owner (S4 P10). The reason travels with it as the evidence line's own words, verbatim, because the evidence line is the reason field of every change (S9 P24). A lead who adds a frame has put a person between the record and the roster, which is the relay the ledger's departure from her hierarchy refuses. The one exception her example does preserve is the record's: a founder speaking the why block in person (S7 P17), which is the founders' voice and not a lead's framing of it.

**The phone is a customer surface until it produces a message for the room.** Slang AI handles phones (the session task). What a customer says on the phone is customer-facing and is canon's; it enters this architecture only where it produces internal information. That information has three destinations and only three: the book on the identity spine, for a reservation, a change, a note for a table, or an allergy; the brief's readiness block, which reads the book for tonight (S4 P10); and the capture, for a complaint, which is an incident the system should know. The rule is the record's own: "the system is not surfacing preferences" is sequence corruption (WP p.14), so a phone-originated message that lives in whoever answered the phone and not on the book is a bus failure by definition. No phone message reaches the room by a person relaying it by voice alone; if a person takes it, they write it to the book before they say it. Section 6.9 holds what answers the phone against canon.

**Schedule changes and off-clock messages.** The stack posts the schedule and places the check-ins (S4 P15). A change to a shift reaches a person on a personal device off the clock because the record's own reason for the stack is that the person does not have to hold the schedule in their head. That is the one thing a surface may push to a person off shift, and it asks nothing of them: a change is effective for a shift only after the person has confirmed it where the surface carries a confirmation, or has been on the clock to read it. Everything else, a recognition prompt, a feedback prompt, a module, a message from a lead, waits for paid time or the person's own choice to look.

**P10 (Recommended).** The inventory as tabled is the house's one account of every speech act: each with an originator, a surface, a language rule, an hour, an audience, a record, a read-back where one exists, and refusals. Every speech act's destination is a record; the brief and the slot read records aloud and originate nothing but the designations' lines. The one thing carries no lead's framing; the reason travels as the evidence line's own words.

**P11 (Recommended).** A recognition is reconciled with the rule that the environment names no person in a room by what it is and where it lives: a peer's own words about an act in one service, on a record, read at the brief verbatim by the room's owner as reader; never a read of a person, a rating, a comparison, a rank, a correction, or a lead's praise. One that fails those is left unread and the peer told why in person. The narrowing of S3 P11.6's "announced" to coverage under the change line is surfaced for ratification beside it, not overwritten.

## 7\. Three times, three channels, made operational

### 7.1 The rule, and its proof

Session 3: important information travels spoken at the brief of every open period, written on the team home, and carried by the domain lead or mentor, and "A change that reaches one period's brief and not another's has not been communicated" (S3 P11.4). Session 8 fixed a structure change's four channels in order: the affected person's check-in, the sheet's version, the brief, the team home (S8 P49). Every change to a range, trigger, goal, designation, mechanism, seat, line, or period carries a change-reach record (I5), written by the stack from records that already exist and by no one's hand: the entry's version date; the date of each affected person's check-in at which it was read, from the person page; the sheet's version date where a seat changed; the date and period of every brief whose record marks the change line spoken; and the team home's version date. A change has reached a person when their check-in line dates after the version, or, for a change that touches no one's seat, when the brief of a service they worked marks it. The third channel, the lead or mentor carrying it, is proven by the check-in date and by nothing else, because a conversation with no record is the record's tribal knowledge (WP p.13).

### 7.2 The order

The order is fixed and it is a rule about who hears first. For a structure change: the person, then the sheet, then the roster, then anyone (S8 P49). For a change that touches no person's seat: the entry, then every open period's next brief, then the team home, with the brief first because the roster reads the team home after it works and the brief is before. For a departure: the tension-loss row and the leads' review line, then the brief of every open period as coverage, then the inventory (S9 P30). For the one thing: the leads' review, then every brief that week, the same sentence. The stack places a structure change's check-in before the version publishes, so that the order is produced by the schedule and not by a lead remembering it; where the affected person's next shift is after the version must publish, the version waits, and a version that cannot wait is read as a load on the change's clock and not as a reason to skip the person.

### 7.3 The read that notices a failure

Session 9 read the channels' order as a diagnostic: "a change the floor heard first at the brief is sequence corruption on the entry" (S9 8.6). The read is a count on the change-reach record, at the leads' review's item 6 weekly and at the house review quarterly, never a person's impression: changes whose brief date precedes the affected person's check-in date; changes with a period's brief record missing the line since the version; changes with the team home's version later than the last brief; the one thing missing from any period's brief record in its week. No line names the room's owner who did not speak it or the lead who did not hold the check-in; a lead's repeated part in the pattern is a load reading on the lead's entry and goes where a lead's range breaches go (`86akhb2jg`, and until it closes the partners' review). ### 7.4 Three times, across languages

A change has not reached a period whose brief spoke it in a language a person on that roster does not hold, and has not reached the house while the team home carries it in one language only. ## 11\. The unblocking practice

Session 8 refused the workbook's process as an escalation up two lines and absorbed its form: the joint half-page as the record of a Class B or C hold, the refusal to hear a unilateral case, and a window (S8 P50). The practice, from the book's version (Book pp. 372 to 377), which is textually the workbook's (extraction notes).

**Who calls it.** Either party to a disagreement about a decision inside another owner's range, or between two owners, once the decision's owner has consulted and the objection stands. Her rule that "if you find yourself in a position where you're blocked by the lack of a resolved decision, it's your duty to instigate this process" (Book p.373) survives as a duty, because a disagreement carried unwritten is a Class B hold never written, which Session 10 named as sequence corruption at the interface (S10 P29). Her first test, whether the decision is reversible and "does it really matter" (Book p.372), is the register's own: a reversible decision inside an owner's range is the owner's, and the objection goes on the record as a dissent and the decision stands (S10 P26).

**The form.** One half-page, written jointly by the parties, in the language they share, translated under section 8.4 where the record's readers need it: the problem, stated as the outcome for the customer or the house and not as either party's position, which is her "goal of the joint enterprise in the voice of the user" (Book p.373) with the record's word; the options considered; the trade-offs both parties see, with the ones they could not resolve marked. Canon's six tests run on it, so it is short. No names appear beyond the seats that hold the ranges.

**Where it goes and who decides.** Inside one domain: the domain lead, in range, on the domain's record. Across domains: the owning domain's lead decides after the consultation the half-page records, and the half-page is the Class B hold on the leads' review record (S3 4.3). Between the two leads: Class C, the half-page on the partners' page, decided by the founder whose domain holds the outcome or by the reserved-class rule where it is one (S3 4.5). Never up two lines, never to a shared manager, never "recursively... until a decision is reached (or the stack overflows)" (Book p.374), because there is no one above both parties at Sŏn and the register already names who decides.

**The window.** Her five business days (Book p.373) is the loop window's shape (S8 P50): the half-page is on the leads' review record by the next leads' review, which is within seven days; the owner decides and writes the reason within the loop-closure window, thirty days (S4 P30); a Class C half-page is read at the next partners' review. A half-page open past the window is a standing item at the house review with its owner named, like any item past the window.

**The unilateral case.** Her three questions a manager asks before hearing one party alone (Book p.374) survive as the owner's refusal: an owner asked to decide by one party asks whether the parties have tried to agree, whether they have written the half-page, and whether the absent party was told the matter would come alone; three yeses and the owner asks the absent party to write their half before deciding; fewer and the owner declines to hear it. Her "It's not acceptable to refuse to unblock jointly" (Book pp. 374 to 375) is the rule that a party who declines to write jointly has their view recorded by the other party as "declined to write," and the owner decides on the half-page that exists.

**The record and the read-back.** The half-page, the decision, and its reason field stay on the record they were written on; the decision is read back on the next scorecard like any change (S9 P26); and her closing example, where the deciding manager "documents the rationale in reply to the escalation email" (Book p.377), is the reason field, on the record and not in a message.

**What it refuses.** A meeting called to resolve it, beyond the review at which its record is read; a decision in a room; a lead hearing one side at the brief or the close; a half-page about a person, whose destination is the check-in or the flag; a half-page used as a performance input.

**P29 (Recommended).** The unblocking practice: either party's duty to write; one joint half-page, the problem as the outcome for the customer or the house, the options, and the trade-offs, under the six tests; decided by the range's owner inside one domain, as a Class B hold across domains, as Class C between the leads, never up a line; on the record by the next leads' review and decided with its reason within the loop-closure window; an owner refuses a unilateral case until the absent party has been asked to write; the decision read back on the next scorecard.
