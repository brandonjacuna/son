## 1. Her ask, and what Sŏn already holds

### 1.1 Her ask

Her onboarding section opens on the moment of arrival: a new person is "on high alert for context and opportunities to learn," and a company that only has them "show up, fill out some paperwork, get set up on your laptop, and start working" has missed "a huge opportunity to start inculcating new hires into the company and the culture" (Book p.216). The curriculum she asks for carries three things in order: "The 'why' of the company, including the mission and the story behind it"; "The 'what' of the business, including current priorities and goals"; "The 'how' of company behaviors, including how people work together and the operating principles" (Book p.216). She asks that "company leadership should take part in onboarding new hires," that "Stories, not bullet points, are what really get a message across," and that the curriculum be "the fastest possible ramp to success" (Book p.216). Her own house runs "a weeklong core onboarding curriculum for every new hire, no matter their level," with team programs alongside, and the manager translates "the company operating system to the team level" in a first 1:1 or a one- to two-hour session (Book p.216).

The one voice in her section from a kitchen says what this page has to answer for a cook: "people just get thrown onto the job. I think this is the wrong way to do it. Training is very important, and understanding all the layers of the company is very important. If you're getting hired as a cook, it's not just about the kitchen," and "when you train someone, it's not like, 'Now you're trained.' No. You always have to be on top of it. It's a continuation of things" (Book p.216 to 217, Dominique Crenn).

Her prep rule: "all prep work should be done asynchronously before the onboarding program starts," paperwork and equipment included (Book p.217). Her nine outcomes for a person emerging from onboarding: a community of other new hires; leadership's vision and the principles; the business and its products; the users' perspective; the operating structures and current goals; a picture of the organization; key processes such as compliance; where the team fits; and "An ability to find internal resources to self-educate and seek assistance" (Book p.217).

Internal mobility: a survey at every team change, five questions, read over time for "bad mobility" (Book p.217 to 218); and manager-to-manager transitions, with a guide in the appendix (Book p.218; p.252 to 253). New hire onboarding: her "Working with Claire" document, the first meeting devoted to mutual expectations across six headings (onboarding, operating approach, management style, communication preferences, initial priorities, the upcoming career conversation), and the invitation to the new person to write their own (Book p.218 to 219). New leader onboarding: standardize, then add; the New Leader Experience with welcome emails, prescheduled meetings and pre-reads, a leadership assessment, a coach, first-month actions, and a ninety-day 360 (Book p.219); Table 5's six objectives and tactics (Book p.220); the daily or every-other-day standup for critical roles, and the "narrow" path between listening and acting (Book p.221); and the direct report's sidebar in nine parts, from "Commit to their success" to "Share stories and histories" (Book p.221 to 224).

Hiring mistakes: most surface "a few months into the person's tenure" as performance, but some right away, even before the start date; four example signals; "anything that violates your employee handbook"; get minor feedback to the person "quickly and strongly"; "The main thing is to do something," rescind or terminate swiftly, a short note to the team (Book p.224 to 225). Closing the loop: a retrospective by the hiring manager; ATS data on time in process, funnel performance, and interviewer stats; engagement and pulse survey prompts; marrying hiring data with performance data at milestones; surveying the rejected (Book p.225 to 226). Her closing line: "Hiring is expensive, but talent is everything" (Book p.227).

The exercises appendix reproduces the workbook's Chapter 3 templates (Book p.228 to 259). Four of the eight are Session 6's and are answered there: the interview framework and rubric, the sample questions, the written exercise, and the candidate review framework (Workbook p.43 to 77; S6 P17 to P55). This page confirmed against the book's versions (Book p.229 to 251) that nothing there is absent from Session 6, and it answers them by reference (section 15). The four that are this page's: the Manager Transitions Guide (Book p.252 to 253; Workbook p.78), Working with Claire (Book p.253 to 257; Workbook p.80 to 83), the Working with Me template (Book p.257; Workbook p.84), and the New Leader Experience with its schedule (Book p.257 to 259; Workbook p.86 to 88). ## 2. What onboarding is for at Sŏn, what it may conclude, and what it refuses to conclude

### 2.1 The intended behavior, stated so it can be tested

The People Systems Designer's first move is to state, in behavioral terms, what the system is supposed to make people do, because a system with no statable intended behavior cannot be evaluated. Her section states an intent, "the fastest possible ramp to success" (Book p.216), and the record states a stronger one, "early success is planned rather than hoped for" (WP p.18). Neither is behavioral. This page states the behavior, and every mechanism after this section exists to produce it or is cut.

At the close of the ninety-day plan, a person in an hourly edge seat: holds their seat solo on the schedule at service tempo, reproducing the seat's entry procedures without a mentor beside them; states their decision-rights range and its escalation triggers unprompted, in their own words, and has used the range at least once inside it; speaks of the customer as the customer in every sentence, spoken and written, and of a dish with felt knowledge of the dishes that exist; has opened the learning platform and moved on it without being asked, so the invisible advocate has a record to read; has told the house, at six check-ins, what was in the way, and can say what the building did about it; and knows the seat after theirs and what it requires, from their own account on the team home, without asking anyone.

That is the whole of it. Onboarding is not for producing a person who feels welcomed, which is a mood, or one who has "been onboarded," which is a document. It is for producing the six behaviors above, and the Realist's addition is that the first of them has to hold on a Friday with a full book, not on the calm Tuesday the sequence was designed on.

### 2.2 What onboarding may conclude

Four things, and each has a record.
