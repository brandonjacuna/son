## 3\. What "team" means at Sŏn, and whether the service periods are one team or several

### 3.1 Her definitions

A team is "a group with a shared long-term mission," lasting "at least one year," working "on a collection of persistent jobs to be done," with lasting structures, norms, and culture invested in it. A project is "a subgroup with a shorter-term goal" inside a team. A working group is "a group with a short-term mission, usually comprised of individuals from multiple teams and functions," "a collection of individuals, not a team," with a directly responsible individual and a spin-down (Book pp. 265 to 268, Table 6). Her introduction adds the distinction that matters: most of what people call teams "are actually just groups of individuals parallel processing their way through work," and the milestone is when people "work as an integrated entity rather than as a set of individuals operating alone" (Book p.262).

### 3.2 What Sŏn holds, and the one sense the word carries

Session 3 settled the goal-bearing units: the company, the service period, the domain, and the person, which "intersect rather than nest," with her division and team units "not used" (S3 P3.2). Session 2 refused her team charter at the team unit because "domain ownership and the founding seat descriptions do that work at eight leadership lines" (S2 P11.1). What neither session did is say what the word "team" means when the record uses it, and the record uses it constantly: "the team," "team development," "team health," "the culinary team," "the whole team" (WP pp. 10, 20). Sessions 9 to 11 inherit whatever this page says.

The team's position is that Sŏn has exactly one team in her sense, and it is the whole house. It has a shared long-term mission (the charter's, when ratified), a collection of persistent jobs that are never complete, and lasting structures invested in it, which is this operating system. Her integrated-entity test is the record's description of a great night: "distributed intelligence rather than coordinated direction," "every element in relationship with every other, information moving laterally without routing through a chain of command" (WP p.8). That is one integrated entity, not several, and it is what the web is a drawing of.

Nothing smaller than the house is a team in her sense. A domain is not a team: it is a set of seats with one owner, and its "structure" is the seat inventory's rows and the domain lead's functions (S5 P33), not norms and a culture of its own. A strand is not a team: it is an intelligence and a primary output (WP p.10 to 11). A service period is not a team, for three reasons this page states and section 3.3 tests: it has no people of its own, because one labor pool staffs the periods (S5 P10) and a person's periods are a field on their sheet; it has no lead of its own, because a period adds no leadership line (section 3.4); and its goals are "one set across both strands and the kitchen" (S3 P7.1), which is her own rule against organizing goals by function, with the unit translated.

So when her Chapter 4 says "team" and the later sessions must translate it, there are three readings, and each sentence of hers takes one: when she means the collective that achieves more as a unit than alone, the word is the house; when she means a manager's reports, the people one person develops and holds check-ins with, the word is the domain, and the "manager" is the domain lead in Session 5's sense; and when she means who is on tonight, the word is the service roster, the people the brief named for that service across every domain, which exists for one service and dissolves at the close. The record's "the floor team" and "the culinary team" are domains. The record's "the team" is the house.

**P6 (Recommended).** Sŏn has one team in her sense, the house. A domain, a strand, and a service period are not teams. Her word is translated three ways for Sessions 9 to 11: the house for the collective, the domain for a manager's reports, and the service roster for who is on tonight. The record's "floor team" and "culinary team" are read as domains.

### 3.3 One team or several, answered for any ratified set, with the interfaces as the test

The answer, stated for any ratified set and not for four: the service periods are one team met at different hours, and the structure makes that true by construction rather than by assertion. A period is a period of one restaurant, and not a second restaurant at the same address, exactly when its daypart charter's dependent interfaces field is not empty and its provided interface is consumed by another period or by the connective layer. The ledger already binds this: "A period whose dependent interfaces are empty is a separate restaurant, not a period of one" (S4 P66). This page adds the structural consequence. A period that passed that test shares the labor pool, the kitchen, the customer record, the leadership lines, the inventory, and the readiness test with every other period; it adds seats, a designation set, a register block, and a period plan; it adds no lead, no line, and no second web.

## 4\. The structure, drawn

The web is drawn in words and once as a text diagram. It has a center and an outside edge and no altitude; the diagram is read from the center outward, never from top to bottom, and nothing in it is above anything else. The record's own open item is the web "rendered as a designed organizational artifact rather than a chart" (WP p.15), and the owner of that render is open (`86akh7ru5`); what is drawn here is the structural content the render must carry, not the render.

### 4.1 The center

The center is "information convergence," "a systems function, not a rank" (WP p.9). It holds the culinary and conceptual vision the leads serve (WP p.10), which is written into the record and canon so that it is code-bearing rather than a person's charisma (S1 section 3). Three founding seats own it as domains: Brandon's seat owns the concept, the operating systems, and the experience; Dominic's seat owns the capital, the financial architecture, and the technology build; the chef partner's seat owns the culinary vision, under whichever account of the seat the ruling chooses (WP p.3; `86akh2qrp`). The founders' cadence is the partners' review, the domain read, and the gate review (S4 P53). The founders assign, review, and escalate no person (S4 P58). Their presence on the floor runs on the service metronome (ledger).

Two leads sit at the center as co-leads of different systems: the Operations Lead, "accountable to the operational outcomes: labor efficiency, cost targets, compliance, vendor performance, and facility integrity," who "oversees those systems and is accountable to their outputs, not their manual production"; and the Maitre d, who "owns the how of the experience," "accountable to experience quality and team health" (WP p.10). "Neither reports to the other" (WP p.10). Each pairs monthly with a partner: the Maitre d with Brandon, the Operations Lead's pairing founder-gated with Dominic recommended (S4 P59, P60).

### 4.2 The domains, and their leads

A domain is a set of seats with one owner, and its lead is "the node with the most connections to it: holding the check-in, opening the seat, deciding the hire; not assigning, not reviewing from above, not holding advancement" (ledger; S5 P33). The domains, with their leads as the record names them and as this page completes them:

*   **The floor.** Two strands, outward-facing and inward-facing (WP p.10 to 11). Domain lead: the Maitre d, for both strands (section 8). Seats: server, host, runner, and any further floor seat a charter calls for. Per-service designations: the room, the door (the record's Lead Host), and the sequence.
*   **Beverage.** "The Head of Beverage owns the program, and outward-facing and inward-facing execution map to the same two-strand logic as the floor" (WP p.11). Domain lead: the Head of Beverage, a seat (section 4.4). Seats: bartender and any further bar seat a charter calls for.
*   **The kitchen.** "Domain owners are Station Leads, and domain execution is Chef on the line and Chef on prep, neither a promotion from the other. The Steward owns the continuity of the kitchen environment" (WP p.11). Domain lead: the chef seat, under either branch of the ruling (S5 P57, P58). Everything below the spine in section 16 is chef-gated.
*   **The operating architecture.** The stack, the schedule, compliance, vendors, the facility, the record's maintenance (WP p.10). Owner: the Operations Lead. This domain has, in the record, no seats beneath it: the back-office administrator is removed by software (WP p.22). Whether it ever holds a seat is a structure change under section 17.

The Operations Lead and the Maitre d are domain leads for their own domains and for no seat in the other's. The check-in of a lead is held by a partner under Session 3's Class C rule, as Session 7 read it (S7 P21, finding 19.7, `86akhb2jg`).

### 4.3 The outside edge, and the strands

Every seat that is not a lead's sits at the outside edge, "the primary receivers of the operation," "the most sensitive position" (WP p.9). Within a domain the seats are strands, not levels. On the floor: the outward-facing strand's primary output is the experience at the table, its intelligence "relational and expressive and emotionally attuned under load," and the customer feels it; the inward-facing strand's primary output is "the operational sequence that makes the outward strand possible," its intelligence "architectural and coordinated and timing-precise," and the customer "never feels this strand, they feel that everything arrived right" (WP p.10 to 11). Server and host are outward-facing seats; runner is an inward-facing seat (section 8). Beverage maps the same way: the bartender is outward-facing, "Bartender always in proximity" (Canon 1.0, section 06), and any bar seat whose output is the program's sequence is inward-facing. The kitchen's strands are chef-gated.

### 4.4 The Head of Beverage, as a seat

The record carries the Head of Beverage three ways: as a functional designation that "owns beverage program execution for this shift," "not permanent elevations above anyone" (WP p.10); as the owner of the program, "In beverage, the Head of Beverage owns the program" (WP p.11); and as the seat that inventory, costing, and purchasing in software "collapse a wine manager and a bar manager into" (WP p.22). The second and third are a seat; the first is what the seat does on a given night (finding 21.3). The team reads it as a seat and a leadership line, because a program is owned across periods and a designation exists for one service, and because the bar seats need a domain lead who opens them, holds their check-ins, and decides their hires, which a nightly designation cannot do (S5 15.7's reasoning, applied here). The Head of Beverage's own check-in is held by the Maitre d, as the lead whose outcomes, experience quality and team health, the program's execution serves, with the Operations Lead holding the program's inventory, costing, and purchasing outputs through the stack. This is one seat with two leads reading two different things, and it is not dual reporting in her sense, because neither lead assigns or reviews the Head of Beverage; each reads a record (section 6.1).

**P9 (Recommended).** The structure is: a center holding the vision, three founding seats owning domains, two parallel leads; four domains, the floor, beverage, the kitchen, and the operating architecture, each with one lead; strands within domains; every non-lead seat at the outside edge. ## 6\. Her lines, her structures, and her hinge, translated or refused

### 6.1 Solid and dotted lines

Her solid line is direct reporting; her dotted line is indirect, and the two are used to give a strong leader "leverage and control, even over supportive functions" (Book p.265). Under business leads, dual reporting means "a primary (solid-line) and secondary (dotted-line) manager," captured "in our HR systems," with both managers collaborating "on goal setting and performance reviews" and "separate 1:1s with each manager" (Book p.275). Every element of that assumes a person standing above another who assigns and reviews, which the ledger refuses.

What Sŏn uses instead is one connection type and two kinds of reading. Every seat has one domain lead, named by seat on the sheet's field 3 (S5 P33), who holds the check-in, opens the seat, and decides the hire. That is the only lead a seat has, and it is not a reporting line, because the lead does not assign or review. Where a seat's work is read by a second lead, the Head of Beverage's program outputs by the Operations Lead, a runner's timing by the kitchen's pass owner, the second lead reads a record, the stack's outputs or the shift close, and holds no check-in and no decision about the person. Her dotted line's function, that a second leader's judgment reaches the work, is carried by the record being read and by Class B consultation before a decision that crosses domains (S3 4.3). Her solid line's function, that someone develops the person, is carried by the domain lead's check-in. No seat has two leads; no lead holds a "separate 1:1"; nothing about a person is written by a second lead.

**P15 (Recommended).** Sŏn has no solid or dotted lines. A seat has one domain lead, and where a second lead's judgment must reach the work it reaches the record, not the person. Dual reporting, the secondary manager, the second 1:1, and any HR-system field for a second manager are refused.

### 6.2 Functional, business-line, vertical, horizontal

Her macro choice is functional or product-line, and within it teams orient vertically (a region, a product) or horizontally (a support function across all verticals) (Book pp. 270 to 271, Figures 15 and 16). Her business unit "has a unique product that is priced for and sold to a specific customer segment, and can operate independently" (Book p.272).

At Sŏn the axes exist and they are not org-chart axes. The domains are functional: the floor, beverage, the kitchen, the operating architecture. The service periods are the record's business lines in her sense of "different first customers at different times" (WP p.35), each with its own reason to exist and its own operating logic (WP p.34). A period could be drawn as her vertical and a domain as her horizontal running across all periods, and the drawing would be accurate and useless, because at Sŏn the intersection has no manager. The domains own the seats and the check-ins; the periods own the charters, the goals, and the interfaces (section 7); and a person sits in one domain and in whichever periods their sheet's field 4 names. Her business unit is refused outright: a period that "can operate independently" is the separate restaurant the interface test refuses (section 3.3), and the record's whole thesis is that the periods share "one layer underneath that holds all of them" (WP p.33).

**P16 (Recommended).** The domains are the functional axis and the periods are the business-line axis, and the structure is neither functional nor business-line because the intersection has no manager: domains own seats and check-ins, periods own charters, goals, and interfaces. The business unit is refused as the separate restaurant the interface test exists to prevent.

### 6.3 The hybrid, the hinge, and business leads

Her hybrid gives a business lead solid-line ownership of product and engineering, dotted lines into the customer-facing functions, and a hinge, "the pivot point where you turn the focus from the product toward the customer," commonly product marketing (Book pp. 272 to 273, Figure 17). Her business lead runs forums, sets goals for dotted-line reports, calibrates their performance, is evaluated on the business's results, and "there is currently no BL ladder" (Book pp. 274 to 278).

The hinge is the one idea in her structures section that names something the record also names, and the record puts it in a room rather than a team. "The bar is the hinge of the dinner-to-late-night transition" (Canon 1.0, section 06). The pivot from one register to another at Sŏn is a place and a person-triggered moment, not a function embedded between two organizations. And the pivot from the operation toward the customer, which is what her product marketing hinge does, is the whole outward-facing strand: "the customer feels this strand" (WP p.11). Sŏn has no hinge team because the customer-facing pivot is a strand in every domain, not a team between domains.

Her business lead's functions map to the period's holders in section 7, and every one of the lead's powers that assumes altitude is refused: a period has no lead who calibrates the people who work it, no forums of its own beyond the brief and the close, and no ladder question because no seat has one. Her forums rule, "create the fewest, simplest, and most lightweight structures that work" (Book p.277), is the ledger's four-part entry test. Her headcount accounting, dollar budgets and "ring-fenced" heads reported at QBRs (Book pp. 276 to 277), touches figures: financials are not a context source for this program, the house has no headcount to ring-fence because one pool staffs every period, and the seat inventory carries a count per charter and no cost (S5 I1). The gap is stated: whatever labor accounting the financial model runs per period is Dominic's domain and is not on this page. Her performance management and calibration route to Sessions 12 to 15; her goal setting is held by Sessions 3 and 4 (S3 P7.1, S4 section 7).

**P17 (Recommended).** The hinge is refused as a team and held as the record holds it: the bar as the room's hinge between dinner and late night, and the outward-facing strand as the customer-facing pivot in every domain. Business leads, their forums, calibration, and headcount accounting are refused; the period's holders in section 7 carry what survives, and labor accounting per period is a stated gap in Dominic's domain.

### 6.4 Keeping the structure flexible, and reexamining it yearly

Her rule: preserve optionality, remind people the structure will change, reexamine "at least once a year," and neither too often nor too rarely (Book p.265). Sŏn's structure changes by versioned entry (section 17) and is reexamined at every gate review and at the mechanism reset (S4 P39), which is more often than yearly while periods are opening and yearly after. The flexibility she wants is built in differently: the structure is fixed and the designations are fluid. Who holds the room, the door, the sequence, and the pass changes by service at the brief; what the seats are and who leads them changes only by version. Her warning that "too often can be destabilizing" is the ledger's first-quarter freeze on the room's mechanisms (S4 section 2).

**P18 (Recommended).** The structure is reexamined at every gate review and at the mechanism reset, changes only by versioned entry, and gets its flexibility from per-service designations rather than from restructuring.

## 7\. Who holds a service period

The ledger binds this page to say who holds a service period's goals, measures, and charter without making the period a team in her sense. Session 3 gave the shape: a period's goals are "one set across both strands and the kitchen," set by "the Maitre d, the Operations Lead, and the Chef Partner together, because the period is the unit at which their domains intersect" (S3 P7.1). Session 2 gave the charter a "held by" field (S2 P11.2). Session 4 gave the charter its drafting and ruling: the partners with the two leads draft, the partners rule, the chef's seat is in every operating logic field (S4 P49, P50). This section says what "held by" holds.

A service period is held by three things and no person. Its charter is held by the partners' page as a versioned document, ruled by the partners, with the chef partner's vote on lunch the record's own rule (WP p.36). Its goals and measures are held by the three leads together, as Session 3 set, with no one of them owning the period: a period metric that crosses the seam is split at the seam (S3 P9.3), and a period metric that cannot be split has as its owner the charter's "held by" entry, which names one of the three leads per metric, never the period. Its interfaces are held by the leads' review's weekly interface read (S4 11.2) once two periods are open. Its live functions on a given service are held by the designations named at that service's brief. Its register, the operating logic field, is where the charter says how "service, authority, and pace differ in this period from dinner" (S2 P11.2), and that field is how authority differs by period without adding a leadership line (`86akh3uxu`): the field states, per period, which ranges widen or narrow, which designations exist, and which triggers change destination. Under dinner alone the field is dinner's own and every other period's field is written against it.

## 10\. Levels and ladders, what a lead enters at, and which seats are salaried

### 10.1 Whether a single house needs them

### 10.3 What a lead enters at

## 17\. How a structure change is made and reaches the team

### 17.1 The change

A structure change is any of: creating or retiring a seat; changing a leadership line, its domain, or its lead; adding a period's seats or designations; changing a designation's rule; changing which seat carries a pool mark or a salaried mark. Each is a versioned entry on the partners' page, on the inventory (S5 I1), stating the reason in the record's own terms: the tension lost or the class that resolved to nothing, the gate that opened, or the charter that was ruled. Her "narrative or talking points that explain why the new structure addresses the strategic need" (Book p.281) survives as that reason line, written before the entry and not after. Her "sketching out the desired team structure without names attached" (Book p.281) is the inventory's own form: rows are seats, and no row carries a name. Her advice to get feedback from an HR partner and colleagues who know the leadership team becomes the leads' review reading the proposed row before the partners' review versions it (S5 P14's trigger).

### 17.2 How it reaches the team

Her method is a message to each affected individual, framed for their growth, with next steps and a monitoring plan, then broader communication (Book p.281 to 282). At Sŏn a change reaches the team by four channels that already exist, in this order: the affected person's own check-in, on the clock, where the change is read together and the person's own line is written (S4 P20), which is her individual message without the framing, because the person page refuses a lead's account of a person; the seat sheet's new version, which re-reads every open candidate for the seat (S5 P13); the shift brief, spoken, as "any change to a range, trigger, goal, or mechanism since the last brief" (S3 shift brief); and the team home, where the inventory and the render show the change to anyone. No change reaches the team by a lead's announcement, by an all-hands, or by a document the person is asked to acknowledge. Her monitoring plan is the readiness rows and the variety pass at the next gate. Internal communications depth, how the house tells itself things, is Session 11's.

**P49 (Recommended).** A structure change is a versioned entry on the inventory on the partners' page, with its reason written first, proposed through the leads' review and versioned by the partners' review. It reaches the team by the affected person's check-in, the seat sheet's version, the shift brief, and the team home, in that order, and by no announcement.

### 17.3 The unblocking process, answered in one paragraph

The workbook's unblocking process documents a disagreement in half a page, emails it to both managers, and escalates "with their respective managers recursively" to a shared manager who decides (Workbook pp. 98 to 102). Its escalation runs up two reporting lines to a person standing above both, which is the structure the ledger's departure refuses. At Sŏn the same disagreement has three destinations that already exist: inside one domain, the domain lead decides in range; across domains, the owning domain decides after consulting the affected one, and an unresolved objection goes to the founder whose domain holds the outcome (S3 Class B); between the two leads, Class C. What survives from the workbook is the form and two rules: the half-page (the problem, the options, the trade-offs) is the form a Class B hold takes on the leads' review record; a lead refuses to hear a unilateral case until the other party has been asked to write it jointly; and the five-business-day commitment is the loop window's shape. The process itself, as a communication practice, is Session 11's.

**P50 (Recommended).** The unblocking process is refused as an escalation up two lines and absorbed as a form: the joint half-page as the record of a Class B or C hold, the refusal to hear a unilateral case, and a window. Routed to Session 11 for the practice.
