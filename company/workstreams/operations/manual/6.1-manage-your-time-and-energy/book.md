# 6.1 Manage your time and energy: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Conclusion, pp. 485 to 490. Full text: `sources/extraction/build/s16/book.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## Why this closes the book

The book's first four chapters and its closing pages share one throughline: self-awareness. Chapters 1 through 5 are about the people around you, the systems, principles, and structures that bring them together in service of the company's mission. None of that works at full power if you yourself are not strong. Managing your own time, energy, and career, and investing in your relationships, is what lets you use everything else in the book (p. 485).

## The struggle is normal

Seniority does not reduce the number of ways a day can go wrong; it multiplies them. A top performer threatens to quit, a top customer defects, a company meeting looms unprepared, a cross-functional project derails, all at once. She points to Ben Horowitz's term for this in *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*: "the struggle," when nothing is easy and nothing feels right (pp. 485 to 486).

## Diagnose what gives and takes your energy

Map good days and bad days. The simplest version: check marks on a calendar, reviewed after a month for the good and bad weeks and the trends inside them. Her own finding: weeks with more than one work event that kept her from dinner and bedtime with her kids were bad weeks, which led to a personal guideline of one late work night a week, occasionally broken (p. 486). The goal is to learn what combination of activities produces your best performance, then set boundaries that protect it.

## Sort your stuck tasks into two piles

Review incomplete to-dos monthly. Tasks that stall tend to fall into two categories (pp. 486 to 487):

- **Tasks you're unsure how to do, or aren't the best-suited person for.** These should have been delegated or done with help sought early.
- **Tasks that don't fit your normal way of operating**, for instance work needing a full day of deep thinking when your schedule is built around meetings. These call for a schedule change, not more willpower.

## Tactics to get through the day

Once you know what energizes and demotivates you, a set of tactics (pp. 487 to 490):

- **Delegate.** Almost everything on your plate is a growth opportunity for someone on your team (cross-references her delegation section at p. 284).
- **Give yourself permission to set boundaries.** No one else will protect them for you. Her example: she only started exercising regularly once she decided it was part of the job of being her best possible self as a leader.
- **Know when you're most productive**, and protect that window. Hers is the morning.
- **Learn to disappoint people with your time.** A report's request almost always feels urgent; managers spend too much time on urgent-but-less-important work. Ask requesters for a real deadline rather than defaulting to same-day, and notice how many "urgent" meetings exist only because a 10 to 15 minute async task didn't happen.
- **Compartmentalize.** A hard 1:1 or bad meeting can derail a whole day if you can't set it aside. Don't schedule all the hard conversations on one day; build in transition time, and place energizing people and activities, even a short walk, strategically in the calendar.
- **Reframe strengths and weaknesses.** Echoing Jeffrey Garten's line from Chapter 1, that your biggest strength may be your biggest weakness: notice the flip side of every compliment. Her own example is operating independently, which produces resentment toward colleagues who "should" have volunteered, and a reluctance to seek input even when she isn't best suited to a task. When you can only see your weaknesses, remember that in a fast-growing company, most mistakes are quickly forgotten because everything is changing anyway; reframe, and move on.
- **Focus on the important and urgent.** The Eisenhower matrix: sort tasks by important versus not important and urgent versus not urgent, and make time for important-but-not-urgent work, which otherwise falls away and comes back to haunt you.
- **Ask for help.** Her Google story: newly responsible for integrating YouTube's operations on top of her existing scope, and a new parent, she broke down in a 1:1 asking her manager to help her prioritize, and it was only in saying it aloud that she recognized how overwhelmed she was. They agreed she needed to recruit a full-time leader for YouTube. Her point, made twice: 1:1s sometimes involve crying, and that's not something to be mortified about.

## Quoted voice

Dan Weiss, president and CEO of the Metropolitan Museum of Art, on self-awareness as a precondition for leadership: learning from mistakes, evolving from feedback, and being a good listener (pp. 490 to 491).
