# 4.1 Team structures: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 4, pp. 261 to 281. Full text: `sources/extraction/build/s08/book.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## Why this chapter exists

Change is inevitable in a high-growth company. Beyond hiring and developing strong leaders and managers, the best way to navigate that change is to invest in building strong teams (p. 262). Many groups that call themselves teams are really just individuals processing work in parallel; they never become a collective that achieves more together than its members would alone. Team development is not a task to finish; it is a set of habits, some the manager's and some the team's, kept up over time (p. 262).

She draws a contrast with the instinct to treat every disruption, including new hires, as a reason to overhaul team process. A person joining a high-functioning team's culture will absorb it if you help them understand the norms and the relationships among them; the capacity to absorb change is itself part of a team's development (pp. 262 to 263).

Her starting sequence for a new role or team: determine what type of group the work requires, diagnose the current state of the team and its members, including their career histories and aspirations, and use that understanding to decide how to delegate and whether to restructure. From there, build the environment and operating approach the team needs to exceed its aspirations (p. 263).

## Team structures follow strategy

Organizational structures exist to bring resources together in whatever arrangement best serves the goals (p. 264). When structuring teams, start with strategy: look at plans and priorities (she points back to Chapter 2 on planning) and arrange teams to serve both near-term goals and long-term trajectory. What a structure emphasizes, such as how much solid-line or dotted-line authority a divisional leader gets, should match what the strategy needs from that division (pp. 264 to 265).

No structure is permanent. She treats structural flexibility as an asset: preserve optionality, since restructuring to meet a changed strategy or changed circumstances is normal. Tell people up front that the structure will change and that they should expect to shift portfolios as the company evolves. Her cadence: reexamine divisional and team structures at least once a year in a fast-moving environment. Too frequent a review is destabilizing; too infrequent lets the structure drift out of step with the business or the talent (p. 265).

## Teams, projects, and working groups

Three organizing constructs, often used interchangeably to the detriment of productivity (pp. 265 to 266):

- **Teams.** A group with a shared, long-term mission, at least a year in duration. Teams work best with few dependencies. Because they persist, invest in lasting structures, norms, and culture. The mission is never fully complete; the team adapts over time to keep making progress.
- **Projects.** A subgroup with a shorter-term goal, usually a few weeks to months, typically drawn from the same team and sharing its long-term mission. Projects are natural subgroups within teams, need little setup, and are sometimes cross-functional in a limited way (for example design and product). Once the task is done, the project should end; if it does not, ask whether it is really a team or sub-team with a mission instead.
- **Working groups.** A group with a short-term mission, usually less than three months, drawing individuals from multiple teams and functions. They are a collection of individuals, not a team, and need more upfront investment to define objectives and a governance structure, since they have no permanent owner. Identify a directly responsible individual (DRI), define scope, and outline how the group spins down once its mission is met. A working group's mission is often to study a problem and recommend a path forward, which then becomes tasks divided among the teams represented in the group.

Before creating any of the three, ask: what are the group's objectives, what skills does it need, and how long will it need to last. If you get the duration or type wrong, which happens, acknowledge it and change the structure, for example moving a working group's work into a permanent team. The failure mode to avoid is letting a temporary structure persist too long: it was never resourced at the right level, and its persistence confuses the permanent teams that should hold ongoing accountability (pp. 266 to 267).

### Sidebar: Code Yellows (pp. 268 to 270)

Stripe's structure, written up by CTO David Singleton, for handling a one-off threat quickly: a working group given special powers to move fast on a specific, usually urgent, risk. A Code Yellow team is shielded from other obligations, resourced quickly (often through painful trade-offs against other projects), runs daily standups and situation reports, may get dedicated physical space, can convene the leadership team with a 24-hour response SLA, often works longer hours including weekends, and reports status at every all-hands. It should last only as long as necessary, capped around ten weeks to avoid burnout, and requires leadership team approval to trigger, so the tool does not get overused.

## Structuring teams: functional versus product, vertical versus horizontal

Organizations are structured functionally or by product or business line. Within an organization, teams orient horizontally or vertically (p. 270).

Earlier-stage companies tend to organize around functions (product, engineering, support, sales), which fits when there is one product, and functional structures also help during rapid growth because people can focus on learning how the function works while onboarding new hires into evolving processes (p. 271).

She treats functional structures as primarily vertical, translating down to the division level, with horizontal teams sometimes embedded within them. A vertical team is dedicated to a region or a specific product area (a sales team for APAC, a product team for payment methods). A horizontal team cuts across all verticals in a division, for example a central analytics team or Stripe's ops platform, central ops, and analytics and insights teams that serve every support vertical (pp. 271 to 272).

### Business units and the hinge

Founders often ask about forming business units once a company has multiple products. In its truest form, a business unit has a unique product priced and sold to a specific customer segment and can operate independently apart from centralized functions like finance and HR. In practice divisions are rarely that clean, because the same customer segment often buys more than one product, which means a structure is still needed to present a single point of contact and a consistent support experience across products, unless each product is branded and sold independently (p. 272).

Where a company wants a single leader accountable for each product's performance while still centralizing distribution and support, the result is a hybrid structure. Designing one means placing what she calls the hinge, the pivot point where focus turns from the product toward the customer. A common hinge is a product marketing team, dedicated by product, representing customer needs and bridging sales and product. As a company adds products, expect to keep reevaluating team structures and moving the hinge, shifting authority and accountability toward whichever product or segment needs it (pp. 272 to 273).

### Sidebar: business leads at Stripe (pp. 274 to 279)

Stripe's version of a business unit leader is the business lead (BL): a clearly identified DRI accountable for a discrete business, with product development roles reporting directly and other functions dotted in. Eligibility requires a clearly defined business, evaluable on metrics mostly extrinsic to the company and largely within the team's control (revenue, margin, active users), and groups must be explicitly approved, usually through the graduation of an emerging business. The write-up covers reporting (dual reporting is common, with a solid-line and dotted-line manager, formally captured in HR systems), headcount accounting (BLs set a dollar budget and are tracked at quarterly business reviews), forums, goal setting, and performance management (BLs are evaluated primarily on their business's outcomes and are expected to meet the same people-manager bar as anyone else). A closing concern: because BL engineering teams can be organizationally separate from the rest of engineering, Stripe works to preserve shared culture, a shared talent bar, and shared technology and best practices across all of engineering regardless of reporting lines.

## Number of reports and layering

As a manager, track the macro company structure even while making micro choices for your own team; how a function like design interfaces with a hybrid structure can need to change depending on where decisions are made (pp. 279 to 280).

On direct reports: she is not a fan of "I" structures, one leader with only one or two direct reports, but also warns against so many direct reports that there is no bandwidth left for strategic work and unanticipated problems. The target is adequately flat, with room for the leaders being planned for (p. 280).

Adding a layer of management under yourself is a common, uncomfortable necessity in high growth. Her method (pp. 280 to 281):

1. Return to strategy and sketch the team structure the goals require, detailing each role's remit without attaching names yet.
2. Write a narrative or talking points explaining why the new structure serves the strategic need and why the new layer is necessary. Like writing a press release before the product exists, this step exposes gaps in the thinking and surfaces objections early.
3. Once the narrative holds up, consider who on the team is "scaling to the call," meaning who has shown the capacity to take on more ownership, and how the assignment supports their development. Weigh which combination of people and functions best serves both the new leader's success and the business. Getting feedback from an HR partner and a colleague or two, especially ones who know the leadership team, can help.
4. When communicating the change, message each affected person individually. Acknowledge that no longer reporting to you may feel like a loss, explain why the new structure serves the team's strategy, describe how the person fits into it, emphasize their long-term growth and what the new role offers them, and say how you will monitor whether the change is working. Tell them the timeline for finalizing and communicating to the broader organization. Remind everyone that further evolution is likely.
