# 4.2 Diagnosing team state: considerations

What the white paper holds, what the earlier work found worth keeping, the options and what each commits Sŏn to, and where Sŏn may diverge from the book. Topics follow the book's order (pp. 282 to 286). "WP p. N" is the white paper; "cons. N" is a section of this file. Nothing here is a decision.

Vocabulary, in plain words: a **failure mode** is one of the five conditions the white paper names as a diagnostic (WP p. 14). An **evidence line** is one written observation that a mode is present somewhere, with its source. The **stack** is the software. Seat, domain, strand, and designation are as 4.1 defines them; check-in, person page, leads' review, house review, gate review, and mechanism reset are as 2.3 defines them.

## 1. What a diagnosis reads, and in what order

**Book.** Once a lasting team exists, ask whether it has the structures, plan, and people to deliver; come back to the operating system first (is the mission or a goal unclear); then read the people and the dynamics; if "is the team executing well" is not a clear yes, dig in (pp. 282 to 283). The team is read first, as a whole; only if it cannot exceed the sum of its parts does the read go to each member (p. 284).

**White paper.** Stated outright: "Before intervening at the people level, identify which failure mode is actually present" (WP p. 14). Three of the five modes say the fix is not a person-level move: tension slack ("the fix is not replacing the person"), sequence corruption ("system maintenance, not people management"), cooling failure ("a bandwidth problem, not a morale problem") (WP p. 14). A departure is "a tension re-establishment problem," not a backfill problem (WP p. 09). Universal problems are design problems, and the first question "is not who failed. It is what allowed this to happen" (WP p. 17). The white paper is silent on what a "team" is for reading purposes; 4.1.2 decides that.

**From the earlier work.** A read at Sŏn can land on six different things, only one of which is a team in the ordinary sense: the house, a domain (a lead's seats), a service roster (tonight's people, dissolved at close), a seat, a service period, and a person. The earlier work proposed a fixed order: name the object, name the mode from a record, name the mechanism the fix touches, and only for tension slack, and only after the domain-level fix is made, may a read reach a person. It also proposed that a problem fitting no mode be recorded as its own outcome ("no regulator": nothing in the structure owns it) and routed to the structure change process (4.1.33), not forced into the nearest mode. The book's "boat" (the people a leader depends on regardless of reporting line) was read as answered by the service roster.

**Options.**
- (a) The book's order: read the house or domain as a team, then each person, with the lead's judgment as the instrument. Commits Sŏn to leads forming and holding written views of people, and to reconciling that with the person page's rule (2.3.11: nothing the person has not heard).
- (b) The white paper's order, strict: object, mode, mechanism, person last and only through tension slack after the domain fix. Commits Sŏn to building the record sources the read needs before it can read anything, and to accepting that some person-level problems will be reached late.
- (c) The white paper's order as the default, with a named exception: a lead who sees a person-level problem may raise it directly in the person's check-in or through Chapter 5's paths, without waiting for the mode read. Commits Sŏn to two doors, and to stating clearly which door each kind of problem goes through so the mode read is not bypassed by habit.

**Divergence.** The book expects the manager's judgment to be the instrument. The white paper puts the record first and the person last. Sŏn will likely diverge from the book here; the question is how far.

## 2. The frame: five dysfunctions or five modes

**Book.** Lencioni's five dysfunctions (absence of trust, fear of conflict, lack of commitment, avoidance of accountability, inattention to results) are her reference for what a survey should look for; a dysfunction found is discussed with the team, and the team commits to change (p. 283). The skill-will matrix gives four team-level readings: too little skill (train, or hire), too much skill (raise the mission), too little will (a bolder mission, name the dysfunction, address individuals sapping will), too much will (set expectations, scope, check talent against goals) (pp. 284 to 286).

**White paper.** Five modes, each with a stated fix (WP p. 14). Behind them, four machine failures: processing, memory, bus, cooling (WP p. 13). The two sets do not map one to one: memory failure (knowledge leaving with a person) has no twin among the five modes. Cooling failure names retention as evidence (WP p. 14), while retention is elsewhere a measure read over time (WP p. 23).

**From the earlier work.** Each dysfunction can be read as a symptom of a mode: absence of trust as sequence corruption or cooling failure depending on source; avoidance of accountability as node overload or a structural hole; inattention to results as a goal or metric problem (2.2). The psychological-safety half of "absence of trust" was held out of the diagnostic and left for the team environment (4.5); only its signals (capture rate, error-reporting rate) were read. The skill-will matrix's team-level readings survive as mechanism reads: "too little skill" is a training gap (3.3), "too much skill" with no next unlock is skill invisibility, "too much will" is a goal-count or bandwidth flag (2.2.23). The earlier work also proposed a causal order for fixes: sequence corruption first (a degraded nervous system corrupts every other read), node overload second, then skill invisibility and cooling failure, tension slack last.

**Options.**
- (a) The modes alone, with the dysfunctions mapped as symptoms. Commits Sŏn to teaching five coined terms to every lead and to a page that defines them plainly.
- (b) The dysfunctions alone, as the book has it. Commits Sŏn to a frame built for a team's interior (trust, conflict), which needs a survey or an offsite to read, and to leaving the white paper's reference unused.
- (c) Both: the modes read structure (house, domain, seat, period); the dysfunctions read the one true team's interior when 4.2.4 surveys it. Commits Sŏn to two vocabularies and a rule for which reads what.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Lencioni's model is widely used with a short team self-assessment and a facilitated discussion of the results; it reads the team's interior, not its structure. Google's internal study of its own teams (Project Aristotle) found psychological safety the strongest factor in team effectiveness, which is one reason the safety question is worth placing deliberately (4.5) rather than losing between frames.

## 3. Surveying the house

**Book.** Survey the group before a meeting or offsite; the leader, an HR partner, or an outside facilitator runs it; discuss results with the team; a good offsite session (p. 283).

**White paper.** Employee NPS "tracked regularly rather than annually"; a cultural labor score; a feedback channel whose loop is closed (WP p. 22); engagement measured across psychological safety and values alignment; exit interviews read for what could have been better (WP p. 23); "feedback runs both directions. Team members weigh in on management effectiveness, operational improvements, and culture" (WP p. 19). Silent on a one-off survey, and on who reads exit interviews.

**From the earlier work.** The book's survey was read as a slower, self-reported channel duplicating four faster ones (the pulse, the feedback channel, the check-in's upward question, the shift close). Under 2.3.3's test, a new mechanism has to buy something the existing ones do not. Against that: the pulse asks one question; none of the four asks the house whether it trusts itself or avoids conflict.

**Options.**
- (a) No separate survey; 4.2.3's frame is read from the continuous channels, and the pulse (2.3.15) gains a small set of questions if a dysfunction needs one. Commits Sŏn to the pulse carrying more than one question, and to 3.4.15's hiring questions sharing the instrument.
- (b) The book's survey, once the house exists, before the quarterly gathering (2.3.25) if held, results discussed with the whole house. Commits Sŏn to a facilitated conversation about the house's own dysfunctions, in front of the house, and to acting on what it hears.
- (c) The survey runs, results are read by the leads and the founders only, and the house hears the change, not the finding. Commits Sŏn to a transparency line (2.2.6) that keeps findings above the floor.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Short recurring pulses (a handful of questions, monthly or quarterly) have largely replaced the annual engagement survey in companies that measure this at all; Gallup's twelve-question survey is the best-known fixed set. Restaurants that survey staff mostly do it at exit, which is the white paper's lagging read.

## 4. The skill-will matrix

**Book.** Skill is the ability to do a task, will the motivation; read the team as a whole, then each member; low skill and low will is a combination a growing company cannot carry; low skill and high will is worth investing in with checkpoints; high skill and high will is the goal, then delegate; high skill and low will is the hardest, and the job is finding the source of the lost motivation: new, episodic, or persistent; something in the person's life; a need for a new role (pp. 284 to 286, returning at p. 391).

**White paper.** Every position has documented competency requirements visible to anyone; the next skill set unlocks after a set period; "no one's advancement waits on a manager's attention or judgment" (WP p. 11). Cross-strand fluency is rare and "the best outward-facing operators may be mediocre in the inward-facing role" (WP pp. 10 to 11), so a low-skill read across strands may be a strand mismatch, not a deficit. Silent on motivation as something a lead reads or records.

**From the earlier work.** The matrix opens at the person (against cons. 1's default order), asks for a will reading that is not one of the documented competencies, and produces a verdict the check-in refuses to make (2.3.9). It was declined as an instrument for placing people. Its team-level readings were kept as mechanism reads (cons. 2). The high-skill, low-will case, the book's hardest, was routed to coaching and hard feedback (5.1, 5.2) and to the performance chapters (5.8) rather than left without a home.

**Options.**
- (a) The matrix as the book has it: a lead places each person privately and uses it to decide what to delegate and where to invest. Commits Sŏn to leads holding written or remembered will readings, which the person page does not carry (2.3.11), and to reconciling that with "no surprises" (WP p. 19).
- (b) The matrix at the domain or house level only, read from records (readiness rows for skill; goal count and pulse for will); no person is placed. Commits Sŏn to sending every person-level motivation question through Chapter 5's conversations, where the person is in the room.
- (c) Not used; the modes and Chapter 5 carry the whole of it. Commits Sŏn to naming, in 5.1 and 5.8, where "find the source of the lost motivation" is done, so the book's hardest case does not fall between chunks.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. The matrix descends from situational leadership (Hersey and Blanchard), which was built to adjust a manager's style to a person's readiness, not to record a rating; most companies that use it do so in conversation, unrecorded.

## 5. Evidence: what a mode is read from

**Book.** Deadlines, metrics, whether the team works well together, how happy the team is with its work, the survey's signals (pp. 283 to 284).

**White paper.** Leading indicators are the nervous system's signal; lagging ones are the X-ray after the fracture (WP p. 12). Named leading indicators: employee NPS, cultural labor score, customer recognition rate (WP pp. 12, 22). The shift close captures incidents (WP p. 13). Tension slack is defined as compensation happening "without visibility" (WP p. 14), which means its evidence must be reconstructed from records kept for other purposes. Cooling failure's evidence includes joy and retention (WP p. 14); retention is also a cultural measure read over time (WP p. 23). Exit interviews are to be read, reader unnamed (WP p. 23). Customer relationships may follow a server to another property (WP p. 25), which sits beside "the knowledge does not leave" (WP p. 14): two accounts of what a departure costs.

**From the earlier work.** Every source in a line should be an openable record: a shift close, an incident capture, a check-in line, a schedule fact, a readiness row, an inventory row. Counts of services or reviews, never money and never a generated percentage. A lead's impression, a feeling, and one person's word were held out as sources. For the floor-management seat, nine load inputs were proposed (check-in count against hours worked, moved check-ins, deferred calls, mentoring load, incident patterns, review load, coaching load) feeding one reading per mode at the reset, with any fix a scheduling, rule, or procedure change and never a second manager-type seat under any title. Two added cooling sources: dropped brief items and displaced closing notes. A first-quarter baseline (incident-capture rate, process-update rate, pulse score, dropped-item count) to be set at the first house review.

**Options.**
- (a) Records only, counts only. Commits Sŏn to building each record before the mode it feeds can be read, and to marking "cannot read yet" honestly in the meantime.
- (b) Records first, with a lead's stated observation admitted as a source when marked as such. Commits Sŏn to a field that distinguishes observed from recorded, and to a rule on how much weight an observation carries at each threshold.
- (c) The book's mix: metrics plus the lead's judgment, unmarked. Commits Sŏn to the book's manager-as-instrument model, in tension with cons. 1's default.

**Counsel question.** Whether any of the counted sources (check-in lines, incident captures) become records with legal weight in a later dispute, and how that shapes what is written. Route with 0.1.

## 6. The evidence line and the person rule

**Book.** A shared diagnosis the team discusses and commits to (p. 283).

**White paper.** Post-mortems are private and process-focused; "the first question is not who failed" (WP p. 17). The fix for tension slack begins at adjacent nodes, not the person (WP p. 14).

**From the earlier work.** Seven fields: mode (one, never a blend), object (never a person), source, count, compensation (which adjacent seats or designations are absorbing the load), the named fix and the mechanism it touches, state (present, not present, cannot read yet with reason). No verdict, no money, identical at every rhythm. A later addition: a failure-type field (basic, complex, intelligent) on the process-update record, set by the reviewing lead, still naming no one.

**Options.**
- (a) The line never names a person. Commits Sŏn to carrying every person-level matter through Chapter 5's records, which sit beside the person page and not on it (2.3.11).
- (b) The line may name a person only under tension slack, after the domain fix, and only in the person's own check-in record. Commits Sŏn to a path with a stated gate.
- (c) The line may name the seat-holder when the mode is on a seat. Commits Sŏn to a record the person may read about themselves, which "no surprises" (WP p. 19) requires be heard first.

## 7. Who writes, and on what rhythm

**Book.** The leader, an HR partner, or an outside facilitator (p. 283); the diagnosis is a moment, ideally an offsite.

**White paper.** The two leads are parallel and accountable to different outputs (WP p. 10). Silent on who reads the house as a whole and on rhythm.

**From the earlier work.** Writers by seat: each domain lead for their domain; the floor-management lead for both floor strands and beverage (if 4.1.5 keeps beverage under it); central operations for system-side lines; one integrator for the house; kitchen lines pending the chef partner. Rhythms: the four already on the calendar (leads' review weekly, house review's scorecard, the reset as the correction point, gate reviews), adding no meeting. A first-quarter freeze (4.3.5): read everything, change almost nothing, log what would change for the first reset.

**Options.**
- (a) Attach to the four existing rhythms, no new meeting. Commits Sŏn to the leads' review and house review carrying more, and to the reset being the only place a fix is designed.
- (b) A standing diagnostic review, separate from the house review. Commits Sŏn to a rhythm that must pass 2.3.3's test and to one more closed hour per cycle.
- (c) The book's offsite, once or twice a year, as the place the house reads itself. Commits Sŏn to a whole-house gathering (2.3.25) with a diagnostic agenda, and to the cost of closing for it.

## 8. Thresholds and the change coupling

**Book.** Moving from dysfunctional to functional takes sustained attention; the manager models and holds the team accountable (pp. 283 to 284).

**White paper.** The process that allowed the failure gets updated so "the next server inherits a system that has already learned from it" (WP p. 13). Silent on thresholds.

**From the earlier work.** Thresholds generalized from three ad hoc rules: one occurrence, a note; the same signal on the same object across three consecutive leads' reviews, a partners' review entry; the same mode on the same object on two consecutive house reviews after a fix, a reset agenda item asking whether the fix or the mode was wrong; a designation held by one person every service for a quarter, a reset question (4.3.12); a mode present at a gate, the gate does not open around it. Every formal change carries its originating line verbatim as the reason, plus a scheduled read-back by the same source that raised the issue; a change whose stated reason is a person is refused. "Model the new behaviors" was read as node overload when done by a lead's presence alone; a versioned change plus a read-back was proposed instead.

**Options for the coupling.**
- (a) Every change entry (4.1.33) carries a line as its reason; a person as reason is refused. Commits Sŏn to Chapter 5 being the only door for person-driven change, and to leads learning to write the line before they propose the fix.
- (b) A line is the normal reason; a person may be the reason when Chapter 5's record supports it. Commits Sŏn to two reason types and a check that the Chapter 5 path was followed.
- (c) The book's posture: the lead owns the plan, models it, and holds the team to it; the record follows. Commits Sŏn to the lead's presence being the mechanism, which the white paper reads as a load on the center (WP p. 14).

## 9. The founders' own seats

**Book.** Operating Principle 4 and the opening of this section: a manager should always be able to say how the team is doing, and the diagnosis includes the manager's own contribution (pp. 282 to 283).

**White paper.** "The mark of these founders is a company built to run without them" (WP p. 06). CPU failure is expensive cognition throttled by the architecture, the example a general manager resolving scheduling conflicts (WP p. 13). Silent on who reads a founder's load.

**From the earlier work.** A load ledger and load line for each founder's seat, with a strict source list (never an impression, a feeling, or one lead's word) and refused fixes (never a chief of staff or assistant role). The reset reads founders' seats the same way it reads other seats, rotating who reads whom so no partner is another's sole reviewer. Flagged open: diagnosing a lead's own overload is "half-blind" until it is decided who receives a lead's own "what's in my way" (2.2.5 and 2.3.14 name the holder; 5.9 carries the rest).

**Options.**
- (a) Founders' seats are read by the same instrument, sources and fixes stated, the other partner writes the line. Commits Sŏn to each founder accepting a written load read from the other, and to 0.3's answer on whether the partners hold the basics for each other.
- (b) Founders' seats are read at the reset but only from the seat load reading's counts (4.1.36), with no fix list. Commits Sŏn to a reading without a remedy, which the reset must still act on.
- (c) Founders' load is a partners' matter (2.3.4), outside the diagnostic. Commits Sŏn to the two of them noticing it themselves, which is what the book warns a manager cannot reliably do.

This file states nothing about either founder's working style or values; those belong to 1.1 and to each founder's own document.

## 10. A lead taking over a running domain

**Book.** When you take over a new team, assess whether it has the structures, plan, and people to deliver (pp. 282 to 283); the bedrock is the people, so evaluate each person's skill and will (p. 294, in 4.4).

**White paper.** Knowledge lives in the system: documented processes, captured customer preferences, skill maps (WP p. 14). New people enter at the outside edge (WP p. 09); a lead enters where 4.1.8 says.

**From the earlier work.** An arriving lead reads the domain's structure (open seats, gaps, unresolved decision-rights items), its plan (goals, projects in flight), its evidence history, and the people only through their own written words; the first concrete read is arithmetic (does the check-in count fit the working schedule), the second is who holds which designations and how often; no survey, no placement, nothing written about anyone before the first scheduled check-in. 3.3.30 already decides how a change of lead is handled for a person; 3.3.45 and 3.3.47 hold the lead onboarding plan and the receiving-team note.

**Options.**
- (a) The read order above, as a section of the lead onboarding plan (3.3.45). Commits Sŏn to a domain's records being good enough to read a domain from, which is a test of the whole system.
- (b) The book's order: the new lead forms their own view of each person in the first weeks, then reads the records. Commits Sŏn to the lead's impression preceding the person's own words.

## 11. Where Sŏn may diverge from the book, in one place

- The instrument: the book's is the manager's judgment; the white paper's is the record (cons. 1, 5).
- The frame: dysfunctions of a team's interior versus modes of a structure (cons. 2).
- The moment: the book diagnoses at an offsite; Sŏn may diagnose on standing rhythms (cons. 7).
- The person: the book places each person on a matrix; Sŏn may never write a will reading (cons. 4, 6).
- The fix: the book's manager models and holds accountable; Sŏn may version the change and read it back (cons. 8).

Each is a divergence Sŏn may choose. None is required by the white paper except the order rule at WP p. 14, and even that is a default Brandon can amend.
