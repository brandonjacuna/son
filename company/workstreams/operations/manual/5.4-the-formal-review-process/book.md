# 5.4 The formal review process: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 5, pp. 399 to 412, with the chapter's appendix templates at pp. 468 to 471 and workbook pages. Full text: `sources/extraction/build/s13/book.md`, exercises at `sources/extraction/build/s13/exercises.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## Why a formal, written process

She argues against the trend of abolishing formal performance reviews in favor of continuous feedback alone. Continuous feedback in both directions is a hallmark of a strong manager relationship, but it should not replace a written document. Done right, a formal review summarizes ongoing feedback conversations and is a good place to outline development plans. If a company is growing quickly, not all managers will be delivering continuous feedback consistently; the written process catches those gaps (p. 399).

Alongside the written review, most companies need a mechanism to assign a performance designation and to submit people for promotion. Without one, this decision-making tends to happen informally and out of the employee's sight, which undermines trust when discovered. Assessment and recognition processes should instead be transparent and guarded against becoming political (p. 399). For people to trust the system, they need to know how they will be measured, that the measurement will be applied fairly, and how results lead to recognition and reward (pp. 399 to 400).

To build that trust, a company needs (p. 400):

- A talent strategy, which can be as simple as "pay for performance."
- Rubrics for how performance will be measured (she points to the job ladder discussion at p. 179).
- Agreement on how to assess results against those rubrics.
- A program to capture performance feedback and promotion nominations, written and numeric.
- A clearly communicated calibration process, so people trust that treatment is equalized across teams at the same level.
- A compensation philosophy and a way for performance and promotion to feed compensation outcomes.

She quotes Reid Hoffman on being "compassionately blunt" with people he hires and works with (p. 400 to 401). The manager's role is central: if they have managed well, the written review holds no surprises; it documents conversations already had. She warns against promising a specific reward, such as a promotion or raise, before the company process has concluded, since the outcome is not yet known; better to talk about it conceptually while continuing to coach (p. 401).

## Minimum viable people processes

Better tools now exist to run people processes without heavy custom build. Her Stripe story: at an early leadership offsite, she argued for a basic performance process rather than a bespoke one, on the logic that time was better spent on product priorities. Perfect can be the enemy of good: seeking a perfect process often produces no process, which is worse than something that is merely fine (pp. 401 to 402).

The three fundamentals of a strong formal review process: performance feedback, calibration, and compensation structures (p. 402).

## Performance feedback

Performance reviews are formal check-ins against the responsibilities in a role charter and the expectations laid out in the job ladder. They also help identify top talent as a company scales, once leaders can no longer personally track everyone's work (p. 402). She quotes chef Dominique Crenn on making people feel they are not just a number, and on a leader's need to listen, including to a director who tells the chef they are wrong (p. 402).

Reviews are most often completed annually, and typically include peer feedback, a self-assessment, and the manager's own assessment; if the person under review manages others, feedback from their direct reports too (pp. 402 to 403). She recommends managers hold review-style discussions with reports more often than the formal cycle: at least quarterly or biannually, so the formal process only formalizes feedback already exchanged. This is also a moment to revisit long-term career development (p. 403).

Core elements of the process (pp. 403 to 405):

- **Peer reviews.** The employee selects three to five peers, with the manager agreeing they are the right ones. Prompts cover how closely the peer works with the person, a strength tied to the job ladder with an example, one capability to improve with suggestions, and which operating principle the person most embodies.
- **Self-assessment.** Submitted at the same time as peer input. Prompts cover top contributions, a capability the person excels at with results achieved, and a development area with ideas for improvement and manager support.
- **Manager review.** Written after the manager reads the peer and self-assessment input. Prompts cover a summary of accomplishments, the one or two capabilities most used, at least one development area with improvement ideas, a promotion recommendation (yes or no) with rationale against the job ladder, and a proposed performance designation.

Her designation scale, from lowest to highest: does not meet expectations, partially meets expectations, successfully meets expectations, exceeds expectations, greatly exceeds expectations (pp. 404 to 405). A simpler three-category version (does not meet, meets, exceeds) is a valid alternative; the tradeoff is nuance for identifying top talent over time versus simplicity. Either way, she suggests a separate, more comprehensive talent review process for assessing potential, since backward-looking performance is only one input to talent identification (p. 405).

### Sidebar: a manager's advice for delivering reviews (pp. 405 to 407)

Adapted from a note by Hannah Pritchett, a former Stripe manager, sent to managers before a review cycle. Her structure: send the review 24 hours ahead of the conversation with a note asking the person to come prepared. In the meeting, spend about 10 minutes reviewing the feedback together, then about 40 minutes looking forward: whether the person feels set up for success, what new projects would build new skills, and how the manager can better support them. This structure avoids arguments about what is in the review; when someone pushes back, acknowledge the perspective but redirect to how the next time could go differently. The forward focus makes clear that a review exists to help the person succeed, not just to render a verdict.

## Calibration

Once formal reviews exist, a company needs a process for groups of managers to compare assessments across their reports, checking that people at the same level and similar contribution are rated consistently. This guards against one manager rating generously while another rates a comparably performing person more harshly. It should run on the same cadence as the performance process, usually annually, and can run twice a year if promotions happen off-cycle (pp. 407 to 408).

In a larger company, calibration draws on data analysis: average designations by group, promotion rates across demographic or work-location lines, with leaders and HR following up on anomalies. In a smaller company, it is more of a roll-up conversation: a leader, often with an HR partner, walks a set of managers through the designations and promotion recommendations for a related group of employees, then that senior tier steps back so the next tier discusses the level below (p. 408).

The calibration leader should review data ahead of time for outliers, understand the rubric, and be ready to push on irregularities. Over time, the meetings should shift from reviewing every person in detail to gut-checking what a given designation means at a level, using individual examples so managers calibrate to a shared bar (pp. 408 to 409). She names a common failure mode: calibration becoming a performative litany where managers justify designations to win approval, rather than an educational process aimed at shared definitions (p. 409).

Her steps for a calibration process (pp. 409 to 411):

1. Managers submit draft reviews, designations, and promotion nominations.
2. The drafts become a packet the calibration group can consult (appendix example at p. 413).
3. A senior leader prepares to facilitate, reviewing data ahead for red flags.
4. Convene managers of similar roles and levels, with a facilitator and HR partner present to track decisions and follow-ups.
5. Remind the group of likely biases (she names unconscious, availability, recency, and confirmation bias) and coach against them.
6. Examine which managers run high or low relative to the group and discuss whether their team is assessed consistently with others.
7. Discuss each level in turn, starting at entry level, agreeing what "does not meet" and "exceeds" look like for that level before moving up.
8. As discussion reaches non-manager individual contributors at a manager's own level, excuse the more junior managers from the room.
9. Note action items for gathering more data on any individual whose assessment sparked debate.
10. Do a final data check for broad parity, then report follow-ups back to the group.
11. Roll outcomes up to senior leadership to check alignment with an expected distribution and against compensation budgets, and to check for bias, such as fewer women promoted in a function, or gaps between remote and office-based assessments (pp. 410 to 411).

The output is usually a spreadsheet or updates to the HR tool recording designation and promotion changes. After calibration, managers finalize feedback in the tool, then typically have a two-week window to deliver reviews in conversations with their reports, before the review is visible to the employee in the tool (p. 411).

### Calibration roles

The manager's job is to understand the process, prepare, and participate, balancing advocacy for their people with objectivity, and to notice their own tendency to rate too harshly or too leniently (pp. 411 to 412).

She is specific that a division leader, not someone from HR, should run the calibration session, since the leader knows the people and the work. She recommends checking your own bias toward people or projects you know best, and guarding against a lowest-common-denominator outcome. Once a company is past about 30 people, performance should resemble a normal curve, and the hardest work is finding the lines between designations. Her test question for the middle of the curve: "Would you enthusiastically rehire this person for the role?" The leader's role throughout is to hold the bar and make sure top performers are truly recognized, not to let others in the room adjudicate what is the leader's own call (pp. 412 to 413).

## Exercises and templates

**Performance review template (pp. 468 to 471).** A structured outline for the written review, covering: name, level, start date, tenure in the review period, proposed designation and promotion recommendation, one-sentence rationale for not choosing a higher or lower designation, and a one-sentence summary of the intended takeaway. The impact section asks for two to three projects with context, duration, collaborators, expected versus actual impact, and metrics where possible, noting that company-building work such as mentorship, recruiting, and diversity, equity, and inclusion contributions should count. Strengths and development areas each ask for one to two items with a designation and supporting examples. The promotion section branches: if no, when the person might be ready and what they need to do; if yes, how long they have shown next-level behavior, examples, and the primary development area at the next level.
