# 5.4 The formal review process: considerations

What the white paper holds, what the earlier work argued, the options, and where Sŏn may diverge from the book. Numbered so `session.md` can point at a section. Nothing here is a decision.

## 1. What the review is for

**White paper.** The performance-management paragraph is the whole of what Sŏn has said (WP p. 19): systems "develop people rather than judge them"; reviews run "every three to six months and focus on development, growth, and the path forward rather than a verdict"; the governing rule is no surprises; feedback runs both directions, and team members "weigh in on management effectiveness, operational improvements, and culture." Advancement is stated separately: a person who is ready "schedules a competency conversation with the appropriate lead and is signed off" (WP p. 19), and "no one's advancement waits on a manager's attention or judgment" (WP p. 11). Pay is stated separately again, as a point system weighted by days worked (WP p. 20). The white paper never says "pay for performance," never names a rating, and never connects the review to advancement or pay.

**The book.** She wants a written review because continuous feedback does not reach everyone consistently, and because assessment and recognition made informally, out of the person's sight, destroy trust when discovered (p. 399). Her list of what a trusted system needs (p. 400) is the useful frame: a talent strategy, rubrics, an agreed way to assess against them, a program that captures feedback and promotion nominations, calibration, and a link to compensation. Each of her six items is either built here, built elsewhere at Sŏn, or deliberately not built; the session should be able to say which for each.

**The tension.** Her review is a formal check-in against a role charter and a ladder, and one of its jobs is to identify top talent once leaders cannot see everyone's work (p. 402). Sŏn has no ladder (4.1.7) and reads "further along" off the progression record (WP p. 11). So the question in 5.4.2 is whether the review keeps her second job at all, or whether the progression record and the ready-now pool do that work and the review is only the developmental moment the white paper describes. The earlier work took the second reading. It is an option, not a given.

## 2. Minimum viable process

She argues for a basic process over a bespoke one, because "perfect can be the enemy of good" and a perfect process often produces no process (pp. 401 to 402). This cuts both ways at Sŏn. The earlier work designed a review with a closed source list, a two-half record, an assembled form, a candor question, and a per-holder count. That is a more designed process than hers, not a less designed one. The session should hold the question of what the smallest review is that keeps the white paper's promises (development, no surprises, both directions) and can run in month six with the stack half-built. A printed page and a half-hour conversation may be that review. The rest can arrive at the reset.

## 3. Dimensions and rubric

**White paper.** Hiring assesses "specific competencies, cultural alignment, and growth potential" and asks "whether the building is equipped to create the environment this person needs to succeed" (WP p. 18). Reviews focus on "development, growth, and the path forward" (WP p. 19). Every position has "documented competency requirements visible to anyone" (WP p. 11). The two lists are never said to be the same list.

**Earlier work.** The hiring reads (3.1.5, 3.2.2) were built as four: competency for the seat, self-directed mastery, conduct under the non-negotiables and inside the discretionary range, and what the building supplies. The argument for reading exactly these at review is consistency: if hiring and review score different things, a person hired on one set is judged on another, and the review becomes the place a new expectation quietly enters. The earlier work also carried a rule that no team-environment behavior becomes a fifth dimension: speaking in meetings, a designation held, a recognition given or received, participation in a process. A proposed mapping of the hiring words to the review words (cultural alignment to conduct, growth potential to self-directed mastery, path forward to the career track re-read) was left for the founders to accept or reword.

**Options.**
(a) The four hiring reads, unchanged. Commits Sŏn to the same instrument end to end and to changing hiring whenever the review changes.
(b) The four reads plus one named addition, such as the person's own development goals from 2.2.14. Commits Sŏn to saying why review reads something hiring could not.
(c) The book's frame: the role charter's responsibilities and the seat's competency sheet, read as a list of expectations met or not. Commits Sŏn to writing charters at seat level, which 2.1.14 may not have done.

## 4. A designation, or states

**White paper.** "Rather than a verdict" (WP p. 19) and "no hierarchy of importance" as an operating principle (WP p. 20). Silent on any scale.

**The book.** Five designations, or three; the tradeoff is nuance for identifying top talent over time against simplicity (pp. 404 to 405). Past about thirty people she expects a normal curve and says the hard work is the lines between designations; her test for the middle is whether you would enthusiastically rehire the person (p. 412).

**Earlier work.** No designation. Each dimension is read as a dated state from a record: competency as the horizon held and the next unlock's date; mastery as the progression record's dated list; conduct as the discretionary-range count's own states (used, exceeded, unused, escalated) and whether a non-negotiable entry exists, with a breach never discussed at a review; what the building supplies scored on the building, item by item, feeding an infrastructure record rather than the person's page. The reasoning: a rating is a verdict regardless of label; the one dimension a lead could honestly grade (competency) is scored by an independent assessor (4.1.11), not the lead; and her reason for designations, differentiating reward by performance (p. 405), does not apply if no review output reaches pay. A risk the earlier work named: an under-used discretionary range should be read first as a question about the workplace, not a fact about the person.

**Options.**
(a) No designation; states only. Commits Sŏn to building the records the states are read from before the first review, and to carrying "top talent" through the progression record instead.
(b) The three-category scale, used only to identify top talent and never to feed pay. Commits Sŏn to a verdict word on the page and to explaining why it exists if it moves nothing.
(c) The book's five, feeding pay through 5.5. Commits Sŏn to calibration (section 8), to a curve once the house is large enough, and to rewriting the white paper's "rather than a verdict."

The finished-answer test for 5.4.4: whichever is chosen, Brandon can say what a person at the middle of the room hears at their review, in one sentence, without a word the review page forbids.

## 5. What the review reads, and what it never reads

**White paper.** "A formal review never contains new information" (WP p. 19). The rest of the source list is inferred from what the white paper builds: check-ins (WP p. 18), the progression record (WP p. 11), the shift close's captured incidents (WP p. 13), the feedback channel (WP p. 22).

**Earlier work.** A closed list of sources, all on the person's own readable page: every check-in line since the last review; the plan's close state at the first review; the career conversation's path and track; the progression state; the fluency record; the discretionary-range count and escalations; the person's own captures; whether a non-negotiable entry exists, as a bare fact; the state of any infrastructure item they raised; the status of channel items their lines generated. And a closed list of never-sources: the readiness form's rows or a "not yet"; any coaching record (5.1.6 decides whether one exists); counts of placed, extended, or moved check-ins (load readings, never a read of the person); failure-type counts; a recognition given, received, or declined; a diagnostic's evidence lines; a hiring scorecard, interview evidence, or the reference note in a lead's packet; a calibration record; a peer's or mentor's account; a work-style label; a lead's note written anywhere; the anonymous pulse; any lagging or pay figure. The reason the lists are closed: the assembling system pulls from named records with no free-text field, so no further source can quietly become an input later.

**A consideration the earlier work raised and did not close.** Whether the review may read a person's development goals (2.2.14) depends on whether those goals are the person's own choice from what the system unlocks or set by the lead. If set by the lead, reading them at review reintroduces the lead's judgment by another door.

**Options.** (a) The closed lists as drafted. (b) The closed lists with a rule that a holder may propose one added source per review, which the person may decline. (c) The book's sources: peer input, self-assessment, and the manager's own observations, with no closed list. Each commits Sŏn to a different answer on 5.4.11 and 5.4.12; the three decisions move together.

## 6. The person's half, peers, and the holder's part

**Self-assessment.** The book's prompts (pp. 403 to 404): top contributions, a capability excelled at with results, a development area with ideas and the support wanted. The earlier work translated them and moved the writing into the paid block, because nothing at Sŏn is owed off the clock (WP p. 19: costs are not shifted to employees). The white paper says nothing about homework. The option of writing ahead on paid time exists and would let the book's day-ahead delivery (p. 405) survive.

**Peer reviews.** The book has the person choose three to five peers with the manager's agreement (p. 403). The white paper gives peers a full mechanism elsewhere: recognition "in the moment," which "lands differently than management praise" (WP p. 19). The earlier work refused peer input to the review: a peer's account of a peer is an informal-network risk, positive peer feedback already has its own channel, and a peer's observation reaches the review only if the person put it in their own captures. 5.3.6 sets the bounds on peer-to-peer feedback and should be read with this. Options: the book's form; one named peer's example per dimension, chosen by the person; none.

**The holder's part.** The book's manager review carries a summary, capabilities used, a development area, a promotion recommendation, and a designation (pp. 404 to 405). The earlier work had the holder author nothing in their own voice and instead read the record aloud in dated, cited acts. That rests on 4.4.2 (what a lead may write about a person) and on the same refusal of free text about a person used elsewhere. It also answers the book's "you really have talent" moment (p. 384, cited in the digest) by making it the formal reading of specific acts rather than a pronouncement. Options: the book's sections; a short holder's note in the person's presence, read back before saving; nothing beyond confirming the assembled record.

## 7. No surprises as a mechanism

The white paper states the rule (WP p. 19) and the book states its manager-side version (p. 401). The design question is how to make it true structurally. The earlier work's three parts: pre-assembly with the holder adding nothing; an observation not on a record goes to the next regular check-in, once, after the person's own account, never to the review; and a closing read-back with one question, whether anything said was not already on a record the person could read, whose answer is a permanent mark. A "something new" answer becomes a count on the holder per period, never a read of the person and never on its own a reason to shorten the interval. Every entry cites the dated line it draws from; an uncited item cannot be entered. A strength not yet named is named at the next check-in, not saved for the review.

The book's alternative is procedural rather than structural: send the review a day ahead and, when someone pushes back, acknowledge and redirect to next time (pp. 405 to 407). The two are compatible if the record is the person's own page: there is nothing to send that they cannot already read.

What the session should weigh: the candor count is a read on leads, and 2.2.5 decides who holds a lead's review. A count that reaches a founder about a lead's candor is a supervisory instrument. It should be named as one, with 5.9 told it exists.

## 8. Calibration

**White paper.** No levels ("only reach," WP p. 9); no hierarchy of importance (WP p. 20); "the ready-now pool surfaces who is furthest along" (WP p. 14). Silent on calibration.

**The book.** Managers compare assessments across reports so the same level and contribution earn the same designation; a roll-up by level, run by a division leader with HR present, checking outliers, distribution, and bias, ending in a spreadsheet checked against compensation budgets (pp. 407 to 413). Her named failure mode: a performative litany where managers justify designations rather than converge on shared definitions (p. 409). Her insistence: the leader who knows the work runs it, not HR (p. 412).

**Earlier work.** No calibration as a committee, roll-up, outlier check, expected distribution, or budget check. With no designations, no levels, one assembled form, and no pay link, there is nothing to calibrate. Its functions are carried elsewhere: fairness across people in the same seat by the single assembled form; a lenient or harsh assessor by the independent assessor pool (4.1.11) and 3.2.39's calibration read on hiring; a bias check by the pay parity read (5.5.25) and 4.7.9's equity checks; a lenient or harsh holder by the candor count.

**What still needs a carrier if (a) is chosen.** Two of her functions have no obvious home in the earlier design: a cross-holder read of whether review records are being written to the same standard (a holder who writes thin person's halves, or whose reviews never generate an upward item), and the equity checks 4.7.18 wants to attach to "the calibration record." A light periodic read of a sample of review records at the reset, run by a founder and producing process acts only, is a middle option.

**Options.** (a) None, with each function's carrier named. (b) A sample read at the reset, founder-run, process acts only. (c) The book's roll-up, which requires designations (5.4.4c).

## 9. Potential

The book separates potential from performance and wants a more comprehensive talent review for it (p. 405). The white paper reads potential as documented, visible progress: the ready-now pool, internal succession, "the people who want it most" (WP pp. 11, 14, 19). 3.1.38 already builds a pool-first check before an outside leadership hire. The question for 5.4.15 is whether anything is read about a person beyond their own record when a seat opens, and if so, that it is never written to their page.

## 10. The block, the conversation, and the record

**The block.** The earlier work: paid, about half an hour to start, in a pre-service window with slack or the closed hour (2.3.12), never mid-service or at close, movable but never cancelled, in the person's language, no third person except an interpreter (4.8.5). The book's shape for the time inside: about ten minutes on the feedback, about forty on the future (p. 406). Those numbers assume an annual review with a written packet; a six-month review on a person's own page may need less.

**The conversation's order.** The earlier work fixed it: read the record aloud, confirm the states, hear the person's half, ask the upward question and repeat it back, close with the read-back and the "anything new" question. The book's advice on pushback, acknowledge and redirect to next time (p. 406), needs a Sŏn form: a disputed record entry goes to the correction path; a disagreement about what a line means goes to the next check-in.

**The record.** Two halves, on the person page, beside the check-in and plan records, with the three kept distinct (the check-in never writes a state, the plan never a running line, the review never a hypothesis or milestone). 2.2.6's transparency line decides who reads it; the earlier work's option there was the person and their lead only. Retention and discovery are counsel questions; 5.2.4 asks the parallel question for hard-conversation records.

**The upward half.** WP p. 19's three targets (management effectiveness, operational improvements, culture) were absorbed as the check-in's "what's in the way" question extended: the holder's own performance, the workplace's mechanisms, and what the building supplies, asked as "what would you change," repeated back without being explained away, written only if the person wants it. An item about a mechanism goes to the channel (4.8.11); an item about the building goes to the infrastructure line (3.3.28); a concern about the holder that cannot be said in the room goes to 5.3.4's destination. How a lead's own team's upward halves reach the lead's review, rolled up by mechanism rather than by name, is a design question 5.9 will also need.

## 11. The review in specific contexts

- **A first review.** About six months after the first paid hour on the earlier work's clock: roughly three months after the ninety-day plan closes and two months after the career conversation (4.4.3, 4.4.3). It reads the plan's close state and the baseline captured then, the career conversation's path and track, and everything since. The readiness form is not reopened.
- **Two strands or two periods.** Connections and their currency are read as facts; fluency is never scored, only whether its window is active; a lapsed window is communicated by the record, not announced by the holder.
- **A designation held often.** The review reads nothing about designations held; a designation held every service is a staffing question (4.3.13), not evidence about the holder.
- **A lead's own review.** Same dimensions, form, and refusals, against the lead's own seat description; the domain's upward feedback rolled up by mechanism. Who holds it is 2.2.5's and 2.3.14's.
- **The kitchen.** Same interval, block, dimensions, and record; the holder and placement against the prep window are the chef partner's (5.4.21).
- **The stack off.** A review can be held from a printed assembly (4.1.25).
- **Load.** Reviews held, moved, and extended are counted with check-in counts on the leads' review and answered by staffing or a leadership line (4.1.35), never by shortening or skipping.

## 12. Where Sŏn may diverge from the book

The earlier work diverged on nearly every form and kept every function: a formal moment on a rhythm that reads a period whole and looks forward; the person's own account read first; no surprises as a built mechanism; most of the time on the path forward; a two-way conversation; people knowing how they are measured and that it is applied fairly. Refused as forms: the designation and its scale; the promotion nomination; the manager-written narrative; peer reviews as input; the self-assessment as homework; calibration as a committee; the talent review on a rubric; the job ladder; the review season and delivery window; the review becoming visible in a tool after the conversation. Each refusal is presented in the sections above as one option among two or three. The white paper supports the direction ("rather than a verdict," no levels, no hierarchy of importance) without requiring any single form.

One divergence deserves its own weighing: the book treats the review, calibration, and compensation as three parts of one process (p. 402). If 5.4.5 keeps the review out of pay, Sŏn is splitting what the book fuses, and the trust the book builds through the link ("how results will lead to recognition and reward," p. 400) has to be built another way: by pay that is legible from records the person can read (5.5). If 5.4.5 lets the review feed pay, most of the earlier work's design has to be revisited, starting with 5.4.4.

## 13. How others have handled it

General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Companies that dropped annual ratings in the 2010s (several large technology and consulting firms did, publicly) mostly kept a written check-in on a rhythm and a separate, quieter mechanism for pay decisions; some later reintroduced a simple rating because managers had no shared language for pay. The lesson the book draws is the same: the decision does not disappear, it moves out of sight (p. 399).
- Her Stripe practice: a basic process bought rather than built, argued for at a leadership offsite on the logic that time was better spent elsewhere (pp. 401 to 402).
- Restaurants with formal reviews commonly tie them to a set schedule (after the first ninety days, then annually) and to a wage step; restaurants without them usually rely on the daily line-up and informal correction, which is what the white paper's "no surprises" rule is designed to make sufficient between reviews.
- Hannah Pritchett's structure for the conversation itself, ten minutes back and forty forward, is a widely copied shape for a developmental review (pp. 405 to 407).
