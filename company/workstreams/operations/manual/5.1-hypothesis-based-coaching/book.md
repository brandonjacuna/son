# 5.1 Hypothesis-based coaching: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 5, pp. 382 to 390. Full text: `sources/extraction/build/s12/book.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## Where coaching sits

Management is iterative and learned mostly through practice (p. 382). Managers tend to fall on a continuum between the "extreme coach," who over-involves themselves in day-to-day work with endless feedback, and the "forgot-to coach," who is clear about desired results but unclear on how to help a report get there. Most managers land somewhere in between, and many wait too long to offer coaching, often until a formal review, because coaching carries the risk of judging someone else's work (p. 382).

Her stance: "feedback is a gift," and a manager's attitude toward it should be one of service. You don't need to be an expert in everything a person does; your role is to help them be better by naming what they do well and how they can improve (p. 382).

Her Paul Bascobert story illustrates two kinds of coaching moments. One was blunt and not very useful ("Think harder," handed back on a draft). The other was formative: rather than praising her team's presentation, Paul asked her how she thought each team member experienced the meeting, drawing out that she had taken the best opportunities for herself instead of giving her team a chance to shine. The lesson was about leadership, not the content of the work (pp. 382 to 384).

She also draws on her own experience: no formal performance review for years early in her career, then a revelatory one at Google that, for the first time, named her strengths explicitly rather than only her development areas. Her conclusion: advocate for both regular informal coaching and formal performance reviews (pp. 384 to 385).

## Intuition as hypothesis

Her core reframe: intuition is not fluffy, it is a hypothesis. You observe a few examples of a phenomenon, sense a pattern, and then, like a scientist, test the theory and collect more data (p. 385).

Managers often hesitate to share hypotheses with reports because it feels like judging them, but good judgment is the job. It parallels business decision-making: form a strategy from an assessment of a need, then confirm it against data. The same logic applies to people: observe a need, hypothesize a root cause or a solution, then test it, sometimes directly with the person (p. 385).

She ties this to Operating Principle 2, "say the thing you think you cannot say." Sharing a hypothesis before you feel fully ready, in a way that the person experiences as ally-like rather than judgmental, is what makes hypothesis-based coaching work (pp. 385 to 386). It also lets a manager move faster: deductive rather than inductive, positing something and then testing it rather than waiting on a long accumulation of proof (p. 386).

Career conversations (Chapter 4) are a natural source of hypotheses; reports sometimes volunteer their own, as in her example of a report who named their own tendency to get pulled into "the thorniest and riskiest problems." That self-observation becomes something to file away and revisit when coaching (pp. 386 to 387).

## The three steps

Intuitive coaching, repeated as needed until reaching an insight worth sharing (p. 387):

1. **Gather data.** You usually have more than you think and need less than you think.
2. **Form a hypothesis.** Develop a sense of the person's strengths and weaknesses from what you've observed.
3. **Test your hypothesis.** Do this rigorously and with vulnerability.

### Gather data

Her Anika and Sonya example: a senior leader felt she only had three data points (feedback from three of Sonya's direct reports) and was uncomfortable acting on so little. Once she accounted for what she already knew, her own history working with those three people, the situation Sonya was operating in (newly managing managers of managers, during a global pandemic, with shifting responsibilities), she recognized she actually had ten or fifteen data points, enough to form a hypothesis (pp. 387 to 388).

### Form a hypothesis

Don't be afraid to use an early assessment as a starting hypothesis. In the Sonya example, the working hypothesis was that Sonya was operating as a "peacetime" leader (consultative, bringing people along) when her new context, a pandemic and a manager-of-managers role, called for a "wartime" leader (more directive, more precise) (pp. 388 to 389).

If the data collected is inconclusive or contradictory, spend more time gathering before forming a hypothesis. Over three to six months of practicing this, a manager can gauge whether their intuition is developing well (most hypotheses proving correct) or needs more data-gathering discipline (most proving wrong) (p. 389).

### Test your hypothesis

The best test is to share the hypothesis directly with the report, framed explicitly as a theory, not a fact: "I'm testing a hypothesis. Let me know whether it feels right to you." Present it as an observation about behavior and its impact, never as a judgment of character. Her contrast: "my guess is that you're a bad communicator" versus "I have a theory that you're not effectively getting important information across to the right stakeholders. For example... Did you sense that?" Follow with "what might you do differently? How can I help?" (pp. 389 to 390).

Testing is easier when a report has strong self-awareness; they can quickly weigh the observation against what they know of themselves. When self-perception is consistently misaligned with what the manager and others observe, that is a "self-awareness gap," which needs more data and detail to close, sometimes by observing the person more directly, such as joining their meetings (p. 390).

Her closing example: a talented person who was not promoted because a self-awareness gap around strategic thinking and relationship-building never closed despite repeated feedback; given a stretch opportunity to prove otherwise, the project failed, and the person left rather than acknowledge the pattern. Her point: the manager's job is to observe and offer opportunity; the report's job is to listen and decide to act. That can't be forced. A best practice drawn from this: before promoting someone, have them perform some of the role's duties first, to surface whether gaps exist (pp. 390 to 391).
