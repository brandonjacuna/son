# 5.1 Hypothesis-based coaching: working session

## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** How a lead at Sŏn coaches a person between reviews: what they read, how they turn it into a theory about the person's work, and how they say it. The book's method (pp. 382 to 391) is gather, hypothesize, test. Sŏn's version runs inside the check-in 2.3 built, and it decides how much of the book's freedom a lead has: whether they may keep notes, whether they may ask about life outside work, whether they may hear what peers say.

**Already decided upstream.** The check-in's form, length, holder, spine, and refusals (2.3.9); its intervals (2.3.10); what the person page holds and who writes it (2.3.11); what a lead may write about a person outside the page (4.4.2); whether the diagnostic ever names a person (4.2.7); what the career conversation produces (4.4.5); the mentor's boundary (3.3.14); the training module standard (3.3.18, 3.3.33). Read those `decisions.md` files first; several briefs below narrow inside them.

**Who else must agree.** Both seated founders on the coaching stance, the sources, the shape, the record rule, and the escalation rule. The chef partner on the kitchen's source list and the kitchen's module examples. Counsel on two questions (cons. 12).

**Why it sits here.** 5.1 is the first performance chunk. 5.2 (hard feedback) uses the shape decided here; 5.3 (feedback culture) uses the sources and the record rule; 5.4 (the formal review) can only be "nothing new" if 5.1 works; 5.8 (low performers) receives what 5.1.9 hands it.

## 3. Briefs

### 5.1.2 Decide whether coaching is a standing part of every check-in or offered when a lead has something to test

**The question:** does every check-in carry an observation from the lead, or does the check-in stand complete on the person's own questions until the lead has a theory worth testing?

**Why it matters now:** the founders hold the two leads' check-ins from the leads' first week (2.3.17). What the founders do in those minutes is what the leads will copy. And the honest description of working at Sŏn (2.1.19) should tell a candidate how they will be coached.

**Openers:**
- Think of the best manager you worked under. How often did they say something about your work that you had not already noticed? Weekly? Once a quarter, and it landed?
- The book's two failure ends: the coach who is in your work every day, and the one who tells you the target and never how to reach it (p. 382). Which one do you become under pressure?
- Sideways: a lead holds thirty check-ins a month. If each one must carry an observation, what does observation number twenty-eight sound like?
- The review is supposed to contain nothing new (WP p. 19). What is the smallest coaching habit that makes that true?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Per check-in, per period, or when it forms?
- If a lead has nothing to say, is the check-in shorter, or does the person's side fill it?
- What tells you at the reset that a lead has drifted to the forgot-to end?

**What the book says:** coaching is learned through practice; most managers wait too long, often until a review, because coaching risks judging someone's work; she advocates both regular informal coaching and formal reviews (pp. 382 to 385).

**White-paper default:** no surprises; if something is wrong or excellent the person knows before the review (WP p. 19, stated outright). No default on frequency.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most written 1:1 formats put the report's agenda first and the manager's observation second or optional; the book's own 1:1 (p. 136) works that way.

**Options:** (a) one observation every check-in; (b) when one has formed, the check-in complete without it; (c) a per-period expectation, strengths and gaps both, on the lead's timing. Depth: cons. 1.

**Watch for:** choosing (a) because it sounds rigorous, then watching leads invent observations; choosing (b) and never noticing a lead who has said nothing to someone in six months.

**A finished answer:** one of the three, the rule for a check-in with nothing to test, and a sentence for 2.1.19 that tells a candidate how they will be coached.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.1.3 Decide what a lead may read to form a hypothesis, and what is never a source

**The question:** which records, and whose words, may a lead draw on when forming a theory about someone's work, and which are off limits even when they would help?

**Why it matters now:** the shift close (2.3.8) and the person page (2.3.11) are being built; if they are the only memory coaching has, they must carry enough. And the mentor rule (3.3.14), the hiring record rule (3.2), and the diagnostic's naming rule (4.2.7) all lean on what this decides.

**Openers:**
- A server tells you, in passing, that the new runner keeps missing the sequence on the eight-top. Is that data about the runner, or a friction report about the sequence? What do you do with it before the runner's check-in?
- The book's Sonya example counts what the leader already knew: her history with three people, the situation Sonya was in, and comes to fifteen data points from three (pp. 387 to 388). Which of your fifteen would the person be able to read?
- Sideways: you worked a service beside someone and saw something no record caught. It is the one source they cannot read back. Do you say it, wait for a record to show it, or say it as impression only?
- The diagnostic (4.2) finds one role's load spilling onto its neighbors. When does that reach the person in the role, and in what words? The white paper calls that role "disengaged" (WP p. 14). Would you say that word to them?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Readable records only, or the book's breadth?
- Peers' words about the work, or about the person, or neither?
- May a lead work a service beside someone in order to observe, and is the person told?
- Does the kitchen's list match the floor's? (Pending the chef partner.)

**What the book says:** you have more data than you think and need less than you think; count your history with the people and the situation the person is in; observe more directly, such as joining their meetings, when self-perception and observation diverge (pp. 387 to 390).

**White-paper default:** the shift close captures incidents and the lead reviews them (WP p. 13); progression is documented with milestones, assessments, and feedback (WP p. 18); the review opens on what the person already knows (WP p. 19). No list, no exclusions.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Blameless post-mortem practice in software operations records what happened and what allowed it, without attributing cause to a person, and is widely taught; it is the model for a capture that is a source about the work and not about the person.

**Options:** (a) readable records plus the lead's own observation as the weakest tier; (b) the book's breadth, including peers and the mentor; (c) records plus peers' words about the work, never about the person. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** an exclusion list so strict that the lead's honest eyes are illegitimate; a source list that quietly makes the mentor a channel; the word "disengaged" arriving from the diagnostic into a conversation.

**A finished answer:** two written lists, the weight of the lead's own observation, the rule for the diagnostic hand-off in words a lead could say, and the observing-alongside rule, with the kitchen's list marked pending.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen's list.

### 5.1.4 Decide whether the principles are the standard a hypothesis references, and the test a principle must pass to be one

**The question:** when a lead says "I have a theory about X," may they add "and this is what the principle about Y asks of us," and which principles are concrete enough to be said that way?

**Why it matters now:** 2.1.5 is writing the principles. If some cannot be observed in one service, this is the moment to send them back, before orientation teaches them as the behavioral standard (WP p. 18).

**Openers:**
- Read the principles from 2.1.5 aloud. For each: could you point to one moment in one service where a person did or did not do it?
- A principle nothing references in feedback is a poster. Is that acceptable for some of them, the ones that are commitments the company makes rather than behaviors a person shows?
- Sideways: a person is told they "did not live the value of care." What do they do differently tomorrow? Now tell them "the table waited four minutes after the plates cleared before anyone returned." Which sentence is the principle for?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Reference expected, permitted, or never?
- Accept the deliverability test as written, or change it?
- Which principles fail, and do they go back to 2.1 as commitments or as rewrites?

**What the book says:** a hypothesis is an observation about behavior and its effect, never a judgment of character (pp. 389 to 390); principles are meant to be lived, not recited (Chapter 2).

**White-paper default:** orientation covers mission, values, and the behavioral standard before operational training (WP p. 18). Silent on principles as a feedback reference.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies that write behavioral values (Netflix's culture memo is the best-known) tend to phrase each as a behavior someone can be seen doing, which is the deliverability test in another form.

**Options:** (a) reference expected when one fits, test gates the list, 5.4 inherits it; (b) never in feedback; (c) strengths only. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** a principle that is really a company commitment being forced into feedback about a person; a lead using a principle as a trait word by another name.

**A finished answer:** the rule, the test accepted or changed, each principle sorted, and a note to 2.1 and 5.4.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.1.5 Decide the shape a hypothesis takes, what it may name, and what it may never be

**The question:** what does a hypothesis sound like at Sŏn, may it be about a strength, and may a lead ask why?

**Why it matters now:** 5.2's hard conversation and 5.4's review both use this shape. The module (5.1.15) teaches it with examples. And the motivation question touches 4.4.6's counsel question about what a lead may ask.

**Openers:**
- Say a hypothesis about someone you have managed, out loud, in one sentence. Now say it as the book would: a behavior in a moment, its effect, "did you sense that?" What changed?
- The book's counter-example is "you're a bad communicator" (p. 390). What are Sŏn's equivalents, the trait words a floor manager reaches for at midnight? Write them down; they become the never-say list.
- Sideways: her formative review named her strengths for the first time in years (pp. 384 to 385). When were you last told a strength with an example? Should a lead at Sŏn be expected to do that on some rhythm?
- Someone who was reliable for a year has missed the sequence three services running. The book would let you ask whether something outside work is going on (p. 286). Do you ask, or do you say "is anything in your way" and leave the door open?

**Narrowing questions:**
- One shape, or the book's looser form?
- Strengths on a rhythm, or as they arise?
- Motivation questions: allowed, allowed once, or refused?
- Skill-will placement: only if 4.2.5 keeps the matrix, and only in the lead's head?

**What the book says:** behavior and its effect, with an example, framed as a theory; never a character judgment; the manager's job is to name what someone does well as much as how they can improve (pp. 382, 389 to 390); motivation questions on p. 286.

**White-paper default:** develop rather than judge (WP p. 19). The white paper uses "disengaged" of a role (WP p. 14); silent on whether a lead may say it of a person.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. The Situation-Behavior-Impact form (Center for Creative Leadership) is the standard shape; most feedback training teaches it and warns against trait words in the same breath.

**Options:** (a) the single shape and the full refusal list, strengths included; (b) the book's shape with the motivation question allowed once, in the check-in's own "what's in your way" form; (c) (a) with the skill-will placement allowed privately if 4.2.5 keeps it. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** a refusal list so long that leads stop saying anything; "self-awareness gap" arriving as a label through the back door; the motivation question becoming a diagnosis.

**A finished answer:** the shape in one sentence, the strengths rule, the never-say list started, and the motivation question answered either way.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.1.6 Decide whether a hypothesis is written anywhere before it is tested, and where it lives until then

**The question:** may a lead write down a theory about a person before saying it to them, and if so where, for how long, and who may read it?

**Why it matters now:** 4.4.2 decides whether a lead keeps any written read at all; this narrows to the hypothesis. It also decides whether the book's three-to-six-month accuracy check (p. 389) is possible.

**Openers:**
- You have a theory about someone and their check-in is three weeks away. Where is it on day ten? In your head, in your phone, in a notebook?
- Sideways: imagine every note a manager wrote about you in your career printed and handed to you. Which ones would you have wanted to read at the time?
- A lead holding thirty check-ins cannot carry thirty theories for a month. If nothing is written, what gives: the interval, the roster, or the theories?
- The earlier work's principle: nothing written means nothing exists for any later process to cite. Is that protection worth more than a lead's memory?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Nowhere, a private note destroyed when said, or a note the person may read?
- How long may one be held untested?
- If nothing is written, how does a lead gauge their own accuracy at the reset?

**What the book says:** file it away and revisit; count how many proved right over three to six months (pp. 386, 389).

**White-paper default:** no surprises (WP p. 19); the person page holds nothing the person has not heard (2.3.11). Silent on private notes.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Many employers treat any manager note about an employee as a record that may be requested or discovered; counsel should say what Texas practice is (cons. 12).

**Options:** (a) nothing written; (b) a private note destroyed once said, with counsel's view on discoverability; (c) a note the person may read on request. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** choosing (a) and leaving leads to keep notes anyway, unmanaged; choosing (b) without asking counsel.

**A finished answer:** one of the three, the holding limit, the accuracy-check method, and the counsel question logged.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.1.7 Decide how a hypothesis is tested at the check-in: the words, how many per check-in, and what follows each outcome

**The question:** at what point in the fifteen minutes does the lead say their theory, in what words, and what happens when the person agrees, disagrees with thin records, or disagrees with thick ones?

**Why it matters now:** this is the content of the coaching insert (5.1.13) and the heart of the module. The founders will do it first with the leads (5.1.17).

**Openers:**
- Run a check-in in your head: the person's three questions, the upward question. Where does your theory go: before they speak, after, or only if it fits what they said?
- The book's words: "I'm testing a hypothesis. Let me know whether it feels right to you" (pp. 389 to 390). Say that in the pre-service window to a line cook. Does it survive? What is the Sŏn sentence?
- Sideways: the person says "no, that's not it," and you know the shift close shows the same thing three times. What do you do in the next thirty seconds?
- The person says "no," and honestly you have one thin data point. Do you drop it, or hold it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- After the person's answers, always?
- One hypothesis per check-in?
- The three outcomes as written, or different?
- Is the outcome recorded anywhere beyond the person's own line?

**What the book says:** share it framed as a theory, ask whether it feels right, then ask what they might do and how you can help; being wrong is data; common understanding can take a few rounds (pp. 386, 389 to 390, 394 to 395).

**White-paper default:** check-ins carry course correction (WP p. 18); the check-in's spine is 2.3.9's. No default on where the lead speaks.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Coaching models that ask the person's view first (the GROW model is the best known) share the book's reasoning that a change the person names is the one that sticks.

**Options:** (a) after the person's answers, one per check-in, outcome recorded only in the person's line; (b) the same with the lead's own note of the outcome, per 5.1.6. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** the theory arriving first and the person's three questions becoming a formality; a lead reading dated records at a person like a charge sheet.

**A finished answer:** the entry point, the framing sentence in the house's languages, the per-check-in limit, and the three outcomes with what the lead does in each.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.1.8 Decide whether a lead may bring a check-in forward to test a hypothesis, and how the count is read

**The question:** when a theory cannot wait for the interval, may a lead pull the check-in earlier, and what does it mean if a lead does that often?

**Why it matters now:** 5.2.7 (where hard feedback may be said) rests on whether there is a legitimate way to get to a private conversation sooner. The scheduling surface (3.3.32) needs to know whether to carry a second trigger.

**Openers:**
- Something happened on Friday. The check-in is in three weeks. What do you do on Saturday: say something in the walk-in, wait, or move the check-in?
- Sideways: a lead who has moved six check-ins forward this month. Is that a lead with six problems, or a seat with the wrong interval?
- If the request carried a reason field, who would read it, and what would it become?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Allowed at all? Who may place one?
- Once per interval, or counted without limit?
- No reason field?
- Read at the leads' review as a load reading?

**What the book says:** many managers wait too long to coach (p. 382); deductive coaching moves faster than waiting on proof (p. 386).

**White-paper default:** none; 2.3.10 holds the intervals as parameters.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most 1:1 conventions allow either party to ask for one sooner; few count it.

**Options:** (a) the holder may, once per interval, paid, no reason, counted per lead; (b) none; a brief heads-up in person, conversation at the scheduled time; (c) unlimited but counted. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** the early check-in becoming a summons the floor learns to dread; a reason field that turns into a record about the person.

**A finished answer:** allowed or not, the placement rule, the limit, the reason-field answer, the count's destination, all marked for the reset.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.1.9 Decide what a repeated, unacknowledged hypothesis becomes, and when it leaves coaching

**The question:** when the same theory has been said, the records have not changed, and the person does not recognize it, at what point is it no longer coaching, and what exactly is handed on?

**Why it matters now:** 5.8 (low performers) needs a defined entry, and 5.1.6's "nothing written" rule means whatever is handed on must be readable records or nothing.

**Openers:**
- You have said the same thing at three check-ins. The shift close still shows it. The person still says "that's not what happened." What is the fourth check-in?
- The book calls this a self-awareness gap and says it often produces a low performer over time (pp. 390, 395). Is "self-awareness gap" a phrase you want said in this building?
- Sideways: before counting the third repeat against the person, what would you want to be sure the house had done? The process fix? The training? The interval?
- What does 5.8 receive: the dated captures and the person's own lines, or a paragraph from the lead?

**Narrowing questions:**
- A fixed count, the lead's judgment, or the formal review as the only route?
- The process-fix check first, always?
- What is handed on, in whose words?

**What the book says:** more data and closer observation first; the manager observes and offers opportunity, the report listens and decides to act, and that cannot be forced (pp. 390 to 391).

**White-paper default:** identify the failure mode before intervening at the people level (WP p. 14); what allowed this, not who failed (WP p. 17).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Progressive-discipline policies in many US employers define escalation by counts of documented conversations; the book's approach is looser, and Sŏn's no-written-record leaning makes counts hard to keep. Counsel's view on what a later separation needs belongs to 5.2.4.

**Options:** (a) a count of consecutive unacknowledged raisings after the process-fix check, records handed to 5.8; (b) the lead's judgment, said to the person before anything moves; (c) the book's route through more data and the formal review. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** a count that becomes a countdown the person can feel; an escalation that carries the lead's narrative rather than the records.

**A finished answer:** the threshold, what is checked first, what is handed on and in whose words, and whether "self-awareness gap" is a house phrase.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.1.10 Decide whether a designation or a stretch assignment is used to test a hypothesis, and what a duty may never be used for

**The question:** may a lead hand someone the room or the door for a service to see how they carry it, and what may never follow from how it went?

**Why it matters now:** 4.1.19 set designations and a rotation floor; the white paper removed the manager's judgment from advancement (WP p. 11). This decides whether the book's "have them perform the duties first" has any place left.

**Openers:**
- The book's story: a talented person given a stretch project to prove a gap was closed; it failed; they left rather than see it (pp. 390 to 391). What did the manager owe that person before the project?
- Sideways: the door designation has rotated to someone you privately doubt. Do you let the rotation run, or step in? Which choice is the reading?
- If a person holds the room badly on Tuesday, what do they hear, when, and from what record?

**Narrowing questions:**
- May a lead assign a duty to test a theory? Is the person told?
- Feedback about a duty: that duty, that service, at their own check-in, citing the record?
- Never a reward, never a sanction, the count read only in aggregate?

**What the book says:** before promoting, have the person perform some of the role's duties (pp. 390 to 391).

**White-paper default:** designations are not permanent elevations (WP p. 10); advancement waits on no manager's attention or judgment (WP p. 11).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Rotating lead positions per service is common in restaurants; treating who holds them as a signal about the person is the load-bearing habit the white paper argues against.

**Options:** (a) no duty is ever given to test a person; the rotation runs, and feedback about a duty follows 86akhcz71's rule; (b) a person may ask for a duty to test themselves, and the lead may agree; (c) the book's practice, with the person told the reason. Depth: cons. 8.

**Watch for:** "she's not ready for the room" becoming the reason a rotation skips someone.

**A finished answer:** the rule for assigning, the rule for telling, and the feedback rule about a duty, with the rotation floor untouched.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.1.11 Decide how a person's own self-observations and the career conversation feed coaching

**The question:** when a person names their own tendency, in a career conversation or a check-in, may the lead keep it and raise it later, and does the person know that?

**Why it matters now:** 4.4 is designing the career conversation; the block that records it is built here (5.1.14). If what a person says there can come back as a hypothesis, they should be told before they say it.

**Openers:**
- Someone tells you in their career conversation that they get pulled into the hardest table every night and lose the section. The book files that away (pp. 386 to 387). Three weeks later you see it. Do you say "you told me this yourself"?
- Sideways: what would you have said differently in your own career conversations if you had known it would be quoted back?

**Narrowing questions:**
- May a lead draw on it? Is the source named?
- Where does a self-named tendency live: nowhere, the block in the person's words, or a check-in line?
- The career conversation is never where a hypothesis is tested?

**What the book says:** career conversations are a natural source of hypotheses; a report's self-observation is something to keep (pp. 386 to 387).

**White-paper default:** the person sits at the center of their own map (WP p. 11). No career conversation described.

**How others have handled it:** Nothing useful beyond the book.

**Options:** (a) the lead may draw on it and says so; the tendency lives only in the person's own words on the block; (b) the career conversation is sealed from coaching; (c) the lead may draw on it without naming the source. Depth: cons. 9.

**Watch for:** the career conversation becoming a place people stop being candid.

**A finished answer:** the three answers, and a line for 4.4.13's guide telling the person what may be drawn on.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.1.12 Decide who coaches each founder by hypothesis, and where

**The question:** who tests theories about Brandon's work, and about Dominic's, in what room, and how often?

**Why it matters now:** the founders will coach the leads before anyone coaches the founders. The book's own story is years without a review (pp. 384 to 385).

**Openers:**
- Who was the last person to tell you a theory about your work that you had not heard? How long ago?
- Sideways: the leads' upward question will produce theories about you. Who reads them, and where do they get said back to you?
- The partners' weekly review (2.3.4): is there a minute in it for "here is what I noticed about how you ran that"?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The other partner, the chef partner, an outside coach (3.3.23), the leads through the upward question, or a mix?
- Same shape and refusals as 5.1.5?

**What the book says:** the review that named her strengths was formative; managers who are never coached repeat their patterns (pp. 384 to 385).

**White-paper default:** none.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Cofounders commonly use an outside coach or a peer group for this; few make it a standing item between themselves.

**Options:** (a) the partners at the weekly review under the same rules; (b) an outside coach each; (c) the upward question read at the leads' review. Depth: cons. 10.

**Watch for:** this brief becoming about either founder's style or values; it is about the mechanism only.

**A finished answer:** who, where, how often, for each founder; the same shape and refusals or not.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
