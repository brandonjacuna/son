# 4.8 Team communication: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 4, pp. 360 to 362. Full text: `extraction/build/s11/book.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## The chapter's closing note

She frames the chapter's final section around a line from business school professor Sigal Barsade, quoted earlier in the chapter, on change management: "communicate, communicate, and by the way, communicate" (p. 360).

Her summary of what most of her team advice reduces to: set expectations well, share the same information with everyone, and build an environment for open exchange. Communication runs through everything a manager does, one on one, with the team, and up and down the division and company, so it's worth understanding how your company approaches internal communication so you can build your own layer on top of it consistently (pp. 360 to 361).

## Communication as trust-building

As covered in Chapter 2, internal communication is a mechanism for building trust, reinforced by sharing meeting notes and decisions and by continuing to communicate even when things are uncertain. As a team grows, it helps to map the formal and informal ways team members actually get information. The more a company or team scales, the more information-sharing has to be formalized, or people end up learning things at different times, which breeds a perception that politics and relationships, not process, are how things get done (p. 361).

## Pass-downs

Managers sit on more information than the average employee. Neglecting to route relevant pieces down to reports leaves them missing context they need to do their jobs. Her recommendation: a standing "pass-downs" agenda item in the team meeting, carrying information from company leadership, usually received from your own manager, either as new information or as added framing on something leaders already announced by email or at an all-hands (p. 361).

Judgment is still required on what to share and how to frame it. Her Google example: Sheryl Sandberg, in staff meetings, would share a piece of information and then say "I think the right way to think about this is..." to contextualize a leadership decision or action for the room (pp. 361 to 362).

## Two supporting practices

- **Team snippets.** A weekly document every team member fills in, so the team gets visibility into what everyone is doing and the status of important work without spending meeting time on status updates. For a larger team or division, she suggests a weekly or monthly cadence of shared updates that reinforce goals and priorities, celebrate wins, surface challenges, and invite ideas, used above all to keep the team connected to its leader; personal anecdotes and photos are welcome, not a distraction (p. 362).
- **A personal end-of-day habit.** She describes closing each day by thinking through what she needs to tell other people, using her old commute as reflection time to decide what her division needed to know and then sharing it, usually by chat or email. Her instruction is to find whatever communication method works for you and apply it consistently (p. 362).
