## 3. Briefs

### 4.6.2 Decide what "distributed" means at Sŏn, and which problem each team shape carries

**The question:** which of the book's three problems, coordination, cohesion, or participation, is the one each team shape at Sŏn will actually suffer from?

**Why it matters now:** the next three decisions each design against one problem. Naming the wrong one builds a fix for a problem Sŏn does not have (a team chat for a crew that dissolves at close) and leaves the real one (the kitchen never hearing the floor's brief) alone.

**Openers:**
- It is the second week of late night. Who on the late crew has ever met the morning crew? What do they know about each other, and how?
- Sideways: at Coqodaq, when something changed on Tuesday's service, how did Thursday's crew find out? Who was the person that told them, and what happened when that person was off?
- The book's Dublin engineer waited three days for an approval from San Francisco. Who is Sŏn's Dublin?

**Narrowing questions:**
- For a single service's crew: is the problem ever coordination, or is it participation (the person who cannot hear)?
- For two periods that never overlap: is this cohesion (they are not a team) or coordination (they hand a room to each other)?
- For the two leads across periods: do they need to feel like a team, or only to hand off cleanly?

**What the book says:** three challenges; Table 9 maps which dominates by shape: a few remote people on a co-located team face participation; a remote group under one manager faces cohesion; a team split across offices faces coordination; a fully remote company faces coordination and cohesion (pp. 332 to 337).

**White-paper default:** bus failure, information not moving between domains because it rides on relationships, is the named failure (WP p. 13); the house runs across the day, so the team never co-occurs by design (WP p. 33). No row for shifts.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Hospitals and hotels treat the shift handover as a coordination problem with a fixed written form (structured handoff notes at a nurses' station, a front-desk log), not as a cohesion problem; that is the closest analogue to a restaurant's shift-to-shift case.

**Options:** (a) one problem, one design (the record). (b) name the dominant problem per shape and design each (cons. 1). Depth: cons. 1.

**Watch for:** calling everything coordination because the record is already built; forgetting the founders and the chef partner as a shape.

**A finished answer:** a short table, shapes down the side, the dominant problem beside each, and Brandon can say which later decision answers each row.

**Needs agreement from:** no one; a framing decision.

### 4.6.3 Decide the founders' norms for working apart before the building exists

**The question:** how do two founders, and a chef partner who may be found in another city, work as one leadership team before any of them share a room every day?

**Why it matters now:** this is the only genuinely distributed relationship at Sŏn before opening, and the book's advice to over-document norms applies to it most. It is also the pattern any later remote seat inherits.

**Openers:**
- Think of the last decision you and Dominic made by text. Where does it live now? Could a third person find it?
- Sideways: the chef search may find someone who is not in Austin yet. On day one of that relationship, what do they need to know about how you two already work, and where would they read it?
- When you send Dominic a question at eleven at night, what do you expect back, and by when? Does he know that?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Where does a partners' decision live once made: the partners' page (2.3.6), the decision-rights register, a thread?
- What is settled in writing and what always waits for a call?
- What response time does each partner owe the other on a written question, and is it different during a service?

**What the book says:** document communication norms in more detail than you think you need; everyone on the same footing, as at Automattic where everyone dials in from a separate place even if some share a building (pp. 338 to 339); a team channel, side conversations documented, structured touchpoints (pp. 343 to 344).

**White-paper default:** the two founders own different domains and the chef partner is a founding seat (WP p. 03); silent on how they work day to day.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Distributed founding teams commonly keep one written decision log and a stated response norm; the book's own example is the norm of moving any work conversation to a place everyone can see (p. 335).

**Options:** (a) the partners' page carries the norms; nothing separate. (b) a working-apart page (4.6.17) that the chef partner and any remote seat later join. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** norms that describe how Brandon prefers to work rather than what the partners agree; a rule Dominic has not seen.

**A finished answer:** where decisions live, response times, written versus call, how the chef partner joins, and which page holds it; Dominic has agreed.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.6.4 Decide the test before any seat works away from the building

**The question:** may any seat at Sŏn work mostly away from the building, and if so, what has to be true first?

**Why it matters now:** the Operations Lead's job as the white paper describes it is largely reading systems, which can be done from anywhere, and the leadership hires are the next hires. A posting that is silent on this will be read by candidates either way.

**Openers:**
- The Operations Lead is at their kitchen table reading the labor forecast on a Tuesday. Is anything wrong with that picture? What about on a Saturday night?
- Sideways: Stripe once required remote employees to have remote managers, so the manager knew the experience from inside. Who at Sŏn would manage a remote seat, and have they ever been the remote one?
- The book's four factors are a mature operating system, the role, manager support, and the person's experience. Which of the four does Sŏn fail today?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Is the answer the same for the Operations Lead, the Maitre d, and a bookkeeping or build seat?
- If yes for anyone, what does the house owe them: a named contact every service, a cadence, in-person time?
- Is a chef partner during a search a remote seat, or a partner not yet arrived?

**What the book says:** weigh the operating system's maturity, the role's coordination load, manager support, and the person's experience level before adding a remote member (pp. 342 to 344).

**White-paper default:** the Operations Lead "oversees those systems and is accountable to their outputs, not their manual production" (WP p. 10); nothing places anyone remotely.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Multi-unit restaurant groups commonly run finance, purchasing, and people functions away from any one building; single-unit operators almost never do. Sŏn is a single unit built to become more.

**Options:** (a) no seat works mostly away. (b) the Operations Lead may, in part, once the stack runs; no floor or kitchen seat may. (c) case by case with the test written now. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** answering for the Operations Lead based on who Brandon imagines in the seat; a test that no one could pass before opening.

**A finished answer:** a yes or no per seat class, the test in four lines, and what the house owes anyone who passes it.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.6.5 Decide what a shift may decide without an absent owner, and how that decision reaches the owner and the next shift

**The question:** when the person who owns a decision is off, what may the shift do on its own, and how does what it did get back to the owner and forward to the next shift?

**Why it matters now:** this is the Dublin engineer's problem in a restaurant: a wait that lasts past the service is a customer let down or a delivery missed. The ranges (2.2.2) exist; the list of waits does not.

**Openers:**
- It is nine on a Sunday, the Maitre d is off, and a regular's table wants something outside the server's range. Who decides, and how does the Maitre d find out on Tuesday?
- Sideways: the book's engineer waited three days, twice, for the wrong access group. Name three things a Sŏn shift will wait on that have nothing to do with the customer: a login, a delivery cutoff, a swap.
- Which of those waits would you rather see decided wrong on shift than left undecided until the owner is back?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Which waits get a range, which get an on-shift decider (the room's designation holder?), and which are allowed to wait, listed?
- Does a decision made on shift exist in the close before the next brief, always?
- Does the owner hear of it through their record, or is someone expected to tell them?

**What the book says:** build self-service access to most systems and mechanisms that unblock people across time zones without breaking security (pp. 333 to 334); the moment a side conversation becomes a decision, it moves to where everyone can see it (p. 335).

**White-paper default:** the server resolves inside their range and the system captures the incident in the close; the lead reviews and the process is updated (WP p. 13). No list of waits.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Hotels give the night manager a written authority list precisely because the owners of most decisions are asleep; the list is short and the log is mandatory.

**Options:** (a) everything gets a range or a decider. (b) customer-facing waits get ranges, back-of-house waits get the designation holder. (c) some waits are allowed to wait, named. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** a range so wide it is a policy change; the kitchen's list decided without the chef partner.

**A finished answer:** the list of waits, each marked, and the sentence about the close and the owner's record; the kitchen's list marked pending.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders for the ranges; the chef partner for the kitchen's list.

### 4.6.6 Decide the rule for the person who is not in the room

**The question:** when someone misses the room, by arriving late, working another period, not sharing the brief's language, or being in the other domain, how does the same information reach them, and how do they answer back?

**Why it matters now:** the white paper calls the front line the most sensitive position. A person who cannot hear the brief is a sensor unplugged. This is also where 4.7's "every voice reaches someone" and 4.8's language list meet.

**Openers:**
- A runner arrives ten minutes into the brief because the bus was late. What do they know at first seating, and who told them?
- Sideways: at Automattic everyone dials in from a separate place, even people in the same building, so no one is the lone remote voice. What is the Sŏn version, where nobody is the one who missed it?
- Picture the kitchen during the floor's brief. What did the floor just decide that the pass will need to know at six thirty?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Design for the exception (catch them up) or for everyone (everyone reads the written brief)?
- Is a catch-up logged, and if so as a process signal only?
- How does the person answer back: the check-in's upward question, the channel, the round?

**What the book says:** obvious barriers are easy to design around; the subtle ones (missing the room's dynamic) do more damage; document what happens in every meeting; level the playing field rather than accommodate (pp. 335 to 339).

**White-paper default:** the front line is the most sensitive position (WP p. 09); language choice protects dignity at the interview (WP p. 17); nothing on the brief's reach. 2.3.7 carries a rule that the brief is spoken in every roster language.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Airlines brief every crew member from the same written sheet before every flight, regardless of who was in the room when it was written; the sheet, not the meeting, is the record.

**Options:** (a) design for the exception. (b) design for everyone: written first, spoken as a reading. (c) both, with a logged catch-up read as a process signal. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** a rule that quietly makes the bilingual person the channel; a log that becomes a mark on the late person.

**A finished answer:** one rule per way of missing the room, the everyone-or-exception choice stated, and the answer-back path named.

**Needs agreement from:** no one beyond Brandon; the kitchen's side is the chef partner's.

### 4.6.7 Decide the in-person cadence for people who never share a shift

**The question:** once the day opens, who never shares a shift, and which of them meet in person, how often, at what cost?

**Why it matters now:** 2.3.25 decides whether a quarterly whole-house gathering exists; this decides what it is for and who else needs one. The cost is closed service, which the white paper does not weigh.

**Openers:**
- Month four. The morning crew and the dinner crew have never been in the building at the same time. Does that matter? To whom?
- Sideways: the book's revenue leaders flew to San Francisco quarterly for two days. What is the honest Sŏn equivalent: a closed Monday, a paid hour before service, nothing?
- What did in-person time between you and Dominic produce this year that a call did not?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Is the whole-house gathering the cadence for crews, or is there none?
- Do the two leads and the chef partner need a cadence of their own beyond the leads' review, once they work different periods?
- What is the fallback when the gathering cannot be held: a written note, nothing?

**What the book says:** no substitute for in-person time on a cadence, for connection and for alignment; quarterly while forming, twice a year later; virtual half-days are the fallback, not the plan (pp. 339 to 341).

**White-paper default:** dinner runs alone for at least a quarter (WP p. 36), so at first everyone shares one period; silent on gatherings.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Restaurant groups commonly hold an all-staff on a closed day before a season or menu change, paid as a shift.

**Options:** (a) the gathering is the cadence, quarterly, paid. (b) none; cohesion runs through the record and cross-period fluency. (c) only at marked moments. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** deciding the gathering here when 2.3.25 owns it; a cadence set for the founders' comfort rather than the crews'.

**A finished answer:** the list of who never overlaps, a cadence or an explicit none for each, paid stated, the fallback named.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.6.8 Decide the rule for social gatherings and the after-shift drink

**The question:** does the house host social time, is it ever anything but voluntary, and what is the rule on drinking on the premises after close?

**Why it matters now:** the after-shift drink is where much of the industry's real information moves, and it excludes the person who does not drink, has a second job, or has a child at home. Whatever the house does, it should be decided rather than inherited.

**Openers:**
- The last restaurant you worked in: where did the real conversation happen after close, and who was never there?
- Sideways: the book's Stripe country leads varied in after-hours socializing by culture and were left room to. What is the Sŏn floor's after-hours culture going to be if nobody decides?
- A new hire does not drink. How do they find out what everyone knows?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Does the house host anything after close, and who pays?
- What is never read into non-attendance, and where is that written?
- Alcohol on premises after close: none, a shift drink within a limit, the chef partner's call for the kitchen?

**What the book says:** leave room for local variation in practices like after-hours socializing; participation in a voluntary gathering is the person's choice (p. 342).

**White-paper default:** "dignity is a precondition for presence" (WP p. 20); silent on staff socializing and alcohol.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Many operators have moved the shift drink off premises or ended it after liability and harassment concerns; others keep it with a stated limit. Both are common.

**Options:** (a) nothing hosted, nothing said. (b) occasional hosted gatherings, voluntary, with the non-attendance rule written. (c) a standing alcohol rule decided separately. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** a rule that exists on paper while leads model the opposite; deciding the kitchen's after-close without the chef partner.

**A finished answer:** hosted or not, paid or not, the non-attendance sentence, the alcohol rule, and where it is written.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen.

### 4.6.9 Decide whether the leads hold a working-cultures conversation, and its boundary

**The question:** does the leadership team ever talk, once, about the working cultures the team brings from the kitchens and floors people trained in, and how is that kept from typing anyone?

**Why it matters now:** the interview-language promise means a multilingual team, and in practice people trained under different norms of directness and hierarchy. A conversation can produce a norm the house states; done badly it scores people.

**Openers:**
- A cook trained in a brigade kitchen and a server trained at a place with no titles are on the same service. What do they each think the other is doing wrong?
- Sideways: Stripe found it was not a mirror of American culture because its founders are Irish. What culture is Sŏn a mirror of, and does the floor know?
- Have you ever seen a "cultural dimensions" exercise applied to a person rather than a market? What happened?

**Narrowing questions:**
- No conversation, one conversation, or one per new lead?
- What may it produce: a norm in 4.5.9, a line in the correction rule 4.5.8, nothing written?
- The boundary: cultures people trained in, never a person's placement; nothing recorded about an individual.

**What the book says:** review the cultural dimensions of the business as a team and discuss implications for day-to-day work; Hofstede as a map (pp. 341 to 342).

**White-paper default:** the interview language is the candidate's choice (WP p. 17); silent on cultural difference.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Nothing verifiable at restaurant scale beyond the book's own Stripe example.

**Options:** (a) none. (b) once, boundary first. (c) each time a lead joins. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** the conversation becoming a read of a named person; a norm stated as "how we do it here" that is really one founder's training.

**A finished answer:** yes or no, who is in it, what it may produce, and the boundary in one sentence.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.6.10 Decide how a domain names what it needs from another when goals are set, and how a slipped dependency is raised

**The question:** when the floor's goal cannot be met without the kitchen doing something, or the reverse, where is that written before the goal is final, and where is a slip raised?

**Why it matters now:** the leads are parallel and neither reports to the other; a dependency nobody named becomes a missed goal both sides explain away. 4.1.37 already put an interface read line on the leads' review; this decides what feeds it.

**Openers:**
- The floor sets a pace goal for dinner. Whose fire order does it depend on? When did the kitchen hear about it?
- Sideways: at the pass, dependencies settle in seconds. Which dependencies do not settle at the pass and drag on for a quarter?
- What did "I told the kitchen" mean the last time it failed?

**Narrowing questions:**
- A dependency line on each goal page, a standing interface list, a leads' check-in, an embedded person, or a temporary working group under 4.1.29?
- Named before the other domain's goal is final: always?
- From a slip to the cross-domain hold (2.2.3): how many repeats, and who calls it?

**What the book says:** identify dependencies during planning, align before the other team's plan is final, a check-in that starts as an email, embed a representative, a working group with a DRI for substantial dependencies, escalate constructively; both the hair-trigger escalator and the leader who stays stuck fail (pp. 347 to 349).

**White-paper default:** the two leads are parallel (WP p. 10); the customer feels only that everything arrived right (WP p. 11); bus failure is the named risk (WP p. 13). No form for a dependency.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Kitchens and floors that work well usually have an explicit list of what each owes the other before service (the 86 list, allergies by station, fire timing); the slower goal-level dependencies are rarely written anywhere, which is the gap the book names.

**Options:** (a) documented interfaces on a cadence. (b) a dependency line per goal page only. (c) a leads' check-in. (d) an embedded person each way. Depth: cons. 8.

**Watch for:** a list that goes stale silently; a form so heavy the leads stop using it by month two.

**A finished answer:** the form, the "before final" rule, the place a slip is read, and the path to the hold; the kitchen's side marked pending.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen's side.

### 4.6.11 Decide when a recurring dependency is removed rather than managed, and who may raise it

**The question:** how many times does the same dependency slip before it stops being a thing to manage and becomes a structure question?

**Why it matters now:** the book's closing point is that structure sometimes removes a dependency entirely. Without a count, the leads manage the same slip forever; with one, the reset (2.3.30) or 4.1.32 hears it.

**Openers:**
- Name a dependency you have seen every restaurant manage weekly for years that a different structure would have removed.
- Sideways: the white paper removed a reservations manager and a lead host into one seat because software absorbed the handoff. Which floor-kitchen handoff might a structure or the stack absorb the same way?

**Narrowing questions:**
- A count at the reset, any-time raising at the leads' review, or both?
- Who decides the structural answer: 4.1.32's reexamination, the reset, the partners?
- The record names the structure, never a person: kept?

**What the book says:** ask whether a different strategy, architecture, org structure, or set of priorities could remove the dependency (pp. 349 to 350).

**White-paper default:** identify the failure mode before intervening at the people level (WP p. 14); silent on dependencies.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Nothing useful beyond the book.

**Options:** (a) a count at the reset. (b) either lead any time. (c) both. Depth: cons. 8.

**Watch for:** a count so high nothing ever qualifies; a structure change made for one slip.

**A finished answer:** the count, who may raise it, where it is decided, and the naming rule.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.6.12 Decide the course-correction sequence when a unit misses its goals

**The question:** when a unit misses its committed goal service after service, what does the lead do, in what order, and does the unit ever talk about it as a group?

**Why it matters now:** the brief refuses anything about performance (4.5.13), so the book's "open team conversation" has no natural room at Sŏn. Without a sequence, a lead improvises, and improvised versions of this conversation are how a unit learns it is being blamed.

**Openers:**
- Dinner has missed its pace goal for three weeks. The servers have a theory, the kitchen has a different one, and you have a third. Where does that conversation happen, and who is in the room?
- Sideways: the white paper's failure-mode reference says to identify the mode before touching the people. Walk the three-week miss through the five modes. Which one is it, and how would you know?
- Have you ever been in a "why are we behind" meeting as a line employee? What did you hear?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The trigger: how many services or weeks?
- The read first, then check-ins with a hypothesis about the work; then a unit conversation always, never, or only for some branches?
- If a unit conversation runs, where: a paid block? Who facilitates?
- The four questions: kept as written, or in Sŏn's words?

**What the book says:** test a hypothesis in 1:1s aimed at the work; then an open, curious team conversation: why are we behind, in or out of our control, what now with owners, how much do external reasons weigh (pp. 345 to 346).

**White-paper default:** blame the process, fix the process; post-mortems are private and process-focused (WP p. 17); the failure-mode reference comes before any people-level intervention (WP p. 14). No unit conversation described.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Well-run kitchens debrief a bad service the same night, briefly and about the sequence; the slower "we have missed for a month" conversation is rare in restaurants and usually happens as a manager's decision rather than a unit's.

**Options:** (a) the book whole, with a paid block. (b) the read and check-ins, no group conversation. (c) the read first, a unit conversation only for the collaboration and whole-unit branches. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** the conversation drifting into the brief; the lead running it before the read; the kitchen's version decided for the chef partner.

**A finished answer:** the count, the sequence in order, the room or the explicit "no group conversation," the questions, and where the outcome lands.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen.

### 4.6.13 Decide the goal-reset rule

**The question:** who may change a committed goal mid-cycle when the goal was wrong, and what does the house hear?

**Why it matters now:** a goal that was wrong and is quietly kept teaches the unit that goals are decoration; a goal reset without a record teaches that goals move when leadership feels like it. 2.2.17 set the goal form; nobody has said who may reset one.

**Openers:**
- The pace target turns out to have been set from a room that had not been built yet. Do you change it in week two, or live with it to the quarter?
- Sideways: the white paper says a settled-looking answer to an open question is the tell of a deck that has not been pressure-tested. What is the operating-system version of that tell?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Partners only, the lead with a partner, or wait for the reset unless unsafe?
- The record: original, change, reason, date. Anything else?
- What the house hears, through which channels, and what it says about whose error it was (the goal's, not a person's).

**What the book says:** adjust the goal as a team, document the original, the change, and the reason, be transparent internally about the reset and what you learned (p. 346).

**White-paper default:** open items named rather than hidden (WP p. 22); silent on resetting goals.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies that run quarterly goals commonly allow a mid-quarter change only with a written reason visible to everyone who saw the original.

**Options:** (a) partners only. (b) the lead with a partner at the leads' review. (c) wait for the reset unless unsafe. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** a reset that is really a miss relabelled; a reset the unit hears about from the number changing.

**A finished answer:** who, the record, who is told and how, and the sentence that a reset is never a person's failure.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.6.14 Decide how a collaboration problem between two people is handled, and whether a move is a lever

**The question:** when two people cannot work a service together, and it is not a disagreement about a decision, what happens, in what order, and may one of them be moved?

**Why it matters now:** 4.5.10 handles a disagreement about a decision. This is different: a server and a runner, a cook and an expo, who make each other worse every night. In a house that runs across the day, a move is easy to disguise as scheduling, which is exactly why it needs a rule.

**Openers:**
- Two good people, one station, every Friday a disaster. What have you seen managers do? What did the rest of the floor conclude?
- Sideways: the book says meet each separately, then together, each sharing nonjudgmental feedback on work style. Have you ever seen the "together" part go well? What made it work?
- If you move one of them to late night, how does the floor read it, and how does the person?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The difference from 4.5.10, in one sentence.
- Separately then together: who holds each, what is written, what is never written?
- Is the style vocabulary (1.1.4) used in the joint conversation?
- A change of period or station as a lever: allowed? The order that keeps it from being punishment (the person's own check-in first; the record names the structure).
- When does it become 5.2's feedback or 5.8's performance matter?

**What the book says:** separate conversations, then a joint one; nonjudgmental feedback on work style; stay neutral; if nothing improves, consider moving one or both (pp. 346 to 347).

**White-paper default:** the team holds through tension between elements (WP pp. 08 to 09); management absorbs blame from staff (WP p. 17). No move described.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Restaurant managers commonly solve this by scheduling the two apart and saying nothing; the book's point is that saying nothing is the failure.

**Options:** (a) the book's sequence, move allowed with the order rule. (b) the sequence, no move; it becomes 5.2's after the joint conversation. (c) the move first, conversation after (the industry default, named so it can be declined). Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** the lead's own conflict with one of the two; a move recorded as "scheduling."

**A finished answer:** the distinction, the sequence with holders, the vocabulary question, the move rule with its order, and the handoff to 5.2 or 5.8.

**Needs agreement from:** no one beyond Brandon; the kitchen's version is the chef partner's.

### 4.6.15 Decide how friction across a whole unit is read, who the neutral party is, and whether the style vocabulary returns

**The question:** when a whole unit is at odds, often with its lead, who is neutral enough to interview it, what do they ask, and does the style vocabulary come back for a group?

**Why it matters now:** the book's neutral party is HR. Sŏn has none, by design. The answer has to exist before the first unit needs it, because that is the worst time to invent one.

**Openers:**
- The dinner floor is quietly against its Maitre d. Who could sit with six servers one at a time and be believed?
- Sideways: the book calls it a 360 for a team. Who is neutral about the leadership team itself? Is anyone inside the house?
- The book's answer to most friction is mutual self-awareness. Do you want the working-style words from 1.1 in a room of twelve?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Neutral party for a floor unit, for the kitchen, for the leadership team: named by role.
- What the interviews ask; what they never record (nothing evaluative about a named person, 4.2.7).
- Who reads the summary; does a session with the unit follow?
- Style exercises with a whole unit: yes, no, leads only?

**What the book says:** a neutral party interviews team members like a 360, writes a summary, holds a session on what everyone will do differently; most friction traces to a lack of mutual self-awareness, so return to the assessments (p. 347).

**White-paper default:** no HR seat; the back-office administrator is removed by software (WP p. 22). Silent on neutrality.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Small companies without HR commonly use an outside facilitator for exactly this, once or twice a year at most; the cost is the point, it keeps the tool rare.

**Options:** (a) the other lead or a founder outside the domain. (b) an outside person, for every unit including leadership. (c) the other lead for floor and kitchen, an outsider for leadership. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** "neutral" meaning the founder who hired the lead; the summary becoming a document about the lead.

**A finished answer:** the neutral party by unit, the questions' scope and refusals, the reader, the session rule, and the vocabulary answer.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 4.6.16 Decide the threshold that makes a live risk the whole team's business, and what leadership says when it has no plan yet

**The question:** which risks on the live-risk line reach the floor, in what order and words, and what may leadership say when it does not yet know the fix?

**Why it matters now:** the line exists (4.1.30, 4.1.31). Staff will hear about a lease, a permit, a partner, or a slow month faster than any statement; the choice is whether they hear it from leadership first. This also feeds 4.8.20, the statement form.

**Openers:**
- The landlord sends a letter. Nothing changes tonight's service. Does the floor hear, and when: now, when it changes something, never?
- Sideways: the book's allowed script is, in substance, "we do not have a plan yet, it may take a while, and I am working on it." Say that to a room of servers in your voice. What do you add?
- Reid Hoffman's team ran an eight-week test to find out if the threat was real before reacting. What is the Sŏn version of "test it first" for a bad month?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The threshold: changes what someone does on a service; affects everyone's hours, pay timing, or safety; or lower?
- Written first, then the brief: always?
- Who speaks, in whose words?
- Does the statement restate why the work matters and name the next small step, or is that performed conviction?
- Is "test before reacting" the owner's first move on the line?

**What the book says:** be transparent to a point, reiterate the vision, move forward; the failure is pretending to have it figured out; Stripe communicated each decision methodically with a daily page (pp. 350 to 352); test the threat before reacting (pp. 352 to 354).

**White-paper default:** for the brand and the table, honesty about what happened, the real impact, and the fix (WP p. 31); never assert the unproven (WP p. 36). Nothing on what staff are told.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. The book's Stripe account is the reference; operators in 2020 who told staff weekly what they knew, including "we do not know," are widely reported to have kept more of their teams, a pattern rather than a study.

**Options:** (a) the old work's threshold. (b) a lower one: any risk on the line for a set number of days is named. (c) the threshold plus a standing "what leadership is working on" line in the house note (4.8.8). Depth: cons. 9.

**Watch for:** a statement that promises a fix leadership does not have; a threshold set to spare leadership hard questions; anything with a figure in it.

**A finished answer:** the threshold in a sentence, the order, the speaker, the allowed script in Sŏn's words, the vision-and-next-step rule kept or declined, and the test-first line.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
