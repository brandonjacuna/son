# 4.6 Team-building complexities: considerations

Topics follow the book's order (pp. 331 to 353, exercises pp. 364 to 377). "WP" is the Sŏn investor white paper, September 2026. "Book" is *Scaling People*. Where the white paper is silent, it says so.

## 1. What "distributed" means at Sŏn

**What the white paper holds.** The house runs across the day under one name (WP p. 33), dinner runs alone for at least a quarter, and the morning and late night follow once dinner is steady (WP p. 36). So the whole team shares one period at first and then stops overlapping. The white paper names the failure directly: information that "cannot move between domains because the communication infrastructure is verbal handoffs, tribal knowledge, and informal relationships between individuals. When the relationship breaks, the information stops. This is not a culture problem. It is a systems problem" (WP p. 13). Its remedy is the shift brief and shift close as the two ends of a service (WP pp. 14, 26) and the system as the place knowledge lives (WP pp. 07 to 08). It never calls this a distributed-team problem, and it is silent on where the founders and the chef partner work from before the building exists.

**From the digest.** The old work's framing holds up: the trigger for a deliberate coordination design is non-co-presence by shift-hour, not headcount. The book's Table 9 has no row for this. The closest row is "a team split across two or three offices," where coordination dominates (Book pp. 336 to 337). Two other rows also apply: the two leads once more than one period is open behave like "a whole team that is remote but reports to one manager without being a real team," a cohesion problem; and a kitchen person during a floor brief, or a person who does not share the brief's language, is the book's "few remote people on an otherwise centralized team," a participation problem.

**Options for 4.6.2.**
- (a) Treat every team shape as a coordination problem and solve it once, with the written record (2.2.11, 4.8.6). Commits Sŏn to one design and accepts that cohesion and participation get less attention.
- (b) Name the dominant problem per shape, as Table 9 does, and design against each. Commits Sŏn to three small designs (4.6.5 for coordination, 4.6.7 for cohesion, 4.6.6 for participation) and to a read of each at the reset.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book's distributed team is a set of people who all persist week to week. A shift crew forms at the brief and dissolves at close. The book's remedies that assume persistence (a team Slack channel, recorded meetings) translate only partly; the ones that assume a record (strong notes, a norm that decisions move to writing) translate whole.

## 2. The founders and any seat away from the building

**What the white paper holds.** Brandon owns the concept, the operating systems, and the experience; Dominic owns the capital, the financial architecture, and the technology build; the chef partner is a founding seat (WP p. 03). The Operations Lead "oversees those systems and is accountable to their outputs, not their manual production" (WP p. 10), work that can be done away from the floor. Nothing in the white paper places anyone remotely, and nothing describes a chef search that runs in another city (3.1.19 decides how the chef partner is found).

**From the digest.** The one genuinely distributed relationship before opening is the founders themselves, with a chef partner possibly found elsewhere. The book's advice to "document communication norms in more detail than you think you need to" (Book p. 338) applies most directly to them.

**Considerations.** The book's four factors before adding a remote member (Book pp. 342 to 344) read well for Sŏn: the maturity of the operating system is exactly what chunks 2.2 and 2.3 are building; the role matters (an Operations Lead reading the stack is a different case from a Maitre d whose whole job is presence); manager support is the founders' own; experience level counts. Stripe once required remote employees to have remote managers, on the theory that the manager then knows the experience from inside; it now trains any manager to do it well (Book p. 343). The book's counsel to try being remote yourself for a stretch (Book p. 345) is something the founders are already doing by circumstance.

**Options for 4.6.4.**
- (a) No seat works mostly away from the building. Commits Sŏn to hiring both leads locally and to the Operations Lead being present even for desk work.
- (b) The Operations Lead may work partly away once the stack is running; no floor or kitchen seat may. Commits Sŏn to a documented cadence and a named contact on every service for the absent seat, and to the book's four-factor test.
- (c) Decide case by case with the test. Commits Sŏn to writing the test now.

**Where Sŏn may diverge.** The book's remote employee is the exception in an office. At Sŏn the "office" barely exists; the two founders may be the only people who ever do desk work, and their norms are the whole of this topic until opening.

## 3. Coordination across shifts: the absent owner and the side conversation

**What the white paper holds.** The server "resolves it using their authorized judgment and generosity range, the system captures the incident in the shift close, the appropriate lead reviews, the process that allowed the failure gets updated, and the next server inherits a system that has already learned from it" (WP p. 13). Every server carries a bottle to gift and managers carry a spirit (WP p. 26). The ranges themselves are 2.2.2's and 2.2.4's. The white paper does not list what a shift waits on when its owner is off.

**From the digest.** The Dublin engineer lost three days, twice, waiting for a US-hours approval to the wrong access group; the lesson is a self-service interface rather than an approval queue (Book pp. 333 to 334). The book's rule that the moment a side conversation becomes a work decision it moves to a format everyone can see (Book p. 335), and its caution that chat tools have retention limits so a permanent record lives elsewhere (Book p. 336), both carry forward. 4.8.6 holds the record rule; 4.6.5 holds what a shift may decide.

**Considerations.** The restaurant version of the Dublin case is concrete: a lead is off, and a server needs a comp beyond the range, a swap on tomorrow's schedule, a supply order before a delivery cutoff, a reservation exception, or a login that has stopped working. Each either has an on-shift decider, a self-service range, or a wait. A wait that lasts past the next service is the book's failure. The white paper's decision-rights logic (authority lives in the domain, WP p. 07) points toward ranges; the operating question is which waits are worth the risk of a range and which are not.

**Options for 4.6.5.**
- (a) Every wait on the list gets a range or an on-shift decider; nothing waits past the service. Commits Sŏn to writing ranges for things it may prefer to hold (purchases, schedule), and to trusting the record to catch misuse.
- (b) Customer-facing waits get ranges; back-of-house waits (orders, schedule, access) get a named on-shift decider, usually the room's designation holder. Commits Sŏn to the designation holder carrying decisions outside their domain and to 4.1.19's rotation floor meaning something.
- (c) Some waits are allowed to wait, listed explicitly, with the next brief as the deadline. Commits Sŏn to saying which, so a person is never left guessing.

## 4. The person who is not in the room

**What the white paper holds.** The front line is "the most sensitive position ... They feel the customer, the room, and the cultural temperature of the building before anyone else" (WP p. 09). The pre-shift briefing is as important as the signature dish (WP p. 20). Language choice protects dignity in the interview (WP p. 17); the white paper does not extend that to the brief or the team home, though 2.3.7 carries a rule that the brief is spoken in every roster language and 2.2.15 puts the team home in every house language.

**From the digest.** The book's remote employee who cannot hear the debate and stays silent (Book p. 338) maps to a floor equivalent: a runner, someone whose English is a third language, someone who missed the brief. The book's specific fixes (video links, acoustics) do not transfer; its principle does: no one's ability to be heard should depend on being in the room. Automattic's answer, that everyone dials in from a separate location so no one is the lone remote voice (Book p. 339), is the sharpest version of "design for everyone, not the exception."

**Options for 4.6.6.**
- (a) Design for the exception: the person who missed the brief is caught up by the lead or a peer. Commits Sŏn to a verbal handoff, the failure the white paper names (WP p. 13).
- (b) Design for everyone: the brief is written before it is spoken, everyone reads it, and the spoken brief is the reading aloud. The person who missed it reads the same thing. Commits Sŏn to a lead writing before every service and to the record being readable on a phone in every listed language (4.8.2).
- (c) Both, with a log: the written brief for everyone plus a marked catch-up for anyone who arrived late, logged so the reach review (4.8.15) can see who is repeatedly missing it. Commits Sŏn to the log and to reading it as a process signal, never as a mark on a person.

## 5. In-person time and cohesion

**What the white paper holds.** Dinner runs alone for at least a quarter (WP p. 36). Orientation to the mission and values comes first for every person (WP p. 18). The white paper is silent on staff gatherings, and it treats closed service as a cost without weighing it. 2.3.25 decides whether there is a quarterly whole-house gathering; 4.5.4 and 4.5.21 decide the leadership block; 4.5.24 decides the gathering's team content.

**From the digest and the book.** The book has "not found a substitute for quality in-person time on a regular cadence" and names two payoffs: spontaneous social connection, and the alignment a 45-minute meeting cannot produce (Book pp. 339 to 340). Its cadence is quarterly while forming, then twice a year; a virtual half-day is the fallback, not the plan (Book pp. 340 to 341). At Sŏn the people who never share a shift once the day opens are the crews of different periods, and the leads and chef partner across periods. The leads' review (2.3.13) already meets weekly; the question is the crews.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Restaurant groups that run more than one service commonly hold an all-staff meeting on a closed day or before a menu change, paid as a shift; the practice is widespread enough to be a baseline rather than an innovation.

**Options for 4.6.7.**
- (a) The whole-house gathering (2.3.25) is the in-person cadence for crews who never overlap, quarterly, paid. Commits Sŏn to a closed service or shared window each quarter.
- (b) No whole-house gathering; cohesion across periods runs through the record, the leads, and cross-period fluency (4.1.10). Commits Sŏn to accepting that two crews may never meet, and to reading whether that shows in the pulse.
- (c) A gathering only at the moments the house marks (4.5.23): a period opening, the anniversary. Commits Sŏn to an irregular cadence and to naming which moments count.

## 6. Going global, read for one building

**What the white paper holds.** Nothing on national culture. The interview-language promise (WP p. 17) is the only clue that the team will be multilingual, and by implication multicultural in the working sense: people trained in different kitchens and floors carry different norms on directness, hierarchy, and time.

**From the digest.** The book's team exercise on cultural dimensions (Book pp. 341 to 342) was flagged as a real risk if applied to people rather than markets: scoring an individual on a dimension is reading them by a demographic proxy. Its durable point: participation in a voluntary social or business-adjacent gathering is the person's choice, never a condition of anything (Book p. 342).

**Considerations.** Two things the book folds together should be split. First, the after-hours question is sharper in restaurants than in offices: the after-shift drink is where much of the industry's real information moves, and it excludes people who do not drink, have a second job, or have children at home. Second, a working-cultures conversation among the leads can be useful without ever typing a person, if its object is the cultures people trained in and its output is a norm the house states (the meeting norms, 4.5.9, or the correction rule, 4.5.8).

**Options for 4.6.8.**
- (a) The house hosts nothing after close; social time is the team's own. Commits Sŏn to the after-shift drink happening anyway, off premises, and to saying nothing about it.
- (b) The house hosts occasional gatherings, paid or unpaid stated in advance, voluntary, with a written rule that non-attendance is never read; drinking on premises after close is decided separately. Commits Sŏn to the rule and to leads modelling it.
- (c) A standing rule on alcohol among staff on premises (none; a shift drink within a limit; the chef partner's call for the kitchen). Commits Sŏn to enforcing it evenly, which is harder than writing it.

**Options for 4.6.9.** (a) No conversation; the norms pages cover it. (b) One conversation among the leads and founders, once, with the boundary written first. (c) A conversation each time a new lead joins. Each commits Sŏn to keeping the conversation about cultures, never about a person's placement on a scale.

## 7. Underperforming teams

**What the white paper holds.** "Blame the process, fix the process" (WP p. 17): the first question is what allowed this to happen, not who failed; post-mortems are private and process-focused. The failure-mode reference is to be used "as a diagnostic against any operational problem. Before intervening at the people level, identify which failure mode is actually present" (WP p. 14). Goals, their form, and targets are 2.2.17, 2.2.18, and 2.2.21; the diagnostic is 4.2. The white paper never describes a conversation with a unit about a missed goal, and it never says who may reset a goal.

**From the digest.** The book's sequence: probe the root cause in 1:1s with a hypothesis aimed at the work; then an open, curious team conversation around four questions (why are we behind; in or out of our control; what now, with owners; how much do the external reasons weigh) (Book pp. 345 to 346). Then three branches: the goal was wrong (reset it, document it, be transparent, Book p. 346); a skills or collaboration problem (separately, then together; consider moving one or both, Book pp. 346 to 347); systemic friction (a neutral party interviews the unit like a 360 for a team, a summary, a session, and a return to style assessments, Book p. 347). The digest flagged that an open team conversation framed as "why are we behind" can read as public judgment, and that a transfer can function as quiet punishment.

**Considerations.**
- The brief cannot hold this conversation: 4.5.13 refuses anything about performance there. So a unit conversation, if any, needs a paid block, which is a real cost, or it does not happen as a group and the lead's read plus check-ins carry it.
- The white paper's failure-mode reference is a better first step than the book's, because it forces a structural read (tension slack, node overload, sequence corruption, skill invisibility, cooling failure, WP p. 14) before anyone asks a person anything.
- Sŏn has no HR seat (WP p. 22 removes the back-office administrator). The neutral party for a floor unit is the other lead, a founder from outside the domain, or someone outside the house. For the leadership team itself, only an outsider or the partners are left, and the partners are not neutral.
- The book's "return to your personality and work style assessments" (Book p. 347) collides with the caution in 1.1.4 about the vocabulary spreading beyond the founders. Whether it comes back for a whole unit is 4.6.15's.
- Moving a person between periods or stations is a real lever in a house that runs across the day, and it is easier to disguise as scheduling than in an office. The protection is order: the person's own check-in first, then the record, and the record naming the structure.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. The book's own examples are the source here; hospitals' use of short checklists to prevent errors (Book p. 358, cited in the next chunk) is the nearest analogue to a fixed sequence a lead follows rather than improvises.

**Options for 4.6.12.**
- (a) The book's sequence whole, with the unit conversation in a paid block. Commits Sŏn to the block and to the lead facilitating a hard conversation well (4.5.9's norms).
- (b) The read and the check-ins, no group conversation; the outcome lands as owned actions on the leads' review, and the unit hears the actions through the brief as facts. Commits Sŏn to the unit never being asked as a group what it thinks, which loses the book's "usually more in our control than people think."
- (c) The read first; a unit conversation only when the read points at collaboration or the whole unit, never when it points at the goal or the system. Commits Sŏn to the lead judging which branch before talking to the group.

**Options for 4.6.13.** (a) Only the partners reset a committed goal. (b) The unit's lead resets with a partner's agreement, at the leads' review. (c) Any reset waits for the reset mechanism (2.3.30) unless the goal is unsafe. Each commits Sŏn to the record (original, change, reason, date) and to telling the house through 2.2.11.

## 8. Working with other teams

**What the white paper holds.** The Operations Lead and the Maitre d "are parallel. Neither reports to the other" (WP p. 10). The kitchen is the chef partner's during service (WP p. 10). The outward and inward strands serve one table and "the customer never feels [the inward] strand, they feel that everything arrived right" (WP p. 11). Bus failure is information not moving between domains (WP p. 13). The white paper does not say how a dependency is named or recorded. 2.2.3 holds the cross-domain decision and hold; 4.1.34 holds the joint half-page; 4.1.37 puts an interface read line on the leads' review.

**From the digest.** The book's guide: identify dependencies during planning (what from whom by when), align before the other team's plan is final, a semi-regular check-in that starts as an email, embed a representative each way, a temporary working group with a DRI when the dependency is substantial, escalate constructively to the shared decision-maker (Book pp. 348 to 349). Its closing point: sometimes a different structure removes the dependency rather than managing it forever (Book pp. 349 to 350). The digest's two options carry forward below.

**Considerations.** In a restaurant the dependencies are dense and fast: the 86 list before the brief, allergies by station, fire timing against a table's pace, the pass call. Most of them are settled within a service by the people at the pass, not at a planning meeting. What the book adds is the slower layer: a domain's quarterly goal that cannot be met without another domain doing something (a floor goal on pace that needs the kitchen to change fire order; a cost goal that needs the floor to change how comps are recorded). Those are the dependencies that need naming before the goal is final. The hair-trigger escalator and the lead who lets their unit stay stuck both fail (Book p. 349); the path from a slip to the hold (2.2.3) should be short and used rarely.

**Options for 4.6.10.**
- (a) Documented interfaces: a standing list between each pair of domains, reviewed on a fixed cadence at the leads' review. Commits Sŏn to keeping it current or watching it go stale silently.
- (b) A dependency line on each goal page (2.2.27), named before the other domain's goal is final, and nothing else standing. Commits Sŏn to dependencies existing only where a goal exists.
- (c) A lightweight recurring check-in between the two leads, in addition to or instead of the written form. Commits Sŏn to another meeting, or to the leads' review carrying it.
- (d) An embedded person: a floor lead at the kitchen's brief and the reverse. Commits Sŏn to two people spending part of every service in the other domain's room, which the white paper's lean leadership count (WP p. 22) may not afford.

**Options for 4.6.11.** (a) A count of repeats at the reset turns a dependency into a structure question under 4.1.32. (b) Either lead may raise it any time at the leads' review. (c) Both. Each commits Sŏn to the record naming the structure, not a person.

## 9. Managing through uncertainty

**What the white paper holds.** For the brand and the table: "the response is honesty about what happened, the real impact, and the fix. That is not damage control, it is relationship maintenance" (WP p. 31). The stack is deterministic and nothing load-bearing rides on AI (WP p. 21). Open items are named rather than hidden, because "a settled-looking answer to an open question is the tell of a deck that has not been pressure-tested" (WP p. 22). The white paper does not say what staff are told about a business risk, or when. 4.1.30 decided how a live risk is run (a founder owner, a closing condition, a daily state, no special powers) and left how the wider team hears of it to 2.2.11; 4.1.31 wrote the line's form; 2.2.12's policy carries the crisis rule; 1.2.2 decided what the team hears before and after a decision.

**From the digest.** The book's three moves: be transparent to a point, reiterate the vision, move forward (Book pp. 350 to 351). Its allowed script, in substance: there is no detailed plan yet, it may take a while, and it is being worked on (Book p. 351). Stripe communicated each Covid decision methodically and kept a daily resource page (Book p. 352). The Hoffman sidebar: name the threat, run a bounded test, get a written answer before reacting further; "we needed to acknowledge that we didn't have all the answers, and we needed to have a plan to start getting them" (Book pp. 352 to 354). The old work's rule for what reaches the floor: a brief line only if it changes what staff do that night, or a written statement to everyone if it affects everyone, in the what-happened, real-impact, fix form; otherwise it stays with leadership, and nothing is said before it is written.

**Considerations.** The book's failure mode is the manager who pretends to have it figured out. At a restaurant the more common failure is the other one: staff learn of a risk through rumor (a lease, a permit, a partner leaving, a slow month) before leadership says anything, and silence gets filled. The threshold matters because not every business risk changes a service; a threshold too low makes every bad week the floor's worry, a threshold too high makes leadership look like it hides things. The white paper's discipline of never asserting the unproven (WP p. 36) cuts both ways here: leadership should neither promise a fix it does not have nor deny a risk it does.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. The book's Stripe account (a daily-updated internal page during Covid, two stated priorities, each decision communicated as made) is the reference pattern; hospitality operators during 2020 who told staff what they knew weekly, including "we do not know yet," are widely reported to have retained more of their teams than those who went quiet, though that is a pattern, not a study.

**Options for 4.6.16.**
- (a) The old work's threshold: the floor hears a risk only if it changes what someone does on a service, or affects everyone's hours, pay timing, or safety. Commits Sŏn to most risks staying on the live-risk line.
- (b) A lower threshold: any risk on the line for more than a set number of days is named to the house, in the allowed script, whether or not it changes a service. Commits Sŏn to more statements and to leads fielding questions they cannot answer.
- (c) The threshold plus a standing line in the house note (4.8.8), "what leadership is working on," so the house is never surprised that risks exist. Commits Sŏn to writing that line honestly every issue.

## 10. The chapter's exercises

**Career conversations (Book pp. 364 to 367).** The white paper makes the path explicit and self-paced and calls the system the advocate (WP p. 11); reviews focus on the path forward (WP p. 19); "how people grow beyond the building" is the longest culture measure (WP p. 23). It never puts a person's life history in any conversation, and the check-in (2.3.9) refuses a verdict and a pay conversation but says nothing about a career conversation. The book's version asks about childhood, schooling, and family choices; a general policy against asking about origins or family (a counsel question named in 4.7) cuts against parts of it. Options for 4.4.3: (a) run it as the book has it, voluntary, for leads first; (b) a shortened form that starts at the person's first job, not childhood; (c) not at all, with the ninety-day plan's check-ins (3.3.11) and development goals (2.2.14) carrying the forward-looking half. Each commits Sŏn to the conversation being the person's, nothing written without their agreement.

**Offsite planning (Book pp. 367 to 370).** Served by 4.5.4 and 4.5.21 for the leadership block; this chunk turns the checklist into a kit (4.6.21).

**Leadership snippets (Book pp. 370 to 372).** The lead's weekly note (2.3.19) is Sŏn's version for leads; whether every person writes a weekly line is 4.8.8's.

**The unblocking process (Book pp. 372 to 377).** Served by 2.2.3, 4.1.34, 4.5.10, and 4.5.18. Nothing remains here except 4.6.14's distinction between a disagreement about a decision and two people who cannot work a service together.

## 11. Where Sŏn diverges from the book, in one place

- The book's distributed team persists; Sŏn's shift crew dissolves at close. Remedies that assume persistence translate partly; remedies that assume a record translate whole.
- The book has HR as the neutral party; Sŏn has none, by design (WP p. 22).
- The book's unit conversation has a natural room; Sŏn's brief refuses it (4.5.13), so it needs a paid block or does not happen as a group.
- The book's escalation goes up a chain; Sŏn's goes across, to the partner whose domain holds the disputed outcome (2.2.3).
- The book's crisis is company-scale; Sŏn's most likely crises are a person, a permit, a lease, a slow month, and the floor hears rumors faster than any all-hands.
