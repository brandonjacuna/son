# 6.3 Consider your career: working session

## 1. Orientation

This is the last chunk of the manual and the book's last section (pp. 498 to 503): a founder's own path, and the founders' standing in a house that reads everyone else. Half of it is personal and stays that way: where the path leads, the document a founder keeps, the rhythm of stepping back. The other half is the house's, because the founders have no manager, no board, and no one to tap them on the shoulder: what reads a founder, what absorbs a founder's failure, what the house does when a founder leaves, and what the founders owe the team's path in return. The session ends with a read of every founder-facing rule the manual now holds against the book's closing list.

Working rules for the room: Brandon decides, the facilitator never does. Options and defaults are starting points. Nothing he says about where his path leads, what he is good at, what he enjoys, or what he is paid gets recorded; his career document (6.3.11) is his and never enters this repo or ClickUp. If he starts answering the book's six questions aloud, note that the exercise has begun and stop. Brain dumps go to `notes/YYYY-MM-DD-<topic>.md` in his words before they are funneled, and a brain dump about his own path is saved only if he asks.

Upstream, already decided or in progress: the working-with-me document and its revision rule (1.1.2, 1.1.7); the self-exercises (1.1.6); the knowledge-transfer list (1.1.12); the operating agreement's note (2.1.1); how the partners decide and the status quo (2.1.13); whether a founder's domain failure gets the post-mortem (1.2.3); the departure read and the rule that a founder's departure runs on the same records (4.3.8, 4.3.10, 4.3.17); the handover list (4.4.18); the review holder "for each founder, if any" and the upward half (5.4.7, 5.4.13); the founders' place in the pool (5.5.7); the reset load line (4.2.12); the inclusiveness observation of the founders' seats (4.7.12); the reset and the annual block (2.3.30); the seat inventory (6.1.5, 6.1.17); the read between resets (6.1.4); the concern-about-a-founder path (6.2.9).

## 3. Briefs

### 6.3.2 Decide whether a founder's path at Sŏn is defined structurally, and what the house reads of it

**The question:** in a house built to run without its founders, is a founder's path something the house can describe without describing the founder, and if so, how much of it does the house read?

**Why it matters now:** the handover list (4.4.18) dates the functions passing from the founders to the leads. Read one way it is delegation; read another it is the founder's seat emptying, which is the only account of a founder's path the house could hold without asking. Whether to read it that way is a decision, and it decides whether 6.1.4's overload read and a stalled path are ever the same finding.

**Openers:**
- The white paper describes your career as a shape: into the best version of a segment, then out, carrying the piece worth keeping (WP p. 05). The book says envision the shape, not the title (p. 500). Is Sŏn a segment in that sense, or the end of the series? You do not have to answer here; notice whether you want to.
- Sideways: the book's Chutes and Ladders (p. 499). Which square is a founder on when every by-default load has been handed over?
- If Dominic's seat emptied on schedule and yours did not, what would the house see? Should it see anything?
- The earlier work said a founder stuck holding loads past their dates is a founder whose path has stopped. Is that true, or is it the house reading something it has no business reading?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Structural (the hand-over sequence, and only that); entirely private; or a line of direction spoken between partners at the annual block, unrecorded?
- Confirm: where the path leads is never a house record.
- The convergence with 6.1.4: named, or declined?

**What the book says:** you are your own career coach; careers make sense in hindsight; every five years envision the shape; check ambition against impact; never adopt someone else's path (pp. 498 to 502).

**White-paper default:** the shape of the career as described (WP p. 05); built to run without them (WP p. 06); gated on proof, never a promise (WP p. 38). Nothing on where either founder goes.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Founders who plan their own exit from operations usually do it privately; the ones who write down which functions leave their desk by when are rare, and they are the ones whose companies survive the transition without a scramble.

**Options:** (a) structural; (b) private; (c) a spoken line at the annual block. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** the structural definition becoming a judgment of a founder's ambition; Brandon saying where he wants to go, which is his and not recorded; the convergence with 6.1.4 used as a lever on a founder.

**A finished answer:** the choice; the never-a-record rule confirmed; the convergence named or declined.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 6.3.3 Decide whether each founder keeps the book's personal career document, and whether the house ever references it

**The question:** the book asks you to keep a private document on the roles you have held, six questions each, reread over time (pp. 499 to 500). Do you keep one, is it separate from the working-with-me document, and does the house ever point at it?

**Why it matters now:** the working-with-me document is being written (1.1.7) and its prompts are set (1.1.2). Two of them sit near this document's questions. If the two documents are one, the private answers risk reaching the issued version (1.1.5). If they are two, the founder is asked some things twice.

**Openers:**
- Read the six questions aloud. Do not answer them. Which one would you least want in a document anyone else could read?
- Sideways: the white paper measures the environment by how people grow beyond the building (WP p. 23). This document is how a person keeps track of that for themselves. Should the house offer it to everyone, and never look?
- The book says the document surfaces the complements a leader should hire rather than duplicate (p. 500). 1.1.10 asked that question of the founders together. Did it get answered?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Kept privately and separate; folded into the working-with-me document's private prompts; or not kept?
- If kept: its relationship to 1.1.6 and 1.1.7's strengths line, so nothing is asked twice.
- Offered to leads and at the career conversation (4.4.3) through the kit (6.3.12)? The house never reads one either way.

**What the book says:** the six questions, a few bullets per role, started early, reread every 6, 12, or 18 months (pp. 499 to 500).

**White-paper default:** none. 1.1.2's prompts are the nearest thing.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. A private career journal is common advice and uncommon practice; the people who keep one usually describe it as most useful at the moments the book names, when a role changes.

**Options:** (a) private and separate; (b) folded in; (c) not kept. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** the two documents merging so that private reflection reaches the issued version; the house "recommending" the document in a way that makes not keeping one visible.

**A finished answer:** the choice, each founder for their own; the no-double-asking rule; the kit's audience.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders, each for their own.

### 6.3.4 Decide the founders' own tempo of stepping back: whether the book's 6, 12, and 18-month questions run, and where

**The question:** the book asks three questions on three rhythms (pp. 500 to 502). Does the house hold a moment for a founder to ask them, or is that entirely the founder's?

**Why it matters now:** the annual block (2.3.30) already has the founders' documents on the table. It is the one hour a year the house has set aside that could also hold this, and it is a closed hour the house cannot read. Deciding now keeps the block's agenda honest.

**Openers:**
- When was the last time you asked yourself whether this was the right role to learn what you want to learn next? Do not say the answer; say whether the question had a time.
- Sideways: the white paper's build is gated on proof, with two natural pauses, after the first quarter and after the first year (WP p. 36). Are those your pauses too, or does the house's rhythm crowd yours out?
- If the annual block held a closed hour with no record, would you use it, or would you rather the house not know it exists?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The founders' own; a closed hour at the annual block with no record; or the working-with-me document's revision rule as the only rhythm?
- If the closed hour: the other partner present or not, each founder's choice?
- Confirm the learning half is 6.1.10's.

**What the book says:** six months, twelve months, eighteen months, each with its question; keep two or three directions in mind; keep your own counsel (pp. 500 to 502).

**White-paper default:** the two pauses (WP p. 36); the annual block (2.3.30). Nothing on a founder's own rhythm.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Founders who keep a personal review rhythm usually attach it to something already on the calendar (a year end, a board cycle) rather than invent one; the attachment is what makes it happen.

**Options:** (a) the founders' own; (b) the closed hour; (c) the revision rule. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** a closed hour that acquires an agenda; the revision rule pulling private questions into the document.

**A finished answer:** the choice, each founder for their own; the annual block's agenda updated if (b).

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders, each for their own.

### 6.3.5 Decide the founders' own review: none, the domain read, or a form

**The question:** 5.4.7 named the review holder "for each founder, if any." Is there any? And if not, what reads a founder, and what may none of it become?

**Why it matters now:** the leads' reviews start in the first quarter, held by the partners (5.4.27). The leads will know whether the partners are read by anything. The book's "nobody taps you on the shoulder" (p. 498) is either a fact the house accepts by design or a gap it fills.

**Openers:**
- The white paper's review never contains new information because the person already knows (WP p. 19). Who tells you?
- Sideways: 4.7.12 already has someone watching your brief for who spoke and who was asked. That is a read of you. Is it a review?
- The book went years without a review, and the one that named her strengths was formative (pp. 384 to 385). If no one ever names yours, is that a cost, and to whom?
- Two partners tied in every reserved decision reviewing each other: say what could go wrong.

**Narrowing questions:**
- No review under any name (the four reads that exist anyway); the domain read on 5.4.3's dimensions, in writing, no rating; or a mutual partner review at the annual block?
- Confirm: nothing reaches pay through this program; the founder's own account is on the page if any read exists; a lead's impression of a founder is never a source.
- 5.4.7's "if any" answered by role.

**What the book says:** nobody taps you on the shoulder; outside advice is welcome (p. 498); the review that named her strengths (pp. 384 to 385).

**White-paper default:** developmental reviews every three to six months; feedback both directions; team members weigh in on management effectiveness (WP p. 19). Nothing on a founder's review; founder pay is the agreement's.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. With a board, the board reviews the chief executive annually. Without one, founders commonly have no review at all, and some substitute a coach or a mutual co-founder review.

**Options:** (a) none, with the existing reads made explicit; (b) the domain read on the review's dimensions; (c) a mutual review at the annual block. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** (b) becoming a review by another name; (c) giving a founder a person page the earlier work refused; anything about how Brandon performs being said or written here.

**A finished answer:** the choice; the three confirmations; 5.4.7 told; the leads' page (6.2.13) told what, if anything, they may know of it.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 6.3.6 Decide what absorbs a founder's failure, when no one stands above

**The question:** management absorbs blame from staff (WP p. 17). Nothing is above management. When a founder's mechanism fails, what absorbs it, and what never does?

**Why it matters now:** the first founder failure the leads see will happen in the first quarter, and the leads will learn from it whether the rule about blame reaches the top. 1.2.3 decided whether a founder's domain failure gets the post-mortem; this decides who stands in front of it.

**Openers:**
- The white paper says "Sŏn has done it too, which is how the team knows the trap" (WP p. 17). That sentence is a founder's failure absorbed by being written down and published. Is that the model?
- Sideways: the book says most mistakes are quickly forgotten in a fast-growing company (p. 489). Sŏn's record forgets nothing. Is that heavier or lighter for you? Do not answer; notice.
- A lead's team fails and the lead absorbs it. Your mechanism fails and the leads are in the room. Who speaks?
- Counterexample: a partner who absorbs the other's failures in front of the leads looks strong and builds a dependency. Is that a cost you would take?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The record absorbs it (a process update and a version, an evidence note by the other partner naming no person, the reserved classes held); the other partner absorbs it in front of the leads; or the founder absorbs their own at the leads' review?
- What never absorbs it: a lead, the team, the public? Confirm.
- "Mistakes are quickly forgotten": accepted or declined for the founders?

**What the book says:** reframe and move on; mistakes are forgotten because everything changes (p. 489); be the manager your manager can lean on (p. 502).

**White-paper default:** management absorbs blame; post-mortems are private and process-focused; the process is fixed (WP p. 17). Nothing above management.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Public post-mortems of leadership failures are a practice some technology companies adopted deliberately and most restaurants have never had; the usual restaurant pattern is that an owner's failure becomes a story the staff tells and the owner never hears.

**Options:** (a) the record; (b) the other partner; (c) the founder themselves. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** a lead absorbing a founder's failure; an evidence note that names a person; the status quo used to freeze a fix rather than protect against an unagreed change.

**A finished answer:** the choice; the never-list confirmed; the book's forgiveness accepted or declined.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 6.3.8 Decide what the house does when a founder leaves: continuity only, on the same records as anyone

**The question:** the operating agreement governs a founder's departure. What, if anything, is the house's part, and what is explicitly not?

**Why it matters now:** the white paper's claim that a departure is "bounded, temporary, calculable" (WP p. 14) is untested for the seats where it matters most. 4.3.10 said a founder's departure runs on the same records as anyone's and referred it to 2.1. This is where that is made concrete, after counsel (6.3.7) has said what the agreement already holds.

**Openers:**
- Read 2.1.1's note. What does the agreement say about a principal leaving, and what does it not say that the house would need to know on the day?
- Sideways: the white paper says when a key person leaves the knowledge does not leave (WP p. 14). Run that sentence on your own seat. What leaves?
- If Dominic left, what would sit on your seat the next morning, and for how long? Should that be a dated row or an inheritance?
- What must the house never design here, because it is the agreement's? Say the list.

**Narrowing questions:**
- Continuity only (the departure read on the seat; the knowledge-transfer pass; inventory rows moved as dated overload; the structure-change entry; the leads told with coverage only); plus a scheduled hand-over conversation in the final period; or nothing until it happens?
- The not-ours list: the reason, the vote, terms, pay, equity, notice, restrictive covenants, incapacity and death, employee status. Confirm.
- The chef partner's version: drafted and pending 2.1.10?

**What the book says:** nothing directly; careers wind and the pivotal moments are not always visible at the time (pp. 498 to 499).

**White-paper default:** built to run without them; the departure is bounded when the knowledge stays; the business does not rest on one person (WP pp. 06, 14, 37). Nothing on a founder leaving.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Operating agreements commonly hold buy-sell, incapacity, and deadlock provisions and rarely hold an operational continuity plan; the two are usually written by different people and never read together.

**Options:** (a) continuity only; (b) plus the hand-over conversation; (c) nothing designed. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** the house's note touching anything on the not-ours list; the leads' notification carrying anything about the person; a continuity design that assumes the departing founder is available to run it.

**A finished answer:** the choice; the not-ours list confirmed; the chef seat's version marked pending; 6.3.13 unlocked.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner once seated; counsel.

### 6.3.9 Decide what the founders owe the team's path in return: the dates by which founder-held functions open

**The question:** the functions the founders hold by default before the leads exist are the first seats and designations the internal pool is meant to fill. Beyond the handover list, does the house say so?

**Why it matters now:** the opportunities page (4.7.17) is written for the first cohort. Whether it shows what founder-held functions will open, and roughly when, is decided before anyone reads it. And the book's closing note on privilege (p. 500) has one concrete form here: a founder keeping a load past its date has closed a path someone was on.

**Openers:**
- Name a function you hold today that a person in the first cohort could hold in a year. Is there a date anywhere that says so?
- Sideways: the white paper says advancement requires preparation, not permission (WP p. 14). A founder-held function with no date requires permission. Whose?
- The book asks you to use some of your position to help others who lack the same choices (p. 500). At Sŏn, is the handover list that help, or is there more?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The handover list and the pool-first rule only; plus the opportunities page showing founder-held functions with a horizon; or no commitment beyond 4.4.18?
- The connection to 6.1.20 (a load past its date closes a path): named or declined?

**What the book says:** the privilege of choice, and using some of it for others (p. 500).

**White-paper default:** the path explicit before it starts; preparation not permission; internal succession as the goal (WP pp. 11, 14, 19). 3.1.38, 4.4.18, 5.6.8, and 4.7.17 already hold the mechanisms.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Founders who publicly date their own hand-overs are rare; where it happens, it is usually a successor's start date rather than a function-by-function list, and the function-by-function form is the white paper's own logic applied upward.

**Options:** (a) the list and the pool rule; (b) plus the opportunities page; (c) nothing beyond 4.4.18. Depth: cons. 8.

**Watch for:** a public horizon that becomes a promise the white paper's discipline would refuse (WP p. 36, never assert the unproven); dates set to look generous rather than to hold.

**A finished answer:** the choice; the opportunities page told if (b); the connection to 6.1.20 named or declined.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
