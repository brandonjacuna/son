# 6.2 Foster relationships: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Conclusion, pp. 491 to 497. Full text: `sources/extraction/build/s16/book.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## Why relationships matter

"It's a marathon, not a sprint" and "if you want to go fast, go alone; if you want to go far, go together" are repeated so often because they're true. Company building needs pacing and company (p. 491). Her own hardest moments at Google and Stripe are remembered through the friendships built in meetings, dinners, and late nights when stress turned into laughter, relationships she still draws on. Ask for help, and share the load: management is not just building a complementary team, it's having the mutual self-awareness to look at your whole orbit and seek people with different strengths (p. 491).

## Working with your own manager

Leverage runs both directions: your manager should get leverage from you, and you from them. A good manager unblocks your team, advocates for resources, provides context, helps set priorities, and helps you develop. Managing up, in her sense, means working with your manager to get the best results for your team and company, not being political (pp. 491 to 492). Practical guidance:

- Help your manager be their best; never surprise them with late news of problems or poor results.
- Think about what you appreciate in a report, then apply it upward: your manager likely wants the same things.
- Get to know each other personally, and offer constructive feedback on how the two of you could work better together.

Quoted: Charles Phillips (founder and managing partner, Recognize; former CEO, Infor) on Ron Williams's "two and two": know the people and job roles two levels below and two levels above you, and check whether incentives up and down that chain are aligned (pp. 492 to 493).

## Working with other managers

Managing sideways matters as much as managing up. Understanding where your team sits in the larger organization, and building formal and informal connections, lets the whole produce more than the sum of its parts (p. 493). Beyond hallway encounters, she recommends actively cultivating relationships you can draw on while helping the other person do the same: share your team's mission and goals with key partners and stakeholders, and seek out people who might create or prevent obstacles. Other leaders are resources both as sounding boards, for instance practicing a hard 1:1 before having it, and as sources of organizational information.

Two suggested actions (pp. 493 to 494):

1. Map your team's partners and stakeholders; hold 1:1s with them or sit in on key meetings; share objectives and discuss how to work together.
2. Identify leaders you admire, inside or outside the company; get coffee or lunch to compare management notes. Some of these become people you can call on to pressure-test ideas.

Being a good peer means honoring commitments, listening, helping others, sharing useful information, and, if there's a problem with a fellow manager or their team, raising it directly with them before it escalates (p. 494).

Quoted: Sam Hawgood, chancellor of the University of California, San Francisco, on the value of understanding the personality and pressures of the people and parts of the system you interact with, especially before there's a specific reason to (pp. 494 to 495).

## Working with founders

Founders got their jobs from a vision that became a business; senior managers got theirs by making management their business, which doesn't guarantee they're good managers but does mean experience. Founders, especially first-time founders, may never have seen a company operate larger than their own current size, so a manager coming from other companies can offer insight into what's ahead. Founders also come from first principles and will question assumptions a manager treats as fixed; both sides have something to learn from the other. The relationship carries friction by design, and the manager's job is to figure out what rate of change the organization and founders can and should absorb (p. 495).

Three principles for that work (pp. 495 to 498):

- **Check the fruit.** A Box executive's metaphor: treat your ideas as fruit that isn't ripe yet, put them in a bag, and periodically test one to see if the organization or founder is ready. Often the trigger is an emerging problem the idea solves, or the founder arriving at it independently. Expect many ideas to stay in the bag forever; it's usually better to wait than to force something the company will reject.
- **Build the Camry, not the Escalade.** When a change is worth pushing even if the founders aren't sold, ask for the lightest version, and grow from there. Her own Stripe example: proposing an LMS and a CMS to Patrick Collison, who didn't know the acronyms and, more importantly, wanted to know why. Her mistakes were using acronyms instead of explaining the actual need (one place for training content, one place for company content), and proposing new tools instead of starting with what the team already used. They piloted with an existing document editor first and only adopted dedicated tools later, once the need for something more robust was clear. General rule: propose changes as a pilot, with what's changing, how success will be assessed, and when permanence will be decided.
- **Understand what matters.** Work to understand founders' values the way you would a report's, including the trade-offs they're willing to make for something they value, for instance whether a commitment to polish is worth a delayed launch or a lost customer. If founders haven't written their values down, asking them to could be the start of the founding documents work in Chapter 2. Offsites and 1:1s both work for this.
