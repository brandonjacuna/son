# 0 Management basics checklist: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Introduction, with the Management Prerequisites Checklist at pp. 30 to 32. Full text: `extraction/build/s01/book.md` (pp. 9 to 32) and `extraction/build/s01/exercises.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## Where the checklist sits

The Introduction closes with a checklist of the management basics she will not cover in the book. Her rule: "If you can tick off all the items on this checklist, you're ready to start this book" (p. 24). The basics are still important; she simply assumes them. The book covers the harder things: difficult conversations, layering a team, asking someone to leave (p. 24).

Two ideas from the Introduction frame the checklist.

**Structure comes first.** Her answer to a startup asking which processes to adopt: she will not say which, but "you need them, and you need them sooner than you realize." A game is fun because it has rules and a way to win; a field with random equipment and no rules hurts someone (p. 14). Her associate manager's backhanded compliment, that she taught the importance of stating the obvious, is the same point: make implicit structures and beliefs explicit so a group can become a team (p. 14).

**Few frameworks apply to everyone.** Company-wide frameworks are indicators of priorities, the things everyone must do the same way. But coordination costs are massive, and too many processes consume the headspace that should go to work; uniformity must outweigh variability. She names four areas where company-wide frameworks belong, each a chapter: foundations and planning for goals and resources; a comprehensive hiring approach; intentional team development; feedback and performance mechanisms. Cross-team day-to-day activities may add a few more, kept limited (pp. 20 to 21).

## The checklist (pp. 31 to 32)

Legal: you are aware of your legal responsibilities as a manager in the jurisdiction in which you operate.

You and individual reports:
- Regular 1:1s, weekly or biweekly, rarely rescheduled.
- 1:1 agendas with contributions from both sides, notes, and agreed actions.
- Quarterly goals tracked together.
- Bidirectional feedback sessions every three to six months.
- More formal performance conversations every 3, 6, or 12 months (more often the faster the company changes), weighing both the results and how they were achieved.

You and your teams:
- Weekly or biweekly meetings.
- Show up on time; avoid rescheduling.
- Time together that is useful: aligning on plans, making decisions, workshopping challenges.

You and your division:
- Job descriptions and an interview rubric for the roles you are hiring for.
- Job ladders and levels, clearly communicated.
- A compensation philosophy and a clear framework employees understand.

You:
- Understand your own job description and the function of your team.
- Quarterly goals agreed with your own manager.
- 1:1s on a regular cadence with your own manager.
- Access to regular context from your manager or leaders on company priorities and goals.

## Two Introduction ideas the checklist assumes

**Scaling to the call** (p. 22). As a company grows, people are called to lead in larger and more complex roles; even if the job title is unchanged, its scope has grown. Her question of each person: can they scale to the call? The checklist's ladders and levels assume this kind of growth.

**The metronome** (pp. 17 to 19). Fitts and Posner's three stages of skill (cognitive, associative, autonomous) and the plateau that follows; Ericsson's finding that improvement past the plateau comes from deliberately practicing faster than is comfortable. Her reading: a fast-growing company sets that uncomfortable pace for you. This idea is taken up in chunk 1.4, where the question of pace belongs at Sŏn.
