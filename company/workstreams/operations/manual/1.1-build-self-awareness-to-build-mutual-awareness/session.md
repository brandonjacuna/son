# 1.1 Build self-awareness to build mutual awareness: working session

The guide for the session that works this chunk. Read `decisions.md` first for anything already settled. Task numbers are in `tasks.md`; "cons. N" is a section of `considerations.md`; "WP p. N" is the white paper. This is a Chapter 1 chunk: the session designs the exchange and prepares Brandon to write his own document. The facilitator asks; the facilitator never supplies an answer about him.

## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** The book's first operating principle: know your own values, work style, and strengths well enough to say them out loud, so the people you work with can do the same and the group can function as a team (pp. 36 to 40). At Sŏn that is three equal principals in separate domains who will rarely watch each other work, plus, soon, two leads who report to no one. The chunk decides how the founders make themselves legible to each other (the form, the content, the instruments, the readers, the chef's timing), then has Brandon do the exercises and write his own working-with-me document, which becomes the kit every later writer follows.

**Upstream.** Nothing is required before this chunk. Chunk 0's standing note applies (the house opens near full size and stays there, so the founding team's gaps get filled once). 1.2, 1.3, and 1.4 are already built and point here: 1.2.1 leaves each founder's preferences for hearing they are wrong in their working-with-me document; 1.3.2 asks the kit to follow its ruling on the word "manager"; 1.4.1's words for the human system and the software apply to everything written here.

**Who else must agree.** Dominic, on every decision about the exchange (1.1.1 to 1.1.5, 1.1.9), since the document is a peer instrument and half of the documents are his. The chef partner, once seated, on 1.1.9's consequence for them. No one agrees to the content of Brandon's own document; it is his.

**Why it sits here.** The book puts it first because the other three principles rest on it (p. 37). For Sŏn there is a second reason: the white paper's claim that the house runs without its founders (WP pp. 05 to 06) has never been tested against the founders, and the inventory prompt in this chunk is the first test.

## 2. Agenda

A default order. Brandon may start anywhere.

1. What the exchange is for, and its form (1.1.1).
2. What the document asks (1.1.2), and which exercises feed it (1.1.3).
3. Who reads it, and what the leads get (1.1.5).
4. How far the style vocabulary travels (1.1.4).
5. The chef partner's timing (1.1.9).
6. Brandon's own pass: the exercises and the document (1.1.6, 1.1.7), and what the kit needs to capture.
7. The exchange with Dominic (1.1.10) and the list of what lives in the founders' heads (1.1.12): what happens after the session.
8. Parking lot.

Steps 1 to 5 are decisions to record. Step 6 is a conversation that may run long and should not be forced to finish in the session; the document is written alone afterward.

## 3. Briefs

### 1.1.1 Decide how the founders make their self-awareness mutual

**The question:** when Brandon and Dominic, and later the chef partner, learn how each other actually works, what carries that: a document each writes, a conversation, an outside assessment, or some pairing?

**Why it matters now:** everything else in the chunk hangs on the form. Decide a document and 1.1.2 to 1.1.5 follow; decide a conversation alone and most of the chunk collapses to one meeting and the kit disappears. It also sets what the first two hires are asked to do, since the leads will be asked to follow whatever the founders did (cons. 6).

**Openers:**
- Dominic is unreachable for a month starting tomorrow. What do you not know about how to run his half? Now flip it: what does he not know about yours?
- Sideways: think of the best co-worker you ever had in a room. How long did it take to know how they worked, and what did it cost you both to learn it by collision?
- The book's frame is a manager and reports who see each other every day (p. 37). You and Dominic will see each other's work how often, honestly?
- The chef partner reads the white paper and knows what Sŏn is. What do they read to know who you two are?
- If the answer is "we just talk," what would a lead in month six be able to point to?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Written, spoken, or both? If written, is it something a stranger to the partnership could read cold?
- Is this for the two of you, or is it something every later writer at the lead level follows? The second means a kit.
- What is the one-sentence purpose you would give a lead when you ask them to write theirs?

**What the book says:** self-awareness is the key; once you can name your values and tendencies you can talk about them with your team, and they can do the same, which is what lets a group of individuals function as a team (pp. 37, 39 to 40).

**White-paper default:** none stated. The house is built so it does not depend on either founder's memory (WP pp. 05 to 06), and knowledge held in people rather than the system is the first failure it names (WP p. 13). The old synthesis applied that to the founders' self-knowledge and argued for a written document (cons. 5).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Written "how I work" or "working with me" documents from a leader to their team are common in technology companies and are usually one to three pages, written by the person, revised occasionally, and shared rather than presented.
- Co-founder pairs frequently use a facilitated conversation or an outside coach for this instead of a document; the result is usually not written down and does not transfer to later hires.
- Formal assessments (Insights Discovery, DiSC) are commonly bought for a leadership team as a one-day workshop with a debrief; the vocabulary tends to persist longer than the reports do.

**Options:** (a) a written document on one shared structure, exchanged; (b) a facilitated conversation, no document; (c) a shared assessment plus debrief; (d) a document followed by a conversation. What each commits Sŏn to: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** choosing a document because the old work assumed one; choosing a conversation because writing about oneself is uncomfortable, which is the reason the book gives for doing it; a form that only works for a founder and would embarrass a lead.

**A finished answer:** the form named, who takes part now and later, and the one-sentence purpose Brandon would say to a lead.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 1.1.2 Decide what the working-with-me document covers

**The question:** what does the document ask, and which of the additions the old work argued for does Sŏn keep: the domain's edges, what lives only in the founder's head, how the founder decides, load signals, dependencies on the other partners, what the founder does not know yet?

**Why it matters now:** two of the additions feed other chunks directly. The decision-mode prompt is each founder's draft of the rule 2.1.13 sets; the inventory prompt is the source of 1.1.12. Leave them out and those chunks start from nothing. Put too much in and the document becomes a seat description or a contract, which the old work warned against (cons. 5).

**Openers:**
- Read the eleven prompt titles in cons. 5 aloud. Which one would you rather not answer? Start there; that is usually the one Dominic most needs.
- Sideways: the white paper is thirty-nine pages of decisions. How many of the reasons behind them are written anywhere? Who would explain them to a lead if you were away?
- The book's version is three things: values, style, strengths (pp. 37 to 38). Would a document with only those three tell Dominic how to work with you across the seam between the concept and the model?
- Where does your domain end? Name the nearest three decisions you are not sure are yours.
- Picture the chef partner reading Dominic's document and yours side by side. What would they want to be able to compare?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The book's three, the eleven, or a chosen subset? If a subset, go prompt by prompt: in or out, and why.
- Is the inventory prompt (what lives in my head) in? If yes, it commits both founders to dates and to 1.1.12.
- Is the decision-mode prompt in? If yes, it is a draft for 2.1.13 and the document must say it does not set the rule.
- What must the document never be? Read the old work's list (cons. 5) and accept, cut, or add.
- Revision: after dinner's first quarter and when the seat changes, or a fixed date?

**What the book says:** self-awareness has three components, values, innate preferences and work style, and skills and capability gaps (pp. 37 to 38); the working-with-me document is the exercise she points to for building it further (p. 51); teams become mirrors of their managers, so the blind spot has to be named (pp. 48 to 50).

**White-paper default:** each founder owns a domain outright and is accountable to its outcomes (WP pp. 03, 07 to 08); the house must not depend on a founder's memory (WP pp. 05 to 06, 13). Those two facts argue for the edges prompt and the inventory prompt; the white paper does not say so itself.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Leaders' "how I work" documents typically cover communication preferences, decision style, feedback preferences, working hours, and known quirks; few include an inventory of what the writer alone knows, which is why the old work called that prompt the one that makes it a founding instrument.
- Co-founder agreements and operating agreements sometimes carry a plain-language "how we decide" section; when they do, each founder's own draft of it is usually the starting input.

**Options:** (a) the book's three components; (b) the eleven prompts; (c) a chosen subset. Commitments: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** answering the prompts in the session instead of choosing them; a document that quietly becomes the founder seat description 2.1 owns; leaving out the inventory prompt because it is uncomfortable rather than because it is unnecessary.

**A finished answer:** the prompt list fixed, each addition in or out with a reason, the refusals stated, the revision rule stated.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 1.1.3 Decide which self-assessment exercises the founders use, and whether a formal assessment is bought

**The question:** before writing, does each founder do the book's three exercises (values, the style grid, the strengths inventory), the old work's extended versions, a formal assessment with a facilitator, or nothing?

**Why it matters now:** the exercises are what keep the document from being written from self-image. They are also the kit's intake; whatever the founders do, every lead will do. And a formal assessment is a cost and a vendor vocabulary that the kit would carry for years.

**Openers:**
- Sideways: you have coached people through exercises like these in your consulting work. When did one of them actually change what someone wrote about themselves, and what made the difference?
- The book's test is "do you talk to think or think to talk" (p. 40). Do you already know the answer for yourself, and does Dominic know it about you?
- The old work asked for the style placement three times: at work, at home, under load (cons. 3). Is the under-load one the one you would learn something from?
- Would you rather be handed an outside read of yourself, or write your own and be argued with?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The book's exercises as written, or with the additions (three placements; a cost and guardrail per strength; a column for what the house depends on in you personally)?
- A formal assessment in addition? If yes, which, who facilitates, and does the vocabulary go into the kit?
- Alone before the exchange, or together in a sitting?
- What of the working stays private? The old work's default: only the three values with trade-offs, the placements, and the strengths with costs export.

**What the book says:** values exercise, ten to five to three, each with activities, outcomes, and trade-offs (pp. 64 to 66); place yourself on the grid for home and work (p. 67); list strengths and split into innate and acquired skills and capabilities (pp. 67 to 68); DiSC, Myers-Briggs, and Insights Discovery are named as tools, the last as her favorite for the vocabulary it gave her (pp. 44 to 46).

**White-paper default:** none. The white paper describes the intelligences the two strands require (WP pp. 10 to 11) and names no assessment for anyone.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Insights Discovery and DiSC are typically administered as a questionnaire plus a facilitated group debrief; the four-color or four-letter shorthand is what teams keep.
- The values reduction exercise she adapts comes from Stan Slap's *Bury My Heart at Conference Room B* and is normally done alone, then shared in small groups.
- Strengths inventories in common use (CliftonStrengths is the best known) produce a ranked list and are often criticized for producing no trade-offs, which is the gap the book's version and the old work's cost sentence address.

**Options:** (a) the three exercises with the additions; (b) the three exercises as written; (c) a formal assessment in addition, with a facilitator; (d) none. Commitments: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** doing the exercises in the session (they are done alone; the session decides which); a paid assessment chosen for its authority rather than its use; the three placements collapsing to one.

**A finished answer:** the exercise set named, formal assessment yes or no, alone or together, and the privacy line stated.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

**Capture for the kit:** whatever set is chosen is `intake.md`. Note which exercise Brandon expects to be hardest and why; the guide should warn the next writer.

### 1.1.4 Decide how the work-style vocabulary is used beyond the founders

**The question:** once the founders and leads have a shared shorthand for style, where may those four names appear: only in a person's own document, in a lead's conversation with a founder about themselves, in the whole team's vocabulary, or nowhere?

**Why it matters now:** the leads are the first hires and will hear the vocabulary from the founders; if there is no rule, there is a default, and the default on a floor is that a shorthand for style becomes a label on a person within a week. The white paper's hiring and progression are built on documented competency (WP pp. 11, 18) and its vocabulary for difference on the floor is the two strands; a second vocabulary either sits beside them or contradicts them.

**Openers:**
- Sideways: you have worked rooms where a manager had a word for a type of person. What did that word do to the person over a season?
- The white paper's two strands are "different jobs requiring different types of intelligence" (WP p. 10 to 11). Is that the same thing as the book's grid, or a different axis?
- A lead says to you in a check-in, "I know I go Director under load, so read my Friday message that way." Useful or dangerous?
- Would you want a line cook on week one to know what a Promoter is?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Founders and leads only? With the one upward use, or none?
- If team-wide: taught where, by whom, and what stops it appearing on a schedule note?
- Whichever option: is it written that the grid is never used in hiring, strand or role assignment, review, or pay? That sentence is what 3.1, 3.2, 4.1, 5.4, and 5.5 need.

**What the book says:** the styles are for understanding your likely weak points and who complements you (pp. 41 to 44); a shared vocabulary lets a team talk about difference (p. 45); she later cautions against typologies used as labels on colleagues.

**White-paper default:** the strands are the floor's vocabulary for difference; hiring reads documented competencies "rather than gut feeling" (WP p. 18); progression is competencies anyone can see (WP p. 11). Silent on personality vocabulary.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Companies that adopt a typology company-wide usually find the shorthand outlives its caveats; the caution the book itself gives about a leader calling a colleague by a color is the standard failure.
- Restaurants rarely use personality typologies on the floor at all; where hospitality groups use assessments, it is typically for salaried leadership, not hourly staff.

**Options:** (a) founders and leads only, with one upward use; (b) founders and leads only, no exceptions; (c) shared vocabulary for the whole team; (d) no typology at all. Commitments: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** the option chosen for the founders leaking to the leads by omission; an exception that is really a floor use in disguise; forgetting that hourly staff are never asked to write the document under (a) or (b).

**A finished answer:** the reach named, where the names may and may not appear, and the never-used-for line written in one sentence.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 1.1.5 Decide who reads each founder's document, and what the leads receive

**The question:** do the founders' documents stay among the partners, go to the leads in full or in a shorter version, or reach every hire; and are the leads asked to write their own?

**Why it matters now:** the leads are the first hires. The candidate sheet at the first interview already promises "communication expectations" (WP p. 18), and a lead's first check-in with a founder happens in week one. What the lead knows about the founder by then is decided here or by accident. It also decides whether the document is a peer instrument or a public one, which changes what the founders will write in it.

**Openers:**
- The Maitre d is seated. Their first check-in with you is Thursday. What do you want them to have read about you by then, and what would you rather they never read?
- Sideways: think of a manager you worked under who you understood well and one you did not. What did the first one do that the second did not, and was any of it written?
- Dominic's Operations Lead reads Dominic's document. Should that lead also read yours? Why?
- If a lead writes their own, who reads it: the founders, the other lead, their own team?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Partners only; partners in full and leads a shorter version; one document for all; a version for every hire?
- If a lead version: which prompts stay, which are withheld, and what is the reason you would give a lead for the withheld ones?
- Timing: before the first check-in, or later? That goes to 3.3.
- Do leads write their own, on the same structure, for the founders and each other? Does theirs add anything (the old work suggested: how they will talk about development with their own people)?

**What the book says:** the working-with-me document builds mutual awareness with the people you manage (p. 51); mutual awareness is what lets a group of individuals function as a team (pp. 39 to 40).

**White-paper default:** the two leads are parallel co-leads serving the same center (WP p. 10); communication expectations are on the candidate sheet (WP p. 18); every new person has a ninety-day plan with check-ins (WP p. 18). Silent on what a lead learns about the founders and when. The old work's version: partners in full, leads a shorter version before the first check-in, never below the leads (cons. 6).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- In technology companies a leader's "how I work" document is most often shared with direct reports and sometimes published company-wide; the more public the audience, the blander the document tends to become.
- The practice of asking new managers to write their own on joining is common where the founders have modelled it first.

**Options:** (a) partners in full, leads a shorter version, leads write their own; (b) partners only; (c) one document for partners and leads; (d) a version for every hire. Commitments: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** choosing the widest audience and then writing a document that says nothing; withholding prompts from the leads that they would need to work with a founder; forgetting that the chef partner is a partner, not a lead, and reads in full.

**A finished answer:** the readers named, the lead version's prompts fixed if one exists, the timing handed to 3.3, and the leads' own document yes or no.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 1.1.9 Decide when the chef partner writes and reads a working-with-me document

**The question:** does the incoming chef partner exchange documents with the founders before the partnership is signed, as diligence on the people, or after joining, as onboarding, or some split?

**Why it matters now:** the chef search is a real event in the build (WP p. 29) and 3.1 needs to know what a candidate partner is shown and asked for. The kitchen cannot be tested until the chef is seated and the menu exists, so the chef's timing shapes the whole kitchen hiring sequence; a decision here that delays the seat has costs downstream.

**Openers:**
- Sideways: you have been on both sides of a partnership forming. When did you learn the thing about a partner you wish you had known before signing, and would a document have told you?
- The white paper says the chef partner is "freed from carrying the rest of the business alone" (WP p. 05). What do they need to know about the two people carrying it, and when?
- Would you hand a candidate partner your document before they have committed? What in it would you hesitate over?
- The chef writes theirs on day one and it is about a house they have not worked in. Or on day sixty and it is about a real kitchen. Which document do you want?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Before signing, after joining, or founders' read before and chef's written after?
- If before signing: at what point in the search, and to how many candidates?
- If after: by when, and what does the chef read on arrival?
- Whichever: is the chef told that the founders hold an open question about how the chef seat's authority is described (2.1's question), and when?

**What the book says:** you never know someone else's story; difficult behavior usually has a deeper reason worth understanding (p. 39); mutual awareness before a group can function as a team (pp. 39 to 40).

**White-paper default:** the chef partner is an equal founding partner, not a hire (WP pp. 03, 05); whether lunch is ever built is a decision among all three partners (WP p. 36); a business whose value lives in one person is the dependency the house is built against (WP p. 37). Silent on the order in which new and seated partners learn each other. The old work left the question open with two timings (cons. 7).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Founding teams that add a partner after formation commonly do reference and working-style diligence informally, over dinners and trial projects, rather than by document; a written exchange before signing is unusual.
- Where a "how I work" document exists at all, it is nearly always an onboarding artifact, read in the first weeks.

**Options:** (a) diligence, before signing; (b) onboarding, after joining; (c) the founders' documents read before signing, the chef's written after. Commitments: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** a timing chosen to avoid the awkwardness of showing a candidate a personal document, rather than for the partnership's sake; treating the chef as a hire in the timing while calling them a partner in the document; letting this decision drift until the search is already underway.

**A finished answer:** the timing named, what the candidate partner is shown and when, and 3.1 and 2.1 told.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner is told the choice and its reason.

### 1.1.6 and 1.1.7 Brandon's exercises and his working-with-me document

**The question:** not a decision. This is the conversation that gets Brandon ready to do the exercises alone and write the document alone, and that captures how the writing goes so the kit can carry it. The session does not write the document and does not record any answer.

**Why it matters now:** the first document written is the kit. Whatever order of prompts worked, whatever question unlocked a real answer, whatever he got stuck on, is what every lead and the chef partner inherit.

**Openers:**
- Which prompt do you want to write first, and which last? Say why.
- Sideways: a lead in month six is reading your document to figure out how to tell you a system you designed is not working at the edge. What sentence would you want them to find?
- The white paper names your seat's hazard itself: systems get built for how the founder thinks (WP p. 17). Where in the document does that get addressed, if anywhere?
- The public voice is your seat (WP pp. 03, 30). Which prompt carries what producing content does to your availability?
- What do you not know yet about how you will work in a room that does not exist?

**Narrowing questions (for him to take away, not answer here):**
- Which prompts can be answered from the exercises, and which need a story?
- Which answers are checkable commitments and which are qualities? The old work's test: "you will hear about a concept change before the team does" is checkable; "I will be transparent" is not.
- Where does the domain end? The edge cases, not the center.
- What is on the inventory list, with a date for each item?

**What the book says:** specific over categorical; the strength that is also a weakness, and the mechanism you build against it (pp. 48 to 50); signs of low self-awareness to check against (pp. 50 to 51).

**White-paper default:** facts about the seat only: the concept, the operating systems, the experience, and the public voice (WP p. 03); the content engine's volume is sized to run "without burning the founders out" (WP p. 30). No answer to any prompt.

**Watch for:** the facilitator reflecting back an answer as if it were recorded; a brief for the kit that quietly contains his answers; performed conviction in the draft ("I am committed to," "I believe in"); a document that describes the founder he intends to be.

**A finished answer for the session:** Brandon knows which exercises he is doing, in what order he will write, what stays private, and when he will have a draft; the kit notes are in `notes/`.

**Capture for the kit:** the order that worked; the openers that drew a real answer; the prompt he stalled on and what got him moving; the inputs he needed at hand (the white paper open to his seat, the exercise outputs, a calendar for presence by service period); how long it took; what he wished the instructions had said.

### 1.1.10 Exchange the documents and hold the mutual-awareness conversation

**The question:** not a decision. After both documents exist, what the founders do with them, and what leaves the conversation for other chunks.

**Why it matters now:** the book's payoff is mutual, not individual (pp. 39 to 40). The documents laid side by side are also the first place the founding team's gaps, overlapping edges, and decision drafts become visible, and three chunks are waiting for those: 2.1 (edges and decision drafts), 2.3 (what each needs from the other and how often), 4.1 and 3.1 (the strengths the first hires should carry).

**Openers (for the founders' conversation, not the session):**
- Read the other's edges prompt. Where do the two maps overlap, and what does neither of you claim?
- Read the other's decision prompt. Where would the two drafts collide on a Friday?
- Which of the other's under-load signals have you already seen? Did you read it as intent?
- The book's remedy for mirrors is to hire complements (pp. 48 to 50). Reading both documents, what strength is missing from this team entirely?

**A finished answer:** a note in `decisions.md`, in the founders' words, with the expected collisions and how they will handle them, the edges to hand to 2.1, the decision drafts to hand to 2.1.13, the dependencies to hand to 2.3, and the gaps to hand to 4.1 and 3.1. The documents themselves are not in the repo.

**Capture for the kit:** how the exchange was run (in one sitting or two; who read first; whether a third person was present) and whether that should be the guide's default for leads.

### 1.1.12 Build the founders' knowledge-transfer list

**The question:** whether the founders keep one running list of what each knows that the house depends on and that is not yet written anywhere the house can read it, with a date for each item.

**Why it matters now:** it is the only direct test of the white paper's founding claim that the house runs without its founders (WP pp. 05 to 06). It also has to exist before the leads arrive, because the leads are who the reasons get written for.

**Openers:**
- Name one thing about the concept that is in your head and not in the white paper. Now name the reason behind one decision that is in the white paper. Which was harder?
- Sideways: the vendor who returns your call and no one else's. Is that on any list?
- A lead extends a system you designed and gets it wrong because the reason was never written. Whose failure is that, by the white paper's own rule (WP p. 17)?

**Narrowing questions:**
- One list across the founders, or each in their own document?
- Who reviews it and how often? That is 2.3's; name it here so 2.3 inherits it.
- What counts as "in the system": a page a lead can find, or a page the stack surfaces?

**What the book says:** a strength the team depends on in you personally is the team's blind spot; build mechanisms that force the change (pp. 48 to 50).

**White-paper default:** the memory failure mode, knowledge that lives in people and leaves with them (WP p. 13); when a key person leaves, the knowledge "does not leave. It lives in the system" (WP p. 14). Asserted about the house, never checked against the founders.

**A finished answer:** yes or no to the list; if yes, its form, its owner, and the review question handed to 2.3; the founder-level bandwidth question handed to 6.1.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
