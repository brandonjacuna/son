## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** Her third principle: know whether you are managing to a defined goal or leading toward a new direction (pp. 56 to 60). Sŏn's management layer is cut on a different axis (what and how, two parallel co-leads, WP p. 10), so the chunk is about whether and how her distinction is used at all, one word the house has to settle, and whether people who lead without managing anyone are named as leaders.

**What it depends on upstream.** Nothing hard. The white paper's two-lead structure is taken as decided (WP p. 10); 4.1 designs the structure below it. 2.1.20 (the internal voice) is where the leadership-at-the-center question lands; do not re-run it here.

**Who else has to agree.** All three decisions bind Dominic, since the Operations Lead serves his domain as much as Brandon's; mark them "pending agreement from Dominic" until he has. 1.3.3 reaches the kitchen once the chef partner is seated.

**Why it comes before 3.x and 4.1.** The leads' role descriptions (4.1) and their onboarding (3.3) have to say what the house expects of a lead. If her vocabulary is used, they will be written as leader-and-manager; if the triage rule is used, they will carry the question. And the word "manager" appears on the candidate sheet (WP p. 18), which exists before the first interview.

**The book's frame in one line.** Managers solve the problems that have a fix; leaders take on the ones that keep moving; do not confuse the two (pp. 57 to 58).

## 3. Briefs

### 1.3.1 Decide how Sŏn uses the management and leadership distinction

**The question:** does Sŏn use her distinction as a triage rule on problems, as a second axis over the two leads, or not at all?

**Why it matters now:** the white paper puts both of her modes inside each lead (cons. 1). Laid over the leads as a second axis, her words rank one lead above the other or split each into two people, both of which the white paper forbids (WP pp. 10, 22). Used as a triage question, they add the one thing the white paper does not hold: a way to tell a problem that needs a fix from one that needs a judgment. 2.2 and 3.3 build from whatever is chosen.

**Openers:**
- Think of a general manager you worked under who was excellent. Was the excellence in setting the standard or in making the night run? Could you separate the two in that person?
- The Maitre d sets the emotional standard of the room and develops the team (WP p. 10). Which of those is leading and which is managing? Does the question help?
- Sideways: a service period is three months in and has not found its customer. A lead brings you a process fix. What do you say?
- Her line is that a great manager gets comfortable and a true leader is uncomfortable almost every day (p. 58). Which of the two leads do you expect to be uncomfortable, and about what?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Option (a), (b), or (c) (cons. 1)?
- If (a): the triage question in one sentence a lead can ask at the shift close.
- If (a): where does an adaptive problem go once triaged: the domain owner's judgment, or the founders together, and what marks the line?
- Do the leads' role descriptions in 4.1 carry her words at all?

**What the book says:** leaders set direction and drive change and take on adaptive problems; managers execute, create stability, and solve technical problems; both are necessary; senior managers must develop leadership (pp. 56 to 60).

**White-paper default:** the two-lead what-and-how split, each lead accountable to outcomes, neither reporting to the other (WP p. 10, stated outright); stability produced by the system (WP pp. 10, 21, stated outright); change through the process loop (WP p. 13, stated outright). That her distinction becomes a triage rule is inferred (cons. 1, option (a)).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Heifetz and Linsky's technical-versus-adaptive frame, which she uses, is widely taught as a diagnostic question rather than a job classification; that is the triage reading.
- Restaurant groups that split the general manager role usually split it into a service director and an operations or business manager, which is close to the white paper's cut; where the two are ranked, the ranked one tends to absorb the other's work, which is the failure the white paper predicts.

**Options:** cons. 1 (a), (b), (c), with what each commits Sŏn to.

**Watch for:** choosing (a) and then describing one lead as "the leader"; assuming stability is what a good lead produces personally, which is the failure the white paper names (cons. 1, "the departure"); Brandon describing himself as one or the other, which is 1.1 and 6.x material.

**A finished answer:** the option; if (a), the triage sentence and the routing of an adaptive problem; 2.2, 3.3, and 4.1 told.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 1.3.2 Decide whether "manager" is a word the house uses, and for what

**The question:** is "manager" used for the two leads, only for the function, or not at all?

**Why it matters now:** the book uses the word on every page. The white paper uses "management" as a function and "manager" mostly for the roles it is replacing, and once calls the Maitre d a Hospitality Manager (WP pp. 09 to 10, 17, 22). The old program removed the word entirely. Every document written before 4.1 settles naming (the founding document, the candidate sheet, the kit, the leads' descriptions) has to call the layer something, and a candidate reads the sheet at the first interview (WP p. 18).

**Openers:**
- A candidate at the first interview asks, "who is my manager?" What is the true answer in the white paper's structure, and what word do you want them to hear?
- Sideways: what did the word "manager" mean at the worst-run room you worked in, and what did it mean at the best?
- "Management absorbs blame from staff" (WP p. 17). If the word goes, what absorbs the blame?

**Narrowing questions:**
- For the two leads: "lead," "manager," or the title only?
- For the function: does "management" survive as a word for what the center does?
- Does the answer bind 4.1's convention, or does 4.1 reopen it?

**What the book says:** manager and leader throughout (pp. 56 to 60); management structures have worked for thousands of years (p. 18).

**White-paper default:** none; mixed usage (cons. 4). Role naming is "a separate session" (WP p. 15, stated outright).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies that drop "manager" for "lead" or "coach" usually keep "management" for the function in policy documents, because the legal and payroll vocabulary needs it; nothing more is useful.

**Options:** the word for the leads and the function only; the function only; not at all.

**Watch for:** deciding the whole naming convention here (that is 4.1); dropping the word without deciding what the candidate sheet says instead.

**A finished answer:** a one-line rule for the word, and the three documents it reaches (2.1.17, the candidate sheet in 3.1, the kit in 1.1.11) told.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 1.3.3 Decide whether leadership without a lead role is named and recognized at Sŏn

**The question:** is the senior server who sets a section's tone, the station lead who holds the line's standard, the mentor, the person who writes the training module, called a leader at Sŏn, and if so, in what form?

**Why it matters now:** her third use of the distinction is retaining high performers as leaders in non-manager roles (p. 58). At Sŏn that is nearly everyone, for good, because there are two leads and no ladder (WP pp. 10 to 11). The white paper already recognizes the behaviors (designations, cross-strand fluency in pay, the mentor, paid teaching, WP pp. 10 to 11, 18 to 19) without the word. The candidate sheet's advancement path (WP p. 18) has to say what a person can become here; 4.1 designs the designations and 5.5 the pay, and both need to know whether they are designing "leadership."

**Openers:**
- Name someone you worked with who ran a section better than the managers ran the room and never had a title. What did the place do about it? What should it have done?
- A line cook in year three asks what they can become here, given there is no ladder. Say the answer out loud.
- Sideways: what would it do to a floor if three people were called leaders and the rest were not?
- The white paper says a host can become the Maitre d (WP p. 19). What about the host who does not want to be the Maitre d and is the best host in the city?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Option (a), (b), or (c) (cons. 3)?
- If named: the behaviors in plain words, three or four of them, observable in a service.
- If named: a designation that is not a rank; how does it avoid becoming one within a year?
- Does the word appear on the candidate sheet's advancement path?

**What the book says:** leaders in non-manager roles are worth keeping as such (p. 58); experienced people need mostly a leader (p. 60).

**White-paper default:** strands, not levels; designations mark accountability in the moment and are not elevations; cross-strand fluency recognized in pay; the mentor as cultural steward; paid teaching; internal succession as the goal (WP pp. 10 to 11, 18 to 19, all stated outright). That any of this is called leadership is not in the white paper.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Engineering organizations commonly hold a parallel "individual contributor" track where senior people lead without managing; the pattern is well known and the word "leader" is used for it.
- Well-run restaurants often carry informal captains or trainers whose standing is real and unnamed; the usual failure is that the standing becomes a rank nobody designed.

**Options:** cons. 3 (a), (b), (c).

**Watch for:** naming it and then designing a ladder; not naming it and leaving the candidate sheet with nothing to say about what a person can become; a behavior list that describes a personality rather than something observable.

**A finished answer:** the option; if named, the behaviors and the hand-off to 4.1 and 5.5; the candidate sheet told.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen once seated.
