# 5.6 Managing high performers: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 5, pp. 419 to 430. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn. Full text: `sources/extraction/build/s14/book.md`.

## Why high performers take more energy

Managing extraordinary employees is both the most rewarding and the most demanding part of a manager's job. Top performers pull more than their weight, usually because of their ambition and motivation, and that same drive makes them demanding of themselves and of others. Managing a high performer can take more energy because they exert and demand more energy from those around them. The starting point is understanding what drives them and how they operate, which she ties back to building mutual self-awareness (p. 419).

## Pushers and pullers

High performers tend to fall into two groups; some people show both at different times (p. 420).

**Pushers** are highly ambitious and often critical. They spot what is broken in an organization quickly, set high standards for themselves and others, and use performance reviews to ask why they aren't getting more. They are internally motivated, don't mind ruffling feathers, and will push their manager too.

**Pullers** take on far more work than they should. Managers love assigning them projects because they almost always agree and deliver high quality, but pullers burn out. They suffer silently and may not reveal they are unhappy until they announce they are quitting. They want to be liked and have trouble saying no.

Great employees can have a disproportionately positive or negative impact. Pushers who write off people as "bad talent" can demoralize a team; pullers who become the automatic go-to for important work can crowd out other people's development. The manager's job is to nurture the best version of each high performer while making sure their effect on the wider team stays positive (pp. 420 to 421).

### Pushers: best and worst, and how to support them

At their best, pushers get better results from teammates, model good work, spot top talent, have a sharp radar for organizational problems, and act like owners rather than stopping at team boundaries. At their worst, they treat "good" talent as a narrow, all-or-nothing category, write off people in the "bad" bucket, make others feel distrusted or unfairly pressured, and become "empire builders" who focus on pulling in work rather than developing people (p. 421).

To support them: reward and praise their high standards, promote them and give raises, work with them to spot and develop talent, direct their radar toward real problems (sometimes by having them lead the fix), frame developing others as a growth area they can master, and coach them on delegation and building trust so they learn to exert influence indirectly instead of absorbing all the work themselves (pp. 421 to 422).

### Pullers: best and worst, and how to support them

At their best, pullers are great leads for high-priority projects, can jump into urgent work, model quality, and bring energy that motivates others. At their worst, they burn out, take on work that isn't the best use of their skills, don't delegate in ways that would help others grow, and can demotivate colleagues who never get the best projects (pp. 422 to 423).

To support them: help them prioritize and set boundaries, and before they take on work have them ask themselves whether they are the most qualified person, whether it's the most important thing they could be doing, and what they'd have to give up to take it on. Help them find their own interests rather than just executing what's handed to them (she points to the career conversation guidance, p. 283). Coach them on how to turn down projects, reassure them that declining one project doesn't close off others, and help them identify other people who could take on the work instead (p. 423).

## Additional tactics for high performers

**Anticipate when the work will become boring.** Productive people feel the strain of disengaging work first. A pusher will tell you directly in every 1:1; a puller will likely suffer in silence. Say the hard thing early, such as "I'm worried we won't have enough interesting projects for you in six months," and start brainstorming what's next together, including work outside the team or company if that's where the best option is (pp. 423 to 424).

**Play the person.** She frames her own approach as putting the person's path first, even when the best opportunity is outside the team or the company. Being honest about outside opportunities, rather than waiting to be caught off guard by a resignation, builds the kind of trust that makes people want to follow a leader (pp. 424 to 425).

**Remember there's more opportunity than you think.** Even without an open management role, you can hand someone a real leadership role: strategic projects, team-building work like hiring, or infrastructural work like architecture or running the business review. You can also reshape team structure to create room to grow, for example splitting one large team into three smaller ones with different managers, without shrinking teams so far they become "I" formations. Focus on the type of work a person needs for their five-year aspiration rather than fixating on title. And consider whether a high performer has outgrown their role enough that succession, or moving yourself out of your own job, is the right move (pp. 424 to 425).

**Let go.** If you can't find a high performer real opportunities on your team, don't hold on to them too long: stalling them damages the relationship, your team's output, and your team's reputation as a place to grow. Once you've reached this point, try to land them elsewhere in the company; if there's truly no fit, open your network on their behalf. Either way you earn their long-term loyalty (p. 426).

**Manage for potential, not experience.** Spend the most time on the people with the highest long-term return, not just current high performers. Her favorite puzzle is "interesting" talent: someone with a lot of upside and an unusual skill mix. Unlocking it might mean new projects, courses, or apprenticeship. She also flags people whose ideas come from an unusual angle and can feel disruptive but sometimes deliver the breakthrough a team was stuck on; her Gmail example is a team member whose unsolicited push for peer-to-peer support, once tested, became the new model (pp. 426 to 427).

**Conduct pre-exit interviews and retros.** Interview your top 10 to 20 percent of talent once a year and ask what would make them leave. Even three to five conversations surface pain points quickly, and you can reassure people you're aware of problems and working on them. If someone does leave, run a full retrospective, asking both them and their close colleagues why, since those colleagues are often high performers too (pp. 427 to 428).

**Retaining top talent.** The biggest mistake she sees is a manager deciding top talent is "good enough" and no longer challenging them; high performers like the discomfort of a steep slope. She credits her own career partly to spotting where interesting work is headed (her Gretzky line: skate to where the puck is going) and to identifying and unlocking top performers with more responsibility than anyone expected. Watch whether your team has enough exciting projects for the next six months and the next year; on specialized, small teams like business development where opportunities cap out, be explicit with people about that trade-off rather than letting them find out the hard way (pp. 428 to 429).

**Telling a high performer they won't be promoted.** It's easy to promote too fast; do a gut check on whether someone can truly sustain the next level, and if not, own the decision, tell them specifically what's missing, and treat it as fuel for a genuine high performer to improve. Her example: a talented individual contributor who hadn't yet learned to scale a team through her reports; six months of explicit work on hiring, delegating, and operating for scaled impact got her promotion-ready. If you've been generous with positive feedback but light on constructive feedback, own that gap honestly rather than pretend it isn't a lapse. In a fast-growing company where promotions come often, keep setting expectations, since promotions get rarer as people get more senior and you'll need other ways to recognize them (pp. 429 to 431).
