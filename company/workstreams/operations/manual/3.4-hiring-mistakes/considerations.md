# 3.4 Hiring mistakes: considerations

What the white paper holds, what the earlier work argued, the options, and where Sŏn may diverge from the book. In the book's order. Nothing here is a decision. "WP p. N" is the white paper; "pp." alone is the book.

## 1. When the call was wrong early

**The book.** Most hiring mistakes surface months in as a performance issue; this section is about the other case, realizing quickly, sometimes before the start date. Four signals: confidential company information posted, a misrepresented background, poor treatment of people before the start or during onboarding, arrogance or bad behavior toward new teammates in week one. Anything that violates the handbook or shows extremely poor judgment is grounds to act. "The main thing is to do something": rescind the offer or move to a swift termination, depending on the start date and the jurisdiction; a short note to the team, no overexplaining; where termination is hard, a negotiated departure beats a dragged-out process that damages the team (pp. 224 to 225).

**The white paper.** Silent on early separations. Its nearest commitments: the first question is what allowed this to happen, not who failed; management absorbs blame from staff; post-mortems are private and process-focused (WP p. 17). The candidate reads a behavioral standard before hiring (WP p. 18, "why comes before how"), so 2.1.5's standard is the nearest thing to a handbook until one exists. The two leads are parallel and neither reports to the other (WP p. 10), which matters for who acts on a lead's own hire.

**From the earlier work.** A serious violation between acceptance and the first shift was to run through the same conduct path used at any hiring stage (3.2.7), with the withdrawal handled by whoever manages employment paperwork, with counsel. A serious violation in week one was to go to both leads jointly, with next steps left to the later chunks. Departures were to be told to the team with minimal detail, consistent with the plan never naming a person's situation.

**Options for 3.4.2 (grounds).** (a) The book's four signals plus the behavioral standard, written as a short list a candidate could read in the offer. Commits 2.1.5 to a standard specific enough to be grounds. (b) Any violation of a handbook, once one exists, and until then the founders' judgment. Commits the first cohort to grounds that are not written down. (c) Only conduct that counsel confirms is grounds in Texas. Commits Sŏn to a narrower list than the book's, and to a wait on 0.1.

**Options for 3.4.3 (who acts).** (a) The domain lead acts with a partner as second reader; a lead's own hire goes to the two partners. Commits the partners to being reachable inside a day when the case arises. (b) Both leads jointly, as the earlier work had it. Commits Sŏn to a cross-domain decision for a single-domain matter, which 2.2.3 governs. (c) A partner acts in every case before opening; leads after. Commits the founders to the floor for the first cohort.

## 2. A minor issue in the first weeks

**The book.** For something minor but feedback-worthy, like chronic lateness: give the feedback quickly and directly, watch closely, and part ways with speed if it continues (p. 224).

**The white paper.** No surprises; if something is wrong, the person knows before any review (WP p. 19). Blame the process first (WP p. 17). Cooling failure: the house runs people until they stop and calls it dedication (WP p. 13), which is the reading to test before lateness is read as character.

**From the earlier work.** A minor recurring issue surfaces as a factual check-in line about what is getting in the way (the shift shape, transit, the schedule's language, the call time), not as a manager's verbal correction; only a pattern documented across check-ins that the person has already heard becomes anything more. The book's "quickly and strongly" was read as compatible with this only if "quickly" means the next check-in and the words are about the fact, not the person.

**Options for 3.4.5.** (a) The check-in line, with the process causes checked first, and a named count at which it becomes 5.2's conversation. Commits leads to waiting for the next check-in, which 2.3.10's interval sets, and to a written pattern before a harder conversation. (b) The book's path: direct feedback the same day, watch, act. Commits Sŏn to the lead's judgment and to a conversation that leaves no record. (c) The check-in line for the process causes, plus a same-day factual mention by the lead. Commits leads to two channels for one fact.

## 3. Closing the loop: the retrospective

**The book.** False negatives cannot be tracked; false positives can. When a hiring mistake happens, the hiring manager runs a quick retrospective: what should have been caught, was the capability list wrong, were there flags in feedback or references, who interviewed and referenced, was onboarding thorough. Share the learning with recruiting (p. 225).

**The white paper.** Post-mortems are private and process-focused; the system gets fixed so it does not happen again, to anyone (WP p. 17).

**From the earlier work.** An order of investigation when a hire is not holding: the training and organizational causes first (the module's language and version, the mentor's load, the paired count, the read's conditions, whether tastings happened, whether the house delivered what the person said they needed), and only after those are ruled out does it become a question about the hiring decision, which goes to whoever audits hiring calibration (3.2.39). The plan itself never concludes that the hire was a mistake.

**Options for 3.4.6.** (a) Triggered by an early reversal, an unmet plan after the extension, or a departure inside a set window; run by the hiring manager with a second reader; questions fixed; the record names the process and lives with the calibration records, never on the person page. Commits Sŏn to a retrospective for every unmet plan, not only the dramatic cases. (b) The book's shape: the hiring manager alone, informally, shared with whoever holds recruiting's functions (3.1.14). Commits Sŏn to learning that lives in one person. (c) Retrospective only for early reversals; unmet plans go to the training-infrastructure line (3.3.28) and no further. Commits Sŏn to never asking whether the hiring read was wrong when onboarding was not the cause.

## 4. Reading interviewers and the funnel

**The book.** Even a basic applicant tracking system shows time in process, funnel performance, and interviewer stats (volume, experience, decisiveness versus leniency). Welch tracked which interviewers were best and had them train others or interview alone (p. 225). Later: longitudinal performance mapped against each interviewer's recommendations (p. 226).

**The white paper.** Interviewers are trained, because asking a good question and reading the answer is a learned skill (WP p. 18). The stack is the nervous system and captures what a person would otherwise hold from memory (WP p. 21). Silent on tracking interviewers.

**From the earlier work.** 3.1.37 builds the pipeline source and stage report (time in process and the funnel are there). 3.2.13 decides what makes an interviewer trained and what sends them back, and whether Sŏn has a selected interviewer class. 3.2.39 decides the calibration read. What remains for this chunk is the interviewer read over time: whether an interviewer's hire and no-hire recommendations are read against later plan outcomes, and what may be done with that. The earlier work's constraint: nothing that reads a person is ever a ranking, and the read serves training, not standing.

**Options for 3.4.7.** (a) Read volume, decisiveness, agreement with the decision, and later plan outcomes per interviewer; seen by the calibration read's runner and the interviewer themselves; may change training and class membership, never pay or standing. Commits the calibration record (3.2.41) to carrying the interviewer's recommendation in a form that can be joined later. (b) Volume and decisiveness only, from the scorecards. Commits Sŏn to knowing who is lenient but not whose judgment holds. (c) No interviewer read; the calibration read at the time of hire is enough. Commits Sŏn to never learning which reads were right.

## 5. Asking the team

**The book.** Add hiring questions to the engagement survey (my team embodies the principles; I would recommend the company; we consistently hire excellent people; when someone is not delivering, we act), or run a short pulse during rapid hiring (new people are onboarded well; recent hires meet the team's caliber; the people I work with are ambitious, kind, right for critical roles) (pp. 225 to 226).

**The white paper.** Employee NPS tracked regularly rather than annually (WP p. 22). Engagement across psychological safety and values alignment; retention and internal promotion as evidence the commitments are real (WP p. 23). 2.3.15 sets the pulse's rhythm.

**From the earlier work.** The onboarding read (3.3.27) computes what the pulse's onboarding question would ask an impression about. The remaining book questions ask the team about caliber and about whether the house acts when someone is not delivering; those cannot be computed, and the earlier work kept them for the pulse.

**Options for 3.4.9.** (a) Add the caliber and acting questions, drop the onboarding question because the read computes it. Commits the pulse to two or three more items. (b) Add the book's full set. Commits Sŏn to asking impressions of things it measures. (c) Add none; the eNPS question and the onboarding read cover it. Commits Sŏn to not hearing the team's view of who is being hired.

## 6. Joining hiring to what happens after

**The book.** Surveys and the ATS point you somewhere; the insight comes from connecting hiring data to what happens after someone joins. A light version: a manager survey at six weeks, twelve weeks, and six months. A heavy version: sentiment analysis of interview feedback against later performance. Also interviewer recommendations against longitudinal performance (p. 226).

**The white paper.** Advancement is tied to demonstrated mastery; reviews develop rather than judge (WP p. 19). Heavily deterministic software over a thin layer of AI; AI confined to analysis on top, never load-bearing (WP p. 21). Silent on this join.

**From the earlier work.** The join was to be made from facts already recorded, plan milestones against the hiring record, rather than from a manager's impression at set dates; the manager milestone survey was rejected because it asks for an impression where a fact exists, and text mining of interview notes was rejected as reading people through a machine's guess at their words. The resulting read carries no name and no target.

**Options for 3.4.10.** (a) Plan milestones and later facts against hiring records, computed, per seat class and stage, no names, no impression survey, no text mining. Commits 3.2.41 and 3.3.41 to storing things in joinable form from the first hire. (b) The book's light version: a manager survey at milestones. Commits Sŏn to impressions and to leads filling forms. (c) Both, with the survey as a check on the data. Commits Sŏn to reconciling them.

## 7. Asking the people Sŏn said no to

**The book.** Survey anyone rejected from the first interview onward. Stripe uses an adjusted Net Promoter Score on whether the candidate would still recommend applying, plus feedback on the recruiter, the interviews, and the process; the response rate is modest and the results are not uniformly positive, but they improve the process for everyone after. A rejected candidate's experience is still a customer experience (p. 226).

**The white paper.** No one is ghosted; every candidate receives a clear answer, including the no; a candidate treated well at the no returns to the pool next year (WP p. 18). The process respects the candidate's time and lets them choose language and format (WP p. 17).

**From the earlier work.** 3.1.11 sets the response commitments and 3.2.21 decides what a declined candidate is told and by whom; 3.1.12 decides how a declined candidate enters the pool. The earlier work assumed a short candidate pulse at every decision. What remains is whether Sŏn asks, from which stage, what, and the rule that a response never changes the candidate's pool standing.

**Options for 3.4.8.** (a) From the first interview onward, three questions at most, sent after the decline message, read monthly, stored apart from the file. Commits the applicant system (3.1.33) to a survey step and someone to reading it. (b) From the practical onward only. Commits Sŏn to hearing from fewer people who invested more. (c) None until volume justifies it. Commits Sŏn to the first year's declines going unheard.

## 8. Where Sŏn may diverge from the book, in one place

- **Grounds are tied to a written behavioral standard rather than a handbook**, because 2.1.5 exists before a handbook does (cons. 1).
- **A minor early issue is raised as a fact at a check-in, with the process causes checked first,** where the book has the manager give direct feedback the same day (cons. 2).
- **The retrospective is triggered by an unmet plan, not only by an obvious mistake,** and it never concludes anything about the person (cons. 3).
- **The interviewer read serves training and never standing** (cons. 4).
- **The join to later outcomes is computed from plan facts, not gathered from a manager survey,** and interview notes are never text-mined (cons. 6).
- **A declined candidate's survey response never changes their pool standing** (cons. 7).
- **The book's closing argument stands:** this is the part of hiring most likely to pay off beyond expectations, and Sŏn's version is cheaper than the book's because most of the data is already captured by the person page and the calibration record (pp. 226 to 227).
