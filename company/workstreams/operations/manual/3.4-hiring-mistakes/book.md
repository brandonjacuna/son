# 3.4 Hiring mistakes: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 3, pp. 224 to 227, with related exercises at pp. 229 to 252. Full text: `extraction/build/s07/book.md`, `extraction/build/s07/exercises.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## When you realize you made the wrong call

Most hiring mistakes surface months into someone's tenure as a performance issue (covered in her Chapter 5 material on performance). This section is about the other case: realizing quickly, sometimes before the person's start date, that the decision was wrong (p. 224).

Signals that might reverse a hiring decision (p. 224):

- Posting confidential company information on social media.
- Misrepresenting some part of their background or experience.
- Treating people poorly before their start date or during onboarding.
- Displaying arrogance or bad behavior toward new teammates in the first week.

Her general rule: anything that violates the employee handbook, or shows extremely poor judgment, is grounds to act. For something more minor but still feedback-worthy, like chronic lateness, give the feedback quickly and directly, watch closely, and move to part ways with speed if it continues (p. 224).

What to actually do: the main thing is to do something. Depending on the start date and jurisdiction, that usually means rescinding the offer or moving toward a swift termination. There's no need to overexplain internally; a short note to the team saying the person will no longer be taking the role is enough. Where swift termination is harder, look at a negotiated departure, even if it involves a payout. Paying to separate cleanly beats dragging out a process that damages the team (pp. 224 to 225).

## Closing the hiring loop

False negatives (people you rejected who would have been great) are nearly impossible to track. False positives are not. At the individual level, when a hiring mistake happens, the hiring manager should run a quick retrospective: what should have been caught, was the role's desired capabilities wrong, were there flags in feedback or references, who ran the interviews and reference calls, was onboarding thorough. Sharing the learning with a recruiting partner helps avoid repeating it (p. 225).

The bigger opportunity is a company-wide, data-driven approach to improving hiring. Her Jack Welch example: as CEO of General Electric, he reportedly tracked which interviewers were best and made sure they trained others or were the only ones allowed to interview (p. 225).

Even a basic applicant tracking system (ATS) can surface useful data (p. 225):

- **Time in process.** Are some roles or teams slower to hire, and why?
- **Funnel performance.** The recruiting equivalent of sales metrics: are teams hiring too few or too many relative to benchmarks?
- **Interviewer stats.** Who interviews most, what's their tenure and experience, are they decisive (willing to give a strong hire or no-hire) or overly lenient?

Beyond the ATS, she suggests adding hiring-process questions to the regular engagement survey, or running a short pulse survey during periods of rapid hiring to check whether the hiring process and the environment new hires create still honor the company's principles (p. 225).

Sample engagement-survey prompts, rated 1 to 5 from strongly disagree to strongly agree (pp. 225 to 226):

- The people on my team embody the company principles (optionally, list each principle).
- I would recommend the company as a great place to work.
- We consistently hire excellent new team members.
- When it's clear someone isn't delivering, we do something about it.

Sample pulse-survey prompts for periods of rapid hiring (p. 226):

- New people on my team are being onboarded quickly and effectively.
- The people we've hired on my team in the last six months meet or exceed the caliber of our current team.
- The people I work with are ambitious and hardworking.
- The people I work with are kind and selfless; they optimize for the company and team, not themselves.
- The people in the most critical roles on my team are the right people for the job.

## Marrying hiring data to performance data

Surveys and ATS data point you somewhere; the real insight comes from connecting hiring-process data to what happens after someone joins. This is a heavier lift, but automating it across the ATS, HR information systems, and formal performance reviews (Chapter 5) pays off as the company grows. Two things worth looking at (p. 226):

- New hire performance in the first 12 months against their performance in the hiring process. A lighter version: a quick manager survey at set milestones (6 weeks, 12 weeks, 6 months). A heavier version: sentiment analysis on interview feedback (for example, positive use of the word "resilient") mapped against later performance, to sharpen the interview rubric over time.
- Longitudinal performance data mapped against individual interviewers' hire and no-hire recommendations, to see whose judgment holds up.

## Surveying rejected candidates

Counterintuitive but worth doing: survey people you rejected, not from early screens but anyone who reached the first interview stage or beyond. The results won't be uniformly positive, but they improve the process for everyone who comes after (p. 226).

Stripe's method: an adjusted Net Promoter Score on a 1 to 10 scale, subtracting the percentage giving 0 to 6 from the percentage giving 9 or 10, asking whether the rejected candidate would still recommend applying to Stripe. They also collect feedback on the recruiter, the interview experience, and the overall process. Response rate runs 20 to 30 percent, with an adjusted NPS around 7. Her framing: a rejected candidate's experience is still a customer experience, worth treating as seriously as any other feedback, sting of rejection included (p. 226).

Her closing point: this level of investment in hiring can look excessive to a fast-growing company, but of all the chapter's advice, this is the part most likely to pay off beyond expectations. Hiring is expensive, but talent is everything; treat it as the foundation the rest of the house is built on (pp. 226 to 227).

## Related exercises (pp. 229 to 252)

These sit earlier in the chapter's appendix but support building and correcting a hiring process:

- **Interview framework and rubric (p. 229).** A worked example for recruiting a recruiter, naming competencies assessed at each interview stage.
- **Sample interview questions (pp. 246 to 248).** Organized by theme (working with others, resilience, leadership), each with what the question is seeking and follow-ups.
- **Written exercise example (pp. 248 to 250).** A product manager writing prompt with a scored rubric (written communication, data analysis, customer sense, product sense) and a points-to-decision table.
- **Candidate review: decision-making framework (pp. 250 to 252).** A five-step hiring-committee process: candidate submission, scorecard and rationale review, scorecard verification, discussion and decision, and outcome submission with a feedback loop back to interviewers.
