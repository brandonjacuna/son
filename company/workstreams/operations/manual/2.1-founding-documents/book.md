# 2.1 Founding documents: what the book says

Full text: `sources/extraction/s02/book.md`, `sources/extraction/pilot-2.1/book-exercises-145-150.md`, `sources/extraction/s02/workbook.md`. ## The frame: a house with three parts

Every company exists inside a planning and accountability framework with three parts: founding documents, an operating system, and an operating cadence. She compares them to a house: the founding documents are the foundations, the operating system the structural support beams, the operating cadence the mechanical components (pp. 71, 73). Chapter 2 builds the house in that order. This section covers only the foundations.

## What founding documents are

Founding documents lay out the plan for the whole enterprise: the long-term goals, the principles, and the company philosophy, "why and how you exist and operate" (p. 71). They contain four things: a mission, long-term goals, principles (often called values), and team charters (p. 73).

Why they matter (p. 72):

- They give every operating system a common purpose. Systems built on top of clear founding documents flow from the same objectives and can be replicated up and down the company as it grows.
- They hold in chaos. When things go wrong, purpose and culture are what people steer by.
- They make expectations explicit. When values are known to all, it is easier to give feedback when someone is not meeting them.

When to write them: "relatively early on, once you've established some traction." They become a more important touchstone once a company grows past about 40 or 50 people. Once the reason for being is communicated, attention turns to building the first company-wide operating systems (p. 71).

## Mission

A mission states why you exist (p. 73). Her Stripe story: the Collisons had never formalized one, but a phrase Patrick had written on an early About page, "to increase the GDP of the internet," kept being quoted by employees and candidates until it was simply accepted as the mission. A mission that is not pinned down will usually make itself known (pp. 73 to 74).

Two tests (p. 74):

- **Descriptive.** It is uniquely specific. Another organization should not be able to hold the same mission.
- **Aspirational.** It is unlikely ever to be fully achieved. Microsoft's "a computer on every desk and in every home" and Google's mission are her examples: bold enough to break down into milestones a company would need to hit on the way (pp. 74 to 75).

Missions ladder. Every part of the stack has one: the team's mission serves the division's, the division's serves the company's (p. 75). Her Stripe examples run from company mission down to divisional (Design, Operations) and team missions (Logistics, Foundation, Demand) (pp. 75 to 76). Individuals may have missions too, though those shift as roles change; what matters is that each person knows how their work contributes to the team, the division, and the company (p. 76). Her Microsoft illustration: company mission (a computer on every desk), division mission (build the operating system), team mission (develop the graphical interface) (p. 76).

Her quoted executives: Charles Phillips on purpose being everything, and people expecting more from their company than they used to (p. 74); Eric Yuan on his biggest early mistake being the failure to write down business principles on hiring, firing, performance, and security, because delegation depends on them (p. 76).

## Long-term goals

The bigger-picture ambitions the company hopes to achieve or improve over a period of years (p. 76). Stripe's fit on a two-page document written in 2014 and were unchanged eight years later; examples include growing internet-enabled commerce and accelerating globalization (p. 77). Two points:

- They bolster the mission and give employees context on the company's aspirations and reason for existence (p. 77).
- They are the parent of every shorter-term goal. Company, division, and team goals set quarterly and annually ladder up to them, so having them is "a necessary precursor" to the goal-setting the rest of the chapter covers (p. 77).

## Principles

Mission says why you exist; long-term goals say what you hope to achieve; principles (values) establish the culture that lets you work toward them. Stripe calls them principles because the word connotes a shared system of beliefs and behaviors. Whatever they are called, they should run through all of the company's actions, individual and collective (p. 77).

Her tests: principles must feel authentic to the identity the company is developing organically. Like a brand, they should be relevant, believable, enduring, and deliverable. A little aspiration is fine, but principles that are so idealistic they have no connection to how the company actually operates do not resonate. Her example: a value of caring about customers, held by a company that only knows how many customers it has (pp. 77 to 78).

Why they are hard to write: Schein's three levels of culture (artifacts, espoused values, basic underlying assumptions). Principles try to write down both the visible espoused beliefs and the invisible assumptions (p. 78).

Her drafting method (pp. 78 to 79):

1. Write down notable moments in the company's history: major decisions, critical product choices, rallying moments. Ask what was true in those moments and what belief system prevailed. Start the draft there.
2. Seek input from across the organization. At Stripe, early drafts were written by employees; Patrick reviewed them, drafted his own version, and circulated it for comment before the first official set.
3. Revisit every year or two to keep them authentic and relevant.
4. The test that matters most: are they truly expressed in how the company hires, rewards, leads, and conducts itself.

### Sidebar: a quick guide to Stripe's culture (pp. 79 to 82)

An excerpt from a document Stripe shared with every candidate who reached the in-person interview. Its premise: fulfillment depends on alignment between your values and the organization's, culture is hard to assess from outside, and companies have incentives to say attractive things rather than true ones. So the guide tries to be an honest description of the culture as it is. Its three headings, each pairing the appeal with the cost:

- **We haven't won yet.** The important problems are still unsolved, so it is not too late to matter; and success is far from assured, because most companies at that stage plateau.
- **Move with urgency and focus.** Users' livelihoods depend on Stripe, so slowness matters. Flexibility on when and where to work, but also late nights, some weekends, and cross-timezone meetings. Motivated colleagues raise the bar; comparison can be stressful. Competitive in the sense of pushing a high bar higher, not winner-takes-all. High autonomy, high performers recognized, but no rigid career paths.
- **Think rigorously.** Reason from first principles, seek the best version of the opposing argument, look for the truth in criticism, learn from other fields, and stay judicious: hard discussions, no yelling.

## Team charters

The additional founding document she asks teams to create (p. 83). A team mission is one or two sentences; a team charter is about a page and creates clarity about why the team exists, what its long-term goals are, and what others can expect from it, immediately and over the long term.

The trigger: when a company grows quickly or a division is large, it becomes surprisingly hard to know who is responsible for what work, and to what end. A good manager simplifies by laying out the jobs to be done; the charter is the summary of those jobs (p. 83).

Where it lives: somewhere discoverable, ideally the intranet. Mission and charter together should say what the team does, why it matters to the company, its key metrics, and its major risks and dependencies. Bonus points for a link to a metrics dashboard and a guide to the best way to contact and work with the team (p. 83).

Her template (pp. 148 to 150; workbook pp. 15 to 22), with a dashboard-security team as the worked example:

| Section | What it holds |
|---|---|
| Mission | What the team is responsible for, one or two sentences |
| Vision | What the team is aiming to become for its users and the company, over years |
| Customers | Who the team serves and what it owes them |
| Metrics | Each metric with what it measures and a target value |
| Strategic importance | Why the work matters to the company beyond its direct results |
| Major risks | What could pull the team off course or break it |
| Provided interfaces | What the team hands to others |
| Dependent interfaces | What the team depends on from others |

## Exercises and templates

**Stripe's operating principles (pp. 145 to 147; workbook pp. 12 to 14).** The December 2021 set, given as a worked example. Three groups: how we work (users first; move with urgency and focus; be meticulous in your craft; seek feedback; deliver outstanding results), who we are (curious, resilient, humble, macro-optimistic, exothermic), and leaders (obsess over talent; elevate ambitions; set the pace and energy; make decisions, be accountable; lead with clarity and context; solve problems). Plus a set of older slogans kept as guideposts (we haven't won yet; efficiency is leverage; be meticulous about the foundations; disagree and commit; really, really, really care; operate from first principles; be politely relentless). Each principle is a short name followed by one or two sentences on what it means in practice. The workbook leaves a notes area under each group for your own.

**Team charter (pp. 148 to 150; workbook pp. 15 to 22).** The template above, one workbook page per section with the example and a notes area.

**Organizational foundations (p. 150; workbook p. 22 onward).** A table to test whether you have a clear operating system: can you fill it in for a report, a team, and your division, and is the information documented and easy to find. Her point: if you struggle to fill it in, imagine how your teams feel. This exercise serves the operating system (chunk 2.2) more than the founding documents; it is noted here because the book places it with the Chapter 2 templates.
