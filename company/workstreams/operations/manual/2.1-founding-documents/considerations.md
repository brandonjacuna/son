# 2.1 Founding documents: considerations

What is worth knowing before doing the work in `tasks.md`. Topics follow the book's order. "WP p. N" is the white paper (`sources/extraction/s01/record.md`). Nothing here is a decision; where the old synthesis argued a position it appears as an option with its reasoning and what it commits Sŏn to.

## 1. Founding documents as a whole

### What the white paper holds

The white paper is itself a founding document in her sense: it states why the company exists, what it is building toward, and how it operates, at full depth (WP p. 02). It is written for an investor, it argues rather than states, and it says of itself that it carries "no ask, no terms, and no figures" (WP p. 02). It is not a document a new hire can read before a first shift.

The white paper also incurs two commitments that only founding documents can pay:

- Onboarding: "Why comes before how. Orientation to the mission, the values, and the behavioral standard happens before operational training" (WP p. 18). That orientation needs a mission and values that exist in writing.
- The company is "built so it does not depend on either founder's memory" (WP p. 05 to 06) and its founders' mark is "a company built to run without them" (WP p. 06). A mission and principles that live only in the founders' heads are the memory failure the white paper diagnoses (WP p. 13).

The white paper is silent on a third document that already exists outside it: the operating agreement Brandon and Dominic have worked on. It governs ownership, capital, and the legal authority of the principals and has not been read by this program.

### Considerations

**Her timing rule does not fire; a different one does.** She says founding documents matter most beyond 40 or 50 people, because they replace founder proximity as headcount dilutes it. Sŏn opens near full size and stays there: "roughly eight leadership lines running the full span of the day" (WP p. 22), and no one will be far from a founder. What varies at Sŏn is not headcount but the hour (four service periods under one name, WP p. 33), the domain (three founding seats, WP p. 03), the phase (dinner runs alone for at least a quarter before anything is added, WP p. 36), and the seat that arrives late (the chef partner is still being searched for, WP p. 29). A founding document at Sŏn is what lets a decision made while dinner ran alone reach a late-night team that did not exist yet, and what tells the chef partner what was settled before them and what was held for them.

**The operating agreement is upstream.** Anything the founding documents say about who owns what, how partners decide, or how a principal joins or leaves either restates the agreement, contradicts it, or defers to it. Only the third is safe. Reading the agreement first is cheap; discovering a contradiction later is not.

**Documents that name tools date.** The white paper names specific vendors for the data hub and for payroll and benefits (WP pp. 20, 21), and at least one of those is already out of use. A founding document that names a tool, a vendor, or the address of another document dates the day the tool changes. Commitments and owners age better than systems of record.

**Figures live elsewhere.** The white paper carries no figures by design (WP p. 02) and names the compensation percentage as "still being finalized" (WP pp. 20, 22). Founding documents that carry a figure are re-versioned every time the figure moves.

**Open items are a feature.** The white paper keeps its open items visible (WP p. 15, p. 22) and says why: "a settled-looking answer to an open question is the tell of a deck that has not been pressure-tested" (WP p. 22). A founding document can do the same, listing what is open and who closes it, which is also how the chef partner sees their inheritance on day one.

### Options on scope

**Option A: one short founding document for the company.** Mission, long-term goals, principles, the founding seats, how the partners decide, and open items, in a few pages, read by every new person before their first shift. Commits Sŏn to writing it before the first orientation and keeping it current.

**Option B: the white paper serves as the founding document, with a short internal summary.** Commits Sŏn to maintaining a 39-page investor document as the record of intent, and to the summary drifting from it.

**Option C: separate documents per element** (a mission page, a principles page, seat descriptions), each discoverable on the internal home. Commits Sŏn to more surfaces to version, but each one small.

The old synthesis argued A and gave the document a private name. The name is Brandon's to choose; the function is the same under any name.

## 2. Mission

### What the white paper holds

No mission sentence. The white paper holds a thesis and several sentences that sound like one:

- "A restaurant, read correctly, is not a room that serves food" (WP p. 02). A negation; it says what Sŏn is not.
- Three things a restaurant can be built to compound: the organization, the customer, the brand (WP pp. 02, 06). An investment thesis; any company that read the same argument could hold it.
- "The restaurant is the proof of concept. The connective layer is the asset" (WP p. 34). A statement about the company's structure, not its purpose.
- "Sŏn fights for every hour" (WP p. 33). A positioning line.
- "A portfolio adds restaurants. A platform compounds customers" (WP p. 38). A contrast, not a purpose.

On the entity: the parent company "is not a brand and is not named in customer-facing contexts," and "Sŏn is the only brand the customer meets at the building" (WP p. 36). The building is proving that the layer beneath it can hold more (WP pp. 35 to 36, 38).

### Considerations

**Whose mission.** Her descriptive test says the parent company and the restaurant cannot share one mission. A mission for Sŏn the restaurant is a mission for the proof of concept. A mission for the company has to be true before the layer has held anything but Sŏn, without naming what it will hold, because the white paper's discipline is "to never assert the unproven, to a customer or to a reader" (WP p. 36). This is the hardest constraint on the sentence.

**Her tests, plus two the white paper adds.** Descriptive (no other organization could hold it), aspirational (never fully achieved), never assert the unproven (WP p. 36), and not a description of the restaurant alone (WP p. 34).

**Whether the cuisine is in it.** Korean food is the category Sŏn enters (WP p. 37). Whether the company's mission names it, or whether the cuisine lives one level down in Sŏn's own mission and the service-period charters, is a fork the white paper does not close. Naming it commits the company mission to the cuisine when the layer later holds other concepts (WP p. 36).

**The Stripe pattern may apply.** Stripe's mission was a phrase from an early About page that people kept quoting until it was accepted (pp. 73 to 74). Sŏn plans a documented build across platforms for a year or more before opening (WP pp. 29 to 31). A sentence that the audience keeps repeating during that build would be a mission that "made itself known," and it would arrive with the test of use already passed.

**The ladder.** If the company holds the mission, Sŏn the restaurant holds one beneath it, each service period holds a reason to exist beneath that (WP p. 33 to 34), and each person knows how their work contributes (p. 76). The white paper's inverted web (WP p. 11) is how the individual level already works: a person's development adds threads to the whole.

## 3. Long-term goals

### What the white paper holds

A well-formed set, none carrying a figure, though none labeled as long-term goals:

- The three engines: an organization that compounds what the company knows, a customer relationship that compounds, a brand that compounds earned attention (WP p. 06).
- A company built to run without its founders (WP pp. 05 to 06).
- One restaurant, coherent across the whole day, with dinner protected (WP p. 33).
- The proof the building exists to deliver: that the layer can take a customer who entered in the morning and bring them to late night (WP p. 35).
- The horizon beyond the building: further concepts by category and a membership tier, "gated on the proof, never a promise made before the building earns it" (WP pp. 36, 38).
- What it measures itself by: employee NPS, cultural labor score, customer recognition rate (WP p. 22); retention, internal promotion, engagement, participation in development (WP p. 23).

### Considerations

**Selection, not invention.** The white paper has more candidates than a two-page document needs. The work is choosing which are goals, which are metrics that serve them (chunk 2.2), and which are structural design (chunk 4.1).

**Leading over lagging.** The white paper rejects revenue, labor percentage, and food cost as the primary read: "All lagging indicators. By the time they signal a problem, the damage is done" (WP p. 22). A long-term goal stated as a lagging indicator contradicts the record. Every metric set in 2.2 needs a parent here.

**Where Sŏn may diverge from the book.** Stripe's goals read the same eight years on. Sŏn's horizon is explicitly gated and optional (WP p. 38). One option is to state the horizon as a goal in category terms only, as the white paper does; another is to keep it out of the founding document entirely and let the building's proof open it. The white paper supports either; it names the categories but not the concepts (WP p. 36).

## 4. Principles

### What the white paper holds

No principles list, but a dense set of operating tenets stated as mechanics, most of them already in subject-verb form. The ones the old synthesis surfaced, by page:

- Authority lives in the domain; the person who owns the domain is accountable to its outcomes (WP pp. 07 to 08).
- Knowledge lives in the system, not in people (WP pp. 07 to 08, 13).
- Bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results; "mechanical, not aspirational" (WP pp. 12, 16 to 17).
- Labor is an asset to invest in, not a cost to minimize (WP p. 16).
- Employees are the users (WP p. 17).
- When something goes wrong, the first question is what allowed it, not who failed; management absorbs blame from staff; post-mortems are private and process-focused (WP p. 17).
- Respect for the candidate's time; speed is respect; no one is ghosted (WP pp. 17 to 18).
- Why comes before how (WP p. 18).
- Advancement requires preparation, not permission (WP pp. 11, 14).
- If it matters to the experience, Sŏn pays for it; costs are not shifted to employees (WP pp. 19 to 20).
- No hierarchy of importance: the white paper itself calls this "one operating principle" (WP p. 20).
- Bought where the capability is settled, built where it is the edge (WP p. 21).
- "Customer" is the word, deliberately, because it keeps the transaction and the accountability visible (WP p. 25).
- Staff can be generous without asking permission (WP p. 26).
- The customer never feels at fault, even when they are (WP p. 26).
- Dinner is what everything protects (WP p. 33).
- Never assert the unproven (WP p. 36).

### Considerations

**Sŏn has decisions, not moments.** Her drafting method starts from notable moments in the company's history. Sŏn has no operating history. What it has is a record of decisions, and each of the tenets above is one. Version one of the principles can be drawn from decisions; her method becomes available only after the company has run. That argues for marking version one as pre-opening and revisiting it on a schedule (section 8 below).

**Three registers, not one.** The white paper's tenets are not all principles in her sense. Some are commitments (pay for what matters; benefits from the start, WP p. 20). Some are operating rules (advancement requires preparation; the generosity range). Some are principles that describe a behavior anyone can observe in a service (management absorbs blame; the customer never feels at fault). A later session tested seven candidates against "does it name a behavior a service produces, observable without inferring a state of the person," and found five passed and two were commitments or rules rather than principles. Keeping the three registers separate matters because principles are what hiring scores against and feedback references (chunks 3.x and 5.x); a commitment or a rule is enforced differently.

**Deliverable, by construction.** Her failure mode is the principle that sounds good and does not connect to operation. A principle written as what the company does or refuses, traceable to a decision already made, passes her deliverability test by construction. "Management absorbs blame from staff" is deliverable. "We value humility" is not.

**The protection order is the sharpest one.** "Bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results" (WP p. 12) can be read two ways. As a description of how health produces results, it costs nothing. As a rule that decides trade-offs, it has operating consequences: a short-staffed night is answered by reducing covers rather than thinning the floor; a profitable service period that consumes the team's bandwidth is restructured before it is defended on results. The white paper leans toward the rule: "This is mechanical, not aspirational" (WP pp. 16 to 17). Adopting it as a rule is a real commitment with real cost and is worth deciding on purpose rather than inheriting.

**People and compensation commitments.** The white paper states these case by case: labor as asset (WP p. 16); pay for what matters (WP pp. 19 to 20); dignity as a precondition for presence (WP p. 20); compensation built into menu price, never a service charge, never presented as one, distributed to the whole team including the kitchen (WP p. 20); cross-strand fluency recognized in pay (WP p. 11); benefits from the start (WP p. 20). The old synthesis argued for stating these as a philosophy in the founding document and leaving every mechanism (the percentage, point weights, payment rhythm) to compensation (chunk 5.5). Three reasons: a philosophy reaches cases the record did not anticipate ("there is never a need to adjudicate what deserves care, because the answer is always yes," WP p. 20); recruiting and hiring need to state the commitments before the mechanics are set (WP p. 18 hands every candidate a sheet with compensation on it); and a founding document that carries mechanics dates when the percentage is finalized. Choosing this commits Sŏn to Dominic reviewing the commitment list before the architecture is built against it.

**The chef partner's contribution.** Whether the culinary domain adds a principle of its own is a question for the chef partner once seated. The white paper gives the chef partner the culinary vision (WP p. 03) and does not describe a kitchen principle.

**Her culture guide has a Sŏn parallel.** Stripe handed every in-person candidate an honest description of the culture, costs included (pp. 79 to 82). The white paper already commits to handing every candidate a single sheet with the org chart, their direct lead, compensation and benefits, communication expectations, the training plan, and the advancement path, "nothing withheld" (WP p. 18). An honest culture description is the missing companion to that sheet: what a late-night period asks, what a morning start asks, what a phased opening means for a role, and what a first-time founding team is (WP p. 05).

**Her quoted executives apply directly.** Eric Yuan's regret was not writing down principles on hiring, firing, performance, and security, because delegation depends on them (p. 76). Sŏn's structure delegates outright: authority lives in the domain, and the two leads serve the founders' vision without reporting to each other (WP p. 10). That delegation runs on written principles or it runs on the founders being in the building.

## 5. The founding seats

The book's ladder runs company, division, team, individual. At Sŏn the level below the company is three founding seats, each owning a domain (WP p. 03). The book does not ask for founder job descriptions in this section, but her Chapter 1 checklist asks whether you understand your own job description, and at Sŏn there is no document to understand. The seat descriptions are also the prerequisite for the chef partner's seat: a partner cannot be told what they own until the other two seats are written.

### What the white paper holds

- Brandon: the concept, the operating systems, and the experience; the public voice and the origin of the operating concepts (WP p. 03).
- Dominic: the capital, the financial architecture, and the technology build; "the CFO who owns the financial model and the capital architecture, and the CTO who owns the data architecture and the technology build" (WP pp. 03, 05).
- The chef partner: "owns the culinary vision as an equal founding partner, freed from carrying the rest of the business alone" (WP p. 03); "the chef partner own[s] the food as a founding seat rather than carry the whole business" (WP p. 05); an equal vote on whether lunch is ever built (WP p. 36); "The culinary vision is real and it is led by the chef partner, but the business does not rest on the survival of any one person in it" (WP p. 37).
- Below the seats: the Operations Lead and the Maitre d as parallel co-leads, neither reporting to the other, serving "the culinary and conceptual vision" (WP p. 10). The Chef Partner "owns culinary execution during service, which is not the same as culinary direction overall" (WP p. 10).
- The seat descriptions are load-bearing in the white paper's own terms: domain ownership must be "small, specific, explicitly defined, interlocking" (WP p. 09), and "the absence of a clear path is where politics begin" (WP p. 11).

### Considerations

**Two seams the white paper leaves open.** First, the OS surface. Brandon owns "the operating systems" (WP p. 03); Dominic owns "the technology build" and "the data architecture" (WP p. 05); the proprietary layer of the stack is "the OS surface and the agent layer" (WP pp. 21 to 22). Three assignments land on one object and the record never says who owns what it must do versus how it is built. Second, culinary direction overall: WP p. 10 separates execution from direction, assigns execution, and does not say who holds direction. These are exactly the both-own-it failures the white paper predicts (WP p. 11), findable in its own text.

**Options on the OS surface seam.** (a) Brandon owns the specification (what the surface must do, what the agents decide, what a lead sees at the start of a shift) and Dominic owns the build and its operation, with a stated path for requests the specification did not anticipate. Commits each half to its closest competent owner and pre-states the boundary. (b) One seat owns it outright. Commits the other seat to a request path for everything. The white paper's own pattern (authority with the domain owner, WP p. 08) fits (a); the ruling needs both founders.

**The chef seat, after the brand material is set aside.** The old synthesis found a conflict between the white paper's "chef partner" and a brand document's "executive chef hire." With the brand material out of scope, the white paper's account stands alone: the seat is a founding partner. What remains open is what the white paper's own hedge means for a partner: the business "does not rest on the survival of any one person" (WP p. 37) and is built to run without its founders (WP p. 06). For a partner seat that means an absence rule and knowledge held in the system, the same as the other two seats, not a hiring criterion. A related fact: the chef search is live and is part of the documented build (WP p. 29), so the seat will be filled by someone who joins a company that has already decided a great deal.

**What a seat description contains, one option.** Domain owned; outcomes the seat is accountable to (stated as outcomes, without figures, per WP p. 08); decisions held alone; decisions shared with the other partners; what the seat does not own; seams with the other seats, resolved or marked open; how the seat's authority reaches the two leads without becoming a reporting line; and an absence rule. What it leaves out: the founder's strengths, working style, and failure modes, which are each founder's own to write and belong to chunk 6.x.

## 6. How the partners decide together

The book's decision-making frameworks belong to chunk 2.2. What belongs here is the top of the structure: how three equal partners make the decisions that are theirs jointly, and what happens when they disagree.

### What the white paper holds

- One stated case: whether lunch is ever built "is an equal decision among all three partners, the chef partner included" (WP p. 36).
- An applied pattern, never stated as a rule: authority is assigned to the closest competent owner, the range inside which that owner acts without asking is stated in advance, and escalation triggers are named before the event. Visible in the server's "authorized judgment and generosity range" (WP p. 13), the pre-authorized generosity on the floor (WP p. 26), the content approval framework's three tiers (WP p. 31), and the two leads' parallel domains (WP p. 10). The general rule routes to 2.2.
- Silence on a deadlock rule, on which decisions are reserved to all partners, and on how two partners decide before the third is seated.

### Considerations

**Two equal votes have no majority.** Until the chef partner is seated there are two partners. Every joint decision in this manual runs through that interim, so an interim rule is needed now, not when the third seat fills.

**Candidate reserved classes, drawn from the record.** Building or removing a service period (WP p. 36 already treats lunch this way); changing the founding documents; admitting a new principal; opening a further concept, which the white paper gates on proof (WP p. 38); naming, which the white paper sets aside as a separate session (WP p. 15); and setting the compensation parameters, which the white paper names as open (WP pp. 20, 22). Later sessions proposed adding a decision about a person's staying after a serious line is crossed; that belongs to chunk 5.x and is noted here only so the list is complete.

**Deadlock options.** Majority; unanimity for a named class and majority elsewhere; or domain-owner-decides after mandatory consultation, with the status quo as the default when no decision is reached. Each commits the partners differently: majority makes any two partners able to bind the third on their own domain; unanimity for a class makes the class immovable without all three; domain-owner-decides keeps authority in the domain even at the top. The operating agreement may already hold a voting rule, which is why reading it comes first.

## 7. Team charters

### What the white paper holds

Sŏn has no divisions. It has domains with named owners: Operations Lead, Maitre d, Lead Host, Chef Partner (execution), Head of Beverage, Station Leads, the Steward (WP pp. 10 to 11); two strands, outward-facing and inward-facing, that are "not a promotion sequence" (WP pp. 10 to 11); and about eight leadership lines across the whole day (WP p. 22). Below the leads the structure is "strands, not levels" (WP p. 10).

It also has a unit her book does not: the service period. Three core periods (early morning coffee, dinner, late night), with lunch and brunch "held in reserve, not part of the core and not assumed" (WP pp. 35 to 36). Each period has a different first customer (WP p. 35); the morning is "the lowest-friction way for a customer to meet Sŏn" (WP p. 05, 36); late night is "dinner's own later register" (WP p. 35); dinner "is what everything protects" (WP p. 33). The failure the white paper diagnoses in its peer is precisely a charter failure: "A daypart added as an afterthought, without its own reason to exist and its own operating logic, becomes a worse version of dinner, however well it is executed" (WP pp. 33 to 34). And the opening is phased: dinner alone for at least a quarter, then most likely early morning, then late night; lunch not revisited before a full year and removable from the plan entirely (WP p. 36).

### Considerations

**Her trigger does not fire; her question does.** Her trigger is headcount and division size. At eight leadership lines that never fires. But her question, "who is responsible for what work, and to what end," fires at Sŏn along two axes: the domain (answered by domain ownership, WP p. 09) and the hour (unanswered until each period has its reason to exist and operating logic written down).

**Options on the unit.**

- **Option A: a charter per domain** (kitchen, floor, beverage, operations), on her template. Commits Sŏn to writing what the seat descriptions and domain ownership already say, at one remove, but gives each domain owner the metrics, risks, and interfaces her template asks for. Her provided and dependent interfaces sections map well onto the white paper's account of what breaks when information does not move between domains (WP pp. 13 to 14).
- **Option B: a charter per service period.** Dinner's first, since dinner opens first; each later period's before it opens; lunch's as the form the three-partner decision takes. Commits Sŏn to no service period opening without one, and to the lunch decision having a document to rule on: a lunch charter that cannot be written without describing a worse dinner is the white paper's "no good answer for lunch" (WP p. 36), reached structurally. The old synthesis argued this option and mapped her template to it (see below).
- **Option C: both**, with the domain charters short and the period charters carrying the operating logic.

**Her template, mapped to a service period.** Mission becomes the period's reason to exist as part of one company, laddering to the company mission, not a description of the food. Vision becomes what the period is for the customer over years. Customers becomes the period's first customer, in the white paper's sense of "different first customers at different times" (WP p. 35). Metrics become leading indicators only: recognition, return, employee measures (WP p. 22). Strategic importance becomes what this period gives the layer that no other period does (the morning enrolls a customer on the first visit, WP p. 36). Major risks become the specific ways the period could collapse into a copy of dinner, plus its risks to other periods and to the team's bandwidth. Provided interfaces become what the period hands on: the customer record, standing orders, staff fluency. Dependent interfaces become what it draws from: dinner's kitchen, the reservation record, the culinary logic. Two additions her template lacks: operating logic (how service, authority, and pace differ from dinner, so the difference is designed rather than drifted into), and what in the period is held for the chef partner or filled once the leads exist.

**The count is a parameter.** The white paper describes three core periods and two in reserve. Anything downstream (team structures in 4.1, the cadence in 2.3) that assumes four periods is assuming a decision not yet taken.

**Discoverability.** Her charters live on the intranet (p. 83). The white paper's equivalent is the system every employee can open to see documented competencies (WP p. 11) and the internal home pages the organizational foundations exercise asks about (p. 150). Where the charters live is a 2.2 question; that they must be findable is not.

## 8. Versioning and revision

### What the white paper holds

The white paper keeps an open items list and says what is still being decided (WP pp. 15, 22). It does not say who may change it or how. The phased opening has natural gates: dinner alone for at least a quarter; the first full year before lunch is revisited (WP p. 36).

### Considerations

**Her cadence: every year or two.** Sŏn's version one is drawn from decisions, not moments, so the first revision matters more than usual: it is the first time the principles can be tested against things that actually happened. The white paper's gates offer two natural points, after dinner's first quarter alone and after the first full year, and annually after that. Placing the revision on the calendar belongs to chunk 2.3; deciding that it happens belongs here.

**Who may change the founding documents.** Silent in the white paper. Changing them is a candidate reserved class (section 6). Whether the chef partner holds a voice in versioning before they are seated, and who holds it in the interim, are the same interim questions as deadlock.

**Versioned by decision, not by drift.** The white paper describes a system that updates a process nightly from a shift-close capture (WP p. 13). Founding documents cannot work that way. A version, a date, and an author on each change is cheap and prevents the founding document from becoming whichever copy someone last edited.

## 9. Getting the documents to the team

### What the white paper holds

Brandon is "the public voice" (WP p. 03). Orientation to the mission, the values, and the behavioral standard precedes operational training (WP p. 18). Every new person is paired with a mentor who is "a proven cultural steward" (WP p. 18). The leads carry the founders' vision on the floor (WP p. 10). The white paper does not say which founder speaks the concept to the team internally, at what rhythm, or how the two leads carry that voice when the founders are not in the building.

### Considerations

**The founding documents are on the critical path for the first orientation.** The white paper's onboarding commitment cannot be met for anyone onboarded before the mission and principles exist. One option is to make the founding document a condition of the first cohort's start date; another is to onboard the two leads on a draft and the cohort on the finished version. Either way, the sequence is: founding document, then the culture guide for candidates, then orientation content (chunk 3.x).

**Who carries the voice.** A house built to run without its founders implies the leads must be able to speak the mission and principles without a founder present. Which founder holds the internal voice, and how it is handed to the leads, is a decision the seat descriptions depend on.
