# 4.5 Creating the team environment: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 4, pp. 304 to 330, with the chapter's exercises at pp. 367 to 377 and workbook templates. Full text: `sources/extraction/build/s10/book.md`, `sources/extraction/build/s10/exercises.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## The frame

Rituals and common practices weave the fabric of a team: they help people build relationships and work together creatively, cooperatively, and effectively (p. 304). This section covers the two main rituals she relies on: offsites and meetings.

## Offsites

### Why have one

Offsites generally serve three objectives, usually in some mix (p. 305):

- Evolve a set of people from a work group into a team.
- Evaluate progress and set near-term priorities and goals.
- Engender long-term strategic thinking.

Taking people out of their day-to-day routine sharpens focus, and for a geographically dispersed team, planning together in one place lets them then work well apart (p. 305).

Offsites are not for solving talent problems; that is restructuring's job. They are for strengthening bonds in a team already designed to work well together. Before planning one, assess the state of the team and only commit the time if it is genuinely a wise use of it, or if the team's strategy and composition are themselves in question (pp. 305 to 306).

### Which type, and how often

Building a team is conscious, ongoing work, and it starts with understanding the team's development stage. More senior teams warrant more frequent offsites (very senior teams two or three times a year, adding members each time); more junior teams should hold one at least annually (p. 306).

### Development stages

She uses Bruce Tuckman's four-phase model: forming, storming, norming, performing, with no standard length for any phase (p. 306). Psychological safety, the ability to take interpersonal risks like asking questions or dissenting without fear of repercussions, is what lets a team move from one phase to the next, so actively building it matters throughout (p. 306). Her table of phases (pp. 307 to 308):

| Phase | Descriptors | Guidance |
|---|---|---|
| Forming | Goals, roles, and processes ill-defined; reliance on the manager to direct; little psychological safety; overt politeness | Be more directive; expect silence; provide extra support and modeling; actively build psychological safety through collaborative norms |
| Storming | Boundaries still unclear, toe-stepping; increased psychological safety lets conflict emerge | Work to clean up goals, roles, and processes; frame conflict as healthy and normal, don't squelch it |
| Norming | Goals, roles, and processes clearer; manager becomes more consultative; more commitment and collaboration; cleaner decisions | Celebrate getting through storming; keep honing how the team works; maintain urgency |
| Performing | Team is in flow; healthy debate; smooth conflict resolution | Keep tightening norms; increase the rate of challenge; know the phase isn't permanent |

A forming team benefits from clarity-building work and work-style exercises; a storming team benefits from more relationship and identity work. The offsite agenda should balance task focus and team focus: too much of either and the other suffers, so as a team matures, tilt more toward task (pp. 308 to 309).

### Planning

A shared planning document is a forcing function for good input from everyone, and it brings three benefits: inclusivity (a safe place to weigh in without debating live), an anchoring effect (writing objectives down as a group anchors the conversation), and pre-work (a shared mental space before you arrive) (p. 309).

Her planning checklist, appendix p. 367 to 368:

- One-plus month before: set goals and objectives, schedule date and length, start the agenda with the team, book space.
- One month before: order food, finalize the agenda and assign a DRI to each section, share pre-work templates with DRIs.
- One week before: finalize materials, run through content with DRIs, send agenda and pre-reads, confirm headcount and venue, duplicate session and icebreaker templates.
- One day before: send a welcome email with logistics, confirm vendors, collect supplies.
- Day of: check in, then follow the agenda.
- After: send notes and action items to DRIs, send a feedback form, track action items to completion.

### Running the day

Offsite structure: welcome, check-in exercise, icebreaker, sessions, check-out exercise (p. 369). Suggestions for what to include (pp. 309 to 311):

- **Check-ins** set the day's emotional rhythm: ask how people are feeling and what their objectives are. Model a brief, personal, vulnerable check-in yourself, or have someone who understands the tone do it.
- **Icebreakers** should be fun and personal rather than professional: a meaningful photo, a favorite book or movie, a tour of a personally significant object.
- **Agenda structure** for each section: purpose, agenda, limit (time allotted), and a place for discussion notes and actions; consider a notetaker per session.
- **Check-outs** put people back in a reflective mood: a one- to three-word answer, or a probing question like what decision mattered most.

An offsite becomes a shared memory, especially when held somewhere distinctive; people will locate a moment in time by referring back to it (p. 311).

## Meetings

Meetings can serve many purposes: decision-making, sharing information, live review, alignment, problem-solving. It is easier to describe a great meeting than to run one, and treating them casually turns them into an energy drain rather than a tool for execution, team building, and management (pp. 311 to 312). There are two components to a good meeting: the groundwork laid up front, and the mechanics of running it.

### Groundwork

Meetings only work as well as the relationships among the people in them, and those relationships are not built in a 30-minute weekly slot. For a recurring meeting with a set group, invest first in common understanding and meeting norms: who is on the team, each person's role and work style, and, for a new team, its purpose and norms (pp. 312 to 313).

**Meeting roles** (pp. 313 to 314):

- **DRI**: responsible for the meeting's success; may be the organizer or the leader of the project the meeting supports.
- **Facilitator**: keeps the meeting on time, works through the agenda, and captures decisions and actions with the notetaker. Often the DRI, but not necessarily; some meetings rotate facilitators.
- **Notetaker**: records discussion points, decisions, and action items, since facilitating and taking notes at once is hard.

Notes create psychological safety, give context to anyone who missed the meeting, and support accountability. They should not be a transcript; the best notes, sent afterward, cover top-level topics, decisions, and follow-ups with an owner and a timeframe, plus any open questions for next time (p. 314).

**Mutual ownership**: every participant, not just the organizer, should own the meeting's success. Signs of ownership: participants self-correct for norms, participation is broad and even, and roles rotate. Ways to build it: agreed norms reinforced when violated, assigned or rotating facilitators and notetakers, and the organizer modeling the behavior (being on time, not rescheduling) (pp. 314 to 315).

**Purpose and structure, the PAL framework** (pp. 315 to 316): a meeting should serve only one or two purposes.

- **Purpose**: why are we meeting.
- **Agenda**: what topics serve that purpose.
- **Limit**: how long the meeting and each item will take; trying to cover too much in too little time makes discussion shallow or cuts it off unresolved.

### Meeting norms

Norms are the group's commitments about how the meeting runs and how people show up; agreeing on them up front makes it easier to course-correct later by naming the norm being broken (p. 316). Her recommended practices:

- **Logistics** (pp. 316 to 317): agree on meeting time (and, across time zones, whether to rotate it), whether delegates are allowed, whether pre-reads must be done beforehand or read together at the start, how action-item status gets updated, and electronics use.
- **No topics are undiscussable** (pp. 317 to 318): put uncomfortable issues on the table before they fester, what she calls "stinky fish." She ties this to "say the thing you think you cannot say."
- **Be inclusive** (p. 318): everyone participates and builds on others' points rather than restating them uncredited; the facilitator draws out people who haven't spoken.
- **Disagree and commit** (p. 318): attributed to Andy Grove and used at Amazon. People can disagree during the decision; once made, everyone commits to its success.
- **Have a parking lot** (p. 318): set aside off-topic ideas to revisit, and make sure the facilitator actually revisits them, or the parking lot becomes a dumping ground.
- **Respect action items** (pp. 318 to 319): before closing, confirm every action item has an owner and a timeline, and agree how progress will be tracked.
- **Reconfigure every three to six months** (p. 319): audit whether meetings are useful, involve the right people, and achieve their purpose; consider polling participants. Options: refresh the norms, evolve the membership and topics, or start over.

### Operator mode versus creator mode

Operator-mode (manager) meetings have clear agendas for decisions, updates, and alignment. Creator-mode meetings are for brainstorming, with much less structure and not always a decision as the outcome. Both kinds of people and work need to be balanced; her Stripe leadership offsites were, in effect, recurring multi-hour creator meetings, and she learned through feedback not to over-program them (pp. 319 to 320).

### Running the meeting

Share ahead of time, ideally in writing (pp. 320 to 321):

- **Purpose**: why are we meeting.
- **Agenda**: what topics and why.
- **Limit**: how long, overall and per topic.
- **Decisions**, if applicable: what will be decided and who decides.

**Make it engaging**: an active facilitator who tracks participation and moves the group past a stuck point or a dominating voice; check-ins and check-outs help set the tone (p. 321).

**Check-ins and check-outs** (pp. 321 to 323) are simple and undervalued. Work check-ins surface what people want from the meeting or where their head is on the topic; personal check-ins (often paired with a work question) surface state of mind, which builds empathy and context. Check-outs help people retain what they will take away: a takeaway, a commitment, or a one-word gauge of how the meeting landed.

**Achieve your purpose**: close out each agenda item by confirming what was decided or learned, which builds momentum into the next item; for information-sharing meetings, poll whether people got what they needed (pp. 323 to 324).

**Clarify the decision-making process** (pp. 324 to 326). Name who decides and how, using one of:

- **Autocratic**: one decision-maker, who may consult but always decides.
- **Consensus**: the group agrees together.
- **Democratic**: the group votes and the majority wins.
- **Consultative**: the leader consults the group but decides.
- **Delegated**: the leader and team agree on a delegate whose decision they will all stand behind.

Say the process out loud at the start ("I'm the decision-maker, but I want your input" versus "we'll agree on a joint decision"). As a general practice, the most senior person in the room should hold their opinion until the end, so as not to anchor the discussion, except when deliberately floating a provocative idea to provoke reaction.

**Decision-making frameworks**: she references Bain's RAPID, Gokul Rajaram's SPADE, and McKinsey's seven-step process, cited by Anker's president as the most useful framework he learned (pp. 326 to 327). A decision log, a running record of what was decided, when, and why, can help, though she has had mixed results since it only works when widely adopted and kept current.

**Honor the norms and correct bad behavior** (pp. 327 to 328): remind people of norms periodically, especially with new participants, and model them. Criticize ideas, not people; call out a norm violation matter-of-factly in the moment if needed, but correct a work-style pattern privately, and only if the team has enough shared vocabulary and trust to make that land. Otherwise, give feedback outside the room, promptly, and keep it specific and brief.

### Leadership team meetings

A leadership team meeting is a staff meeting for the people who run a company or division, distinguished by the breadth of topics and the stakes of its decisions; it also sets the model the rest of the company follows, and its cadence should evolve every three to six months in a fast-growing company (pp. 328 to 329).

She invokes Patrick Lencioni's concept of a "first team": the group a leader works most closely with to accomplish goals. A leader's commitment to their first team, the leadership team, should come before their commitment to their second team, the one they directly manage; if the leadership team doesn't act as a first team, the company risks dysfunction (p. 329).

Her Stripe example, a Monday-morning three-hour meeting: each leader shares written "snippets" by Sunday evening, then the group reviews metrics and the product calendar, checks in on leadership hiring, and works through snippet or company topics (pp. 329 to 330).

She also describes how the senior leadership team itself changes rarely, and how Stripe built supplemental structures, an expanded leadership team and later an "operating group," to give newer senior leaders a forum to learn how the company runs without diluting the top team; both structures were retired and replaced as the company's needs changed (p. 330).

## Exercises and templates

**Planning and running your offsite (pp. 367 to 370).** The planning checklist above, plus day-of templates: an offsite structure (welcome, check-in, icebreaker, sessions, check-out), a welcome-email template covering purpose and logistics, and a sample agenda table with session, purpose, time, length, and facilitator columns.

**Leadership team snippets and updates (pp. 370 to 372).** A template for the pre-meeting update leaders submit by Sunday night: an actions-status link, discussion topics with time allotments (actions, a customer visitor, metrics review, recruiting update, audit update, a topic from the CEO, and top goals), standing questions (QBR reflections, what to share more widely, any objections to that, what decisions were made), and a snippets section per leader covering action-item updates, whereabouts, potential discussion topics, initiative status, customer notes, and launch or PR plans in flight.

**Stripe's unblocking process (pp. 372 to 377).** Created by Stripe's CTO to resolve a disagreement that has already been tried collaboratively and failed. First, ask whether the decision is even worth escalating: reversible, low-stakes decisions should just be made and iterated on. For an important, hard-to-reverse, multi-party decision that debate hasn't resolved, the process is: (1) the disagreeing parties jointly write a short document naming the problem, the options, and the unresolved trade-offs; (2) if that alone doesn't produce agreement, they send it to their managers, who either decide (if one manager) or decide together (if two), escalating up the management chain if needed. Unblocking should be attempted jointly but can proceed unilaterally, with notice, if one party refuses to participate; a manager receiving a unilateral request must confirm the requester tried direct negotiation and the joint document first. A worked fictional example (pp. 375 to 377) walks through two engineers, their managers, and an escalation to a shared manager over a technical storage decision.
