# 4.5 Creating the team environment: working session

## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** The book builds a team's fabric with offsites and meetings (pp. 304 to 330). Sŏn's standing rooms already exist on paper: the brief, the close, the check-in, the leads' review, the house review, the reset. This session decides how people are treated inside those rooms, what the house does that no review does (a meal, a marked moment, a gathering's team half), where every kind of thing a person needs to say goes, whether the leadership team is a team or a coordination room, and how the whole environment is read. It ends with a one-page checklist of what any person at Sŏn can count on.

**Already decided upstream, do not reopen.** What counts as a team and whether the service periods are one team (4.1.2). The brief's fixed content and the close's (2.3.7, 2.3.8). The check-in as Sŏn's 1:1 (2.3.9). The leads' review's chair and attendance (2.3.13). Whether a whole-house gathering is held (2.3.25). The mechanism reset and the founders' annual block (2.3.30). Who holds each lead's check-in (2.2.5; several destinations here wait on it). The decision-rights rule and register (2.2.2, 2.2.4). The designation rotation floor (4.1.19). The work-style vocabulary's use beyond the founders (1.1.4). How a mentor pairing ends (3.3.14).

**Who else must agree.** Dominic: the leadership team question (4.5.2), the leadership block (4.5.4), the freeze (4.5.22), the decision log (4.5.26). The chef partner: the staff meal (4.5.12), the kitchen's brief slot (4.5.13, 4.5.20), and whether the chef partner sits in the leadership team (4.5.2). Counsel: the meal's paid-time treatment only.

**Why it sits here.** Chapter 4 has built the structure (4.1), the reads on it (4.2), and how it changes (4.3, 4.4). This section is the fabric between the nodes: the white paper's "elastic connective tissue" that keeps the rigid points from becoming brittle (WP p. 9). It has to be decided before training services, because the brief's slot, the meal, and the destinations page all run there.

**A naming note for the whole session.** The book's "check-in" is a short round at the start of a meeting. Sŏn's "check-in" is the 1:1. This guide says "opening round" and "closing round" for the book's practice.

## 3. Briefs

### 4.5.2 Decide whether the leads' review is a first team, and what a lead owes it over their own domain

**The question:** when the Maitre d and the Operations Lead sit down each week, are they a team whose success each puts ahead of their own domain, or two domain owners coordinating?

**Why it matters now:** the lead seats are hired before anyone else, and a candidate deserves to know at interview which of these they are joining (2.1.19, 3.1.27). The answer also sets what the leads' review can ask of them, and whether the chef partner sits in the room as a peer.

**Openers:**
- The Maitre d has a floor problem that will make the Operations Lead's labor numbers look bad next week. In your house, does that reach the Tuesday review, or does it get fixed quietly first?
- The book says a leader's first team is the one they run the company with, not the one they manage (p. 329). Say that sentence to your two leads in your own words. Does it sound like Sŏn, or like the layer you are trying not to build?
- Sideways: at Coqodaq, fourteen leadership positions around one dinner (WP p. 22). Which of those people, if any, behaved as if the others' success was their job? What made the difference?
- Is the chef partner in this team, or in the partners' room, or both?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Is the failure you want to prevent divided loyalty, or a problem that never crosses the seam? If the second, does a duty to surface do the job without an ordering of loyalties?
- If a lead is asked to put the leadership team first, what does the review have to be for that to be a fair ask?
- Who is in the team by name of seat: two founders, two leads, the chef partner, anyone else (4.1.6)?

**What the book says:** a leadership team's meeting is distinguished by breadth and stakes and models the rest of the company; commitment to the first team comes before the second, or the company risks dysfunction (pp. 328 to 329).

**White-paper default:** the two leads are parallel, neither reporting to the other, both serving the same center (WP p. 10, stated outright). Silent on what they owe each other as a group.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Lencioni's first-team idea is widely used in leadership teams. The common restaurant pairing of a general manager and an executive chef usually meets only when something has gone wrong; a fixed weekly review pre-empts that.

**Options:** (a) the book's first team, said aloud at the lead interviews, with the chef partner in it; (b) co-equal leads with a duty to surface anything that could touch the other domain the same week; (c) the founders are the first team and the leads' review is coordination. Depth: cons. 8.

**Watch for:** an answer that makes the leads' review a place leads report to founders rather than a team; "co-equal" used to avoid asking anything of the leads; a chef partner who is in the team on paper and never in the room.

**A finished answer:** the team named by seats, one sentence a lead is told at interview about what they owe the group, the failure this guards against named, and the chef partner's place settled or parked with them.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic; the chef partner for their own seat.

### 4.5.3 Decide how each kind of team at Sŏn forms, and what its lead does differently while it does

**The question:** the book's stages assume one group meeting repeatedly; Sŏn has a nightly roster that dissolves at close, a cohort, a whole house, and a leadership team. Where does each one form, and what does the person leading it do differently in its first weeks?

**Why it matters now:** the founders will be more directive during training services than they intend to be forever. Saying so in advance keeps the early directiveness from reading as the permanent style, and tells the leads when to expect the hand to loosen.

**Openers:**
- Opening night, the roster has never worked together as this exact group. It cannot storm; it has to perform. What did the brief do at Coqodaq's opening that made that possible, and what did it fail to do?
- The whole house will storm during dinner's first quarter, whatever you call it. What do you expect that to look like at the close, and who catches it?
- Sideways: the two leads meet each other for the first time as hires. Their first real disagreement is the leadership team's storming. Who is in the room for it, and does 2.2.3 already answer what happens?
- What does "be more directive" mean for you, personally, in week two of training services? What will you stop doing by the reset?

**Narrowing questions:**
- For each of the four: where does forming happen (the brief, the training sequence, the pre-opening weeks, the leads' first month), and who leads it through?
- Which of them ever norms as a group, and which only through rules set in advance?
- Does a domain (the whole kitchen, the whole floor) form as a team at all, or only through its lead one-on-one (4.1.2)?

**What the book says:** forming, storming, norming, performing; be directive and build safety while forming, clean up goals, roles, and processes and treat conflict as normal while storming, keep honing while norming (pp. 306 to 308).

**White-paper default:** a new person forms in onboarding, why before how, a mentor from day one (WP p. 18). Silent on the house and the leadership team.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Restaurant openings commonly run friends-and-family or training services precisely so the roster performs once before it has to; the book's forming work is done there whether or not anyone names it.

**Options:** (a) one statement per team object, as above; (b) only the leadership team and the house get statements, and the roster and cohort are held to be covered by the brief and 3.3; (c) decline the stage model as a frame and read team state only from 4.2's diagnostics. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** a statement about founders' behavior that becomes a description of Brandon's working style (that belongs in 1.1, in his own document); a stage model applied to the roster, which cannot storm.

**A finished answer:** four short statements, each naming the forming place, the storming shape, who leads through, and what changes after; or a decision that fewer objects need one and why.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic, for the leadership team's statement.

### 4.5.4 Decide whether the leadership team holds a working block of its own, and whether the first one forms the team before opening

**The question:** does the leadership team, once both leads are hired, get the book's senior-team offsite in any form, separate from the founders' annual block (2.3.30) and the weekly review?

**Why it matters now:** the only cheap moment for a forming block is before opening, when there is no service to close. After that, every block costs a closed day or comes out of the leads' rest.

**Openers:**
- Both leads are hired. It is six weeks before training services. Do the four of you spend a day somewhere with the working-with-me documents and the charters, or do you meet on Tuesday like any other week?
- The earlier work said no offsite for anyone but the founders. It never asked about the leads. Why do you think that was?
- Sideways: what is the one room, outside a restaurant, where you have watched a team become a team in a day? What was on the agenda, and what was not?
- The book warns her own leadership offsites got over-programmed (p. 320). If Sŏn held one, what would you refuse to schedule?

**Narrowing questions:**
- None, once, or recurring? If recurring, how often, and on the closed day (2.3.12) or a closed service?
- What mix: task (the readiness test, the quarter's goals) and team (the working-with-me exchange, the storming rules)? The book says tilt toward task as the team matures (p. 309).
- Off the premises or in the room? The book's shared-memory effect is stronger somewhere distinctive (p. 311).
- Which of the book's scaffolding survives: a written pre-read and a fixed agenda, yes or no; rounds are 4.5.6; a feedback form, yes or no?

**What the book says:** offsites turn a work group into a team, set near-term priorities, and build long-term thinking; senior teams warrant two or three a year; a forming team benefits from clarity work and work-style exercises (pp. 305 to 309).

**White-paper default:** none; silent on offsites. Dinner runs alone for at least a quarter (WP p. 36), so a closed day is not free after opening.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Pre-opening leadership time is common in restaurant openings and is almost always operational; the team-focused half the book describes is the part usually skipped. Stripe ran its leadership offsite as a recurring creator-mode meeting (pp. 319 to 320).

**Options:** (a) no block; (b) one pre-opening block, then none; (c) a recurring block one to three times a year. Under (b) or (c), the scaffolding choices above. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** a block that is the readiness test under another name (that is 2.2.39); a block that exists to read morale (the earlier work was clear it should not); an agenda with no team half at all, which is the failure the book names.

**A finished answer:** none, once, or recurring, with cadence and length if recurring; the task and team mix in one line; whether the first one is held before opening; and what carries the forming work if the answer is none.

**Capture for the kit:** if a block is held, the questions that made the agenda, what was cut, and what the pre-read needed to hold. The meeting page kit (4.5.16) carries a block variant.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic.

### 4.5.5 Decide the roles in a standing meeting: owner, chair, and record

**The question:** the book names a DRI, a facilitator, and a notetaker. Sŏn has mechanism owners on a register and a chair for the leads' review. What are the house's names, and do the chair and the record rotate?

**Why it matters now:** the leads' review runs from the leads' first month. A meeting without a named chair is run by whoever talks first.

**Openers:**
- Tuesday's review. Who opens their mouth first, who watches the clock, and who writes it down? Are those three people or one?
- The book says rotating the facilitator builds mutual ownership (p. 315). Would you hand the leads' review chair to a lead on alternate weeks, or does the founder always hold it?
- Sideways: the best-run pre-shift you ever stood in. Who ran it, and was that the person who owned the night?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Are owner and chair ever different people? If yes, when?
- Is the record a person or a form? If a form, who fills it and by when?
- Does a name like "DRI" earn its place on the floor, or does the house say "owner" because the register already does?

**What the book says:** the DRI owns the meeting's success, the facilitator runs time and agenda, the notetaker records decisions and actions; notes are not a transcript and are sent afterward (pp. 313 to 315).

**White-paper default:** authority lives in the domain and its owner (WP pp. 7 to 8). No meeting roles.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Rotating chairs are common in small leadership teams; a fixed record form is common wherever notes must survive turnover, which is the white paper's memory-failure argument (WP p. 13).

**Options:** (a) the book's three names; (b) owner, chair, record, with the chair rule per meeting; (c) owner and chair always the same person. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** three roles for a four-person meeting; a record that is a person's memory.

**A finished answer:** the names, the chair rule for each standing meeting, and whether anything rotates.

**Needs agreement from:** none beyond Brandon; 2.3.13 already set the leads' review chair, so confirm consistency.

### 4.5.6 Decide whether Sŏn's meetings open and close with a round, and what kind

**The question:** does a standing meeting start with each person saying what they need from it (or how they are), and end with each person saying what they take away?

**Why it matters now:** the earlier work declined this as a prompt for vulnerability the house does not solicit. That is a position worth testing before it hardens, because it is also the book's cheapest device for inclusion.

**Openers:**
- The leads' review starts. Before the first agenda item, does anyone ask "what do you need from this hour?" If not, how does the chair learn that the Maitre d is only here for item three?
- The book pairs a work question with a personal one and says it builds empathy (pp. 321 to 323). The earlier work said the house does not ask people how they feel. Where is your line, and is it the same for the leads' review as for the brief?
- Sideways: a night when you needed the pre-shift to know something about your day, and it did not ask. Would you have said it if it had?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Work round only, work-or-personal by the person's choice, or none?
- Which meetings: the leads' review, the house review, the partners' review; the brief is governed by 4.5.13 and its length by 2.3.7.
- A closing round: one word, one takeaway, or the record read back?
- Is anyone ever required to answer?

**What the book says:** a check-in sets the emotional rhythm and surfaces what people want; a check-out fixes what people take away (pp. 309 to 311, 321 to 323).

**White-paper default:** none. The house offers physical dignity, not emotional prompts (WP pp. 19 to 20, as the earlier work read it).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Opening rounds are standard in facilitated meetings; in restaurants, the pre-shift lineup rarely has one, which the book would call a missed device and the earlier work called a mercy.

**Options:** (a) none; (b) a work round and a one-line closing round; (c) a round that may be personal by choice, never required. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** a round that becomes a status report; a personal round that a new lead feels obliged to perform.

**A finished answer:** for each of the three standing meetings, round or no round, its kind, and the rule that no one must answer a personal question.

**Needs agreement from:** none.

### 4.5.7 Decide how the decision method is named in a room, and whether the most senior person speaks last

**The question:** when a topic reaches a meeting, how does everyone know who decides and how, and do the founders hold their view until others have spoken?

**Why it matters now:** the register (2.2.4) answers most of this in advance. The gap is the topic that is not on it, and the founder who anchors a discussion by speaking first.

**Openers:**
- An item comes up on Tuesday that no register entry covers. What does the chair say in the first ten seconds?
- You have a strong view on the item. Do you say it first, or last? What happened the last time you said it first?
- Sideways: the white paper names exactly one decision method: lunch is "an equal decision among all three partners" (WP p. 36). Why did that one need saying, and what else does?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Register only, with the owner named on the spot for anything off it, or the book's five methods by name for off-register topics?
- Senior-speaks-last as a written norm for founders and leads, with the book's exception for a deliberate provocation?
- Does "consultative" become a rule (some decisions must consult affected parties first), as the earlier work proposed?

**What the book says:** name who decides and how (autocratic, consensus, democratic, consultative, delegated) at the start; the most senior person holds their opinion until the end (pp. 324 to 326).

**White-paper default:** the domain owner decides inside their domain (WP pp. 7 to 8); the server decides within an authorized range (WP p. 13).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. The book cites Bain's RAPID and other frameworks (p. 326); the earlier work declined them in favor of the register.

**Options:** (a) register only, owner named on the spot; (b) register plus the five methods for off-register topics; (c) either, plus senior-speaks-last. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** "consensus" used to mean the founder decides after everyone has talked; a register so complete that no one ever has to name a method, which is the goal, and so this norm is rarely used, which is fine.

**A finished answer:** the chair's sentence for an off-register topic, which methods exist by name, and the senior-speaks-last rule adopted or declined.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic, since it binds him too.

### 4.5.8 Decide the rule for correcting a person in front of others

**The question:** what may a chair or lead say to a person in front of the group, and what is never said in a brief, a close, or a review?

**Why it matters now:** the brief and the close are the rooms most people at Sŏn will ever be in. One correction in front of the crew teaches the whole house what those rooms are for.

**Openers:**
- The close. A server's error is on the capture. What does the person running the close say out loud, and what do they say to the server afterward, and where?
- The book allows telling a colleague in the room that they are "acting a bit red" (p. 328). Does anything like that ever happen at Sŏn?
- Sideways: describe a lead you have worked for who corrected people in front of others and got away with it. Why did they get away with it, and would that reason hold here?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The chair's powers: the item, the clock, the norm. Anything else?
- Is a norm violation named in the moment ("that was a restatement, not a build") or only afterward?
- Where does the private conversation go: the check-in, or sooner (5.2, 5.3)?
- The lead's own standard: is "a change not on a versioned sheet is not a rule" a rule you want written?

**What the book says:** criticize ideas, not people; name a norm violation in the moment; correct a pattern privately, and only when the team has the vocabulary; otherwise give feedback outside the room, promptly, specific and brief (pp. 327 to 328).

**White-paper default (stated outright):** the first question is what allowed this, not who failed; management absorbs blame from staff; post-mortems are private and process-focused (WP p. 17).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Kitchens have a long tradition of public correction and a documented cost in turnover; the white paper's blame-the-process position is a direct rejection of it.

**Options:** (a) the book's rule as written; (b) nothing about a person in any group setting, ever; (c) (b) plus the two guards against a lead's unwritten rule. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** "process" language used to correct a person in front of others by implication ("the process at table twelve failed"); a rule so strict that a chair cannot name a broken norm.

**A finished answer:** the chair's three powers or a different list, the never-said list, and where the private conversation lives.

**Needs agreement from:** the chef partner for the kitchen's rooms.

### 4.5.9 Decide the norms every standing meeting at Sŏn runs by

**The question:** the short list of one-sentence rules anyone can quote when a meeting breaks one.

**Why it matters now:** it collects 4.5.5 to 4.5.8 into a page (4.5.15) the leads read before their first review.

**Openers:**
- Pre-reads: read the night before, or read together in silence for the first five minutes? You have sat in both. Which one did people actually do?
- Phones face down, or laptops open to the record? What does the brief already do?
- Sideways: name the meeting norm you have broken most often in your own career. Would you write it into Sŏn's list?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Pre-reads, delegates, devices, start and hard stop, off-agenda items (parking lot, or the destinations page), how a dissent is recorded once a decision stands, actions leave with an owner and a date. Anything else, or anything to cut?
- Is "disagree and commit" the phrase, or the earlier work's version: the disagreement is written down and the decision is checked later against what happened?

**What the book says:** purpose, agenda, limit; logistics, no undiscussable topics, inclusion, disagree and commit, a parking lot, action items, reconfigure every three to six months (pp. 315 to 319).

**White-paper default:** feedback without follow-through breeds cynicism; the loop gets closed (WP p. 22). Silent otherwise.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Amazon opens with silent reading of a memo. "Disagree and commit" is its stated principle; the book credits Andy Grove (p. 318).

**Options:** the norms are the choices above, each a sentence. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** more than eight; a norm no one could quote at the moment it is broken.

**A finished answer:** the list, each a sentence, ready for 4.5.15.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic.

### 4.5.10 Decide the unblocking path for a disagreement between two people that direct conversation has not resolved

**The question:** two people disagree, neither owns the decision, and talking has not fixed it. What do they do next?

**Why it matters now:** 2.2.3 and 4.1.34 answer this for the two leads. Nothing answers it for a server and a cook, or two designation holders, and the first such disagreement will happen in training services.

**Openers:**
- A server and the pass disagree about firing a course. Tonight it is decided in three seconds by whoever is closer to the domain. Next week it is still a disagreement. Where does it go?
- The book's first test is "is this worth escalating at all?" (p. 373). How much of what you have seen argued about in restaurants fails that test?
- Sideways: the last time you and Dominic disagreed and had to write it down. Did writing it change the disagreement?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Does the leads' joint half-page (4.1.34) extend to anyone, or only to people who each own something?
- Who decides when both people share a lead, and when they do not?
- What if one party will not write?
- Where does this link from on the destinations page: under "a dissent"?

**What the book says:** ask whether it is worth escalating; if so, jointly write the problem, options, and open trade-offs; then the managers decide, escalating only if needed; a unilateral request must show direct negotiation was tried (pp. 372 to 377).

**White-paper default:** none below the founders. The server owns the table (WP p. 13); the domain owner decides inside the domain (WP pp. 7 to 8).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. The process is Stripe's, as the book presents it.

**Options:** (a) the book's process for everyone; (b) the write-up for owners only, and anyone else goes to their lead; (c) reversible disagreements are decided by the nearest domain owner and revisited at the close. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** a path so formal no one uses it; a path that makes the lead the answer to every disagreement, which is the load-bearing pattern the white paper names (WP p. 13).

**A finished answer:** the test, the write-up's three parts, who reads it in each case, and the no-participation rule.

**Needs agreement from:** none.

### 4.5.11 Decide the destination for each kind of thing a person needs to say

**The question:** for a question, a friction, a dissent, a failure report, a concern about one's own lead, a concern about a founder, a serious violation, and a pay question: where does it go, and how fast does it come back?

**Why it matters now:** the candidate sheet promises communication expectations (WP p. 18), the why session runs before operational training (3.3.35), and the page (4.5.17) must exist in every staff language by then. And one destination is missing today.

**Openers:**
- A server in week three has a problem with the Maitre d, who holds their check-in. Today, where does that go? Say it out loud and notice whether it goes anywhere.
- The book says put the stinky fish on the table (p. 317). The earlier work said the real risk is not a topic no one wants to raise but a topic with nowhere to go. Which one worries you more at Sŏn?
- Sideways: the founder is in the building during service and a cook has a concern. Is the founder the destination because they are standing there? The earlier work said no. Do you agree?
- What is the response window a person can hold you to? Seven days to acknowledge, thirty to close was the earlier number. Is that a promise you can keep in the first quarter?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The concern about one's own lead: does it wait for 2.2.5, or does it get an interim destination now (the other lead, a founder, the channel)?
- A concern about a founder: the same channels as any concern? Who reads them?
- A serious violation: both leads the same day, never the brief or close; what else, before 5.10 exists?
- Pay: point to 5.5's pages, and say the check-in never negotiates pay.
- Is "a topic with no destination is itself something to report" a rule you want?

**What the book says:** no topics are undiscussable; put uncomfortable issues on the table before they fester (pp. 317 to 318).

**White-paper default:** feedback runs both directions (WP p. 19); a dedicated channel syncs to the execution system and the loop is closed (WP p. 22). Silent on a concern about one's own lead.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most employers route harassment and discrimination reports on a separate, legally required path with more than one destination; the earlier work kept those outside this page so they never wait on a lead.

**Options:** the earlier work's map (cons. 4), with the open lines named; an interim destination for the lead concern now, or a hold until 2.2.5. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** "escalate" as a destination; a destination that is a person's name rather than a role; a prompt that asks how someone feels.

**A finished answer:** a table of eight rows with destination and window, the missing ones named with a date, and the no-destination rule adopted or declined.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic on the founder-concern row; 2.2.5 for the lead row.

### 4.5.12 Decide whether the pre-service window includes a staff meal

**The question:** is there a meal before dinner service, and if so when, who cooks it, and whether people eat together?

**Why it matters now:** the window's shape locks in during training services. A meal added later displaces something already scheduled there. And the chef partner has to size it.

**Openers:**
- Every serious room you have worked in fed its staff before service. Sŏn's white paper never mentions it. Was that an oversight or a choice?
- Picture the window: the check-in, the brief, and a meal. In what order, and does it fit?
- Sideways: the best family meal you ever ate. What did it do to the service that followed? The worst one?
- Morning and late night, when they open: same answer, or does a coffee crew eat differently?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Together or in shifts, so the kitchen never all sits at once?
- Cooked from what, and by whom, and is that a station's job or the Steward's?
- Every day, or the closed day, or one day a week?
- Inside the paid window (paid time, no separate treatment) or as an unpaid meal period (conditions apply; counsel confirms)?

**What the book says:** team-focused activities balance task focus; a shared meal is among her offsite suggestions (pp. 308 to 309).

**White-paper default:** the beginning of a shift is time to prepare for the customer (WP p. 26). Silent on a meal and on the window's length.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. A pre-service staff meal, often called family meal, is close to universal in full-service restaurants; it is usually cooked by the kitchen from inexpensive or surplus product and eaten before the lineup. It is the industry's oldest team ritual and it costs kitchen time on every busy night.

**Options:** (a) a daily meal before dinner, together; (b) in shifts; (c) none; (d) the closed day or weekly. Depth: cons. 9.

### 4.5.13 Decide the brief's team-content slot: what it may carry, what it never carries, and how a skip is logged

### 4.5.14 Decide the "not currently available" state: what a person may declare, what it protects, and what never reads it

**The question:** may a person at Sŏn say "not this shift, not this designation, not this stretch of the schedule" without giving a reason, and if so, who sees it, what does it protect them from losing, and what is never allowed to read it?

**Why it matters now:** the schedule is built before the first training service and the attendance states (5.8.6) are written into it; if this state is not decided alongside them, the only way a person can step back is by missing a shift, which is then counted. The rotation floor (4.1.19) and the ready-now pool both read who is available. New in the cross-chunk pass; 3.3 and 5.8 both pointed here because it is a promise about the environment, not a performance state. The build rides with 5.8.22.

**Openers:**
- A server tells the Maitre d on Tuesday: I cannot take the room designation this month. No reason offered. What does the Maitre d say, what gets written, and what does the server lose?
- Sideways: the white paper says bandwidth comes before results and that people are not a disposable input (WP pp. 12, 16). Where in the schedule can a person actually act on that sentence today, without asking anyone?
- Who at Sŏn would you not want to be able to see that someone has declared this? Start there.

**Narrowing questions:**
- What may be declared: a shift, a designation, a stretch of the schedule, all three? For how long at a time, and how does it end (a date the person set; the person clearing it; never by anyone else)?
- Who sees the state: the person, the scheduler, the person's lead? Who does not: the other lead, the partners except through a count, the check-in unless the person raises it?
- What it protects: rotation standing under 4.1.19, the pool, the review, pay's days-worked count? Which of those it cannot protect, and does the person see that before declaring?
- Distinct from the attendance states (5.8.6), from the paid hold between a flag read and the partners' decision (5.10.9), and from a departure state (5.10.12): does the person page show them differently, and can one ever convert into another?
- The line that performance, attendance, or rating data never sets or reads it: can a lead ever suggest a person declare it?
- The scheduling tool's capabilities (3.3.32): can it hold a state with no reason field, or is this manual until it can?

**What the book says:** the environment a person can count on includes being able to say no without explaining, and a team that only allows a no with a justification is one where people stop saying it (pp. 296 to 298).

**White-paper default:** none on the state itself. Bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results is the sequence (WP p. 12); labor is an asset, not a cost, and the team is not disposable (WP p. 16); designations mark accountability in the moment and are not permanent elevations (WP p. 10), which is what makes declining one possible.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Scheduling tools carry an "unavailable" flag that is usually a request a manager approves, which is the opposite of a declaration; a few hospitality employers let people block days without a reason up to a cap and treat a block as neutral in scheduling.

**Options:** (a) a declaration: shift, designation, or stretch, no reason, set only by the person, seen by the scheduler and the lead, protecting rotation standing, the pool, and the review, with pay following the days actually worked, ended by the person or a date. Commits Sŏn to a schedule that can carry an unexplained gap and to a tool or a manual fallback that refuses a reason field. (b) A request: the same states, approved by the lead, with the reason optional. Commits Sŏn to a lead deciding whether a person may step back. (c) None; a person arranges cover or asks their lead, as in any restaurant. Commits Sŏn to the attendance count being the only record of a person stepping back.

**Watch for:** a reason field that arrives with the tool's defaults; a state visible to a lead who can then read it as a pattern; "protects the pool" that quietly resets the person's assessment date; the state used to park someone by a lead rather than declared by the person; a cap on how often it may be used that turns a declaration back into a request.

**A finished answer:** what may be declared and for how long; who sees it and who cannot; what it protects and what it cannot; how it ends; the three states it is not; the sentence that nothing about performance sets or reads it; and whether the tool can hold it or 5.8.22 builds the manual form.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen's designations; Dominic for the tool.

### 4.5.25 Decide how psychological safety is read at Sŏn, and when the baseline is set
