# 5.2 Giving hard feedback: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 5, pp. 391 to 394. Full text: `sources/extraction/build/s12/book.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## Where this sits

Intuition-based coaching (5.1) often leads a manager to conclude that a report needs constructive feedback naming an area for improvement. That conversation is hard because it risks hurting feelings and can surface strong emotions: shame, sadness, disappointment, fear. How the conversation is framed determines whether the person becomes defensive or experiences the manager as a collaborator invested in their success (pp. 391 to 392).

## Be an explorer, not a lecturer

Her central reframe: don't set the meeting up as delivering bad news the report must react to. Set it up as a partnership exploring a situation together (p. 392).

This is hard because the manager typically arrives with a hypothesis, gathered evidence, and often a preferred solution already in mind. But offering solutions before both parties agree on the problem risks alienating the report. The sequence that works: first establish shared understanding of the issue, then use that shared ground to build toward improvement together. Just as people tend to learn more from their own mistakes than from being told about others', a person is far more likely to change if the recognition of the need to change, and the idea for how, comes from themselves rather than from the manager (p. 392).

The goal is to get the report thinking alongside the manager and generating their own solutions. Two ways to open that kind of conversation (p. 392):

### Option 1: ask an open-ended question

Good openers are neutral and open, such as "how do you think that presentation went yesterday?" or "how do you think this quarter is going?" Bad openers presuppose an answer, such as "do you think that meeting could have been better?" The worst are closed yes-or-no questions like "do you think you're bad at presenting?", which signal the manager has already made up their mind (pp. 392 to 393).

If the report responds with acknowledgment ("I wish I had been more prepared"), the conversation can move to root causes and solutions together, from shared understanding. If the report responds with denial ("the presentation was pretty good!"), move to Option 2 (p. 393).

### Option 2: share an empathetic observation

Frame the observation as neutral and specific, not a judgment. Her example: "I was thinking about that presentation you gave. It was so strong at the start, but I thought it could have been even more impactful at the end. What did you think?" If asked for detail, get concrete: "I noticed your talking points became less concise toward the end," or for delivery issues, "I noticed that the last two projects you owned each missed their deadlines by two weeks. Is there something I can help with?" (pp. 393 to 394).

Contrast supportive, objective, specific language with emotional, generic language such as "I feel like you've gotten a lot worse at making presentations lately." Generalities and judgments put people on the defensive; specific, empathetic observations open dialogue (p. 394).

When sharing an observation, own it as your perception, not an objective description of the person: "feedback is just holding up a mirror and describing the image you see." You're not stating what the person actually is, only how you experienced them, and that framing keeps the report in the conversation rather than asking them to simply concede (p. 394).

If a direct empathetic observation doesn't land, cycle back to open-ended questions a few times. If denial persists, it may be time to state your own answer plainly: "Huh. My impression was that it was not that great. For example..." Reaching common understanding of the development area can take a few rounds, but she notes people are usually more self-aware than a manager expects (pp. 394 to 395).

## When self-awareness is the blocker

If the person isn't self-aware and more data doesn't close that gap, a change may be needed. Lack of self-awareness doesn't automatically mean someone is a low performer, but it makes coaching to improve and scale with the role difficult, which is often what causes someone to become a low performer over time (p. 395). She points to her later section on managing low performers for what follows when this doesn't resolve.
