# 3.3 Onboarding: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 3, pp. 216 to 223, with the chapter's exercises at pp. 246 to 259 and workbook pages on working-with-me documents. Full text: `sources/extraction/build/s07/book.md`, `sources/extraction/build/s07/exercises.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## Why onboarding matters

A new hire's first days are like a first day of school: everything feels important, and people are on high alert for context and cues. Companies that just hand someone paperwork, a laptop, and a desk are missing the chance to start inculcating the person into the company and its culture (p. 216).

Onboarding should feel true to the business and cover three things (p. 216):

- The "why" of the company: the mission and the story behind it.
- The "what" of the business: current priorities and goals.
- The "how" of company behaviors: how people work together and the operating principles.

Her strong belief: company leadership should take part in onboarding, ideally in a recurring session where leaders talk about values and principles and, better still, tell stories of those principles in action. Stories, not bullet points, are what land (p. 216). Dominique Crenn, chef and owner of Atelier Crenn, is quoted making the same point about a kitchen hire: understanding the whole operation, not just your station, and refreshing that understanding continually rather than treating training as a one-time event (pp. 216 to 217).

At Stripe, every new hire goes through a weeklong core onboarding curriculum regardless of level, alongside function-specific programs that start in week one and run into week two. As a manager, you should know what the company and division curricula cover and decide what else your new hire needs in their first weeks, whether that's folded into your first 1:1 or a dedicated one- to two-hour session translating the company operating system down to the team level (p. 216).

## What a good program produces

If onboarding is well designed, prep work (paperwork, equipment) happens asynchronously before the program starts. New hires should come out of it with (p. 217):

- A sense of community with other new hires, an informal network regardless of seniority.
- An understanding of leadership's vision and the company's values or principles.
- Deeper knowledge of the business, the industry, and key products.
- Insight into users and their feedback.
- Knowledge of the operating structure and current goals and priorities.
- A visual sense of the org structure.
- An understanding of key processes, like compliance or media contact procedures.
- A sense of where their team fits in the bigger picture.
- The ability to find internal resources and self-educate.

## Internal mobility

Internal moves are often overlooked in onboarding, but they deserve the same attention. People change jobs for one of three reasons: running to something, running away from something, or being pushed out as a poor performer. The first is the healthy case, so it is worth collecting data early. When someone changes teams, a short survey to the person, their old manager, their new manager, and the people team can ask (p. 217):

- Was the move initiated by the employee or the company?
- Is it developing the person on their current path, or a change in direction?
- Did the former and new manager talk about the transition before it was decided?
- On a 1 to 5 scale, how satisfied was the person with their current team and manager?
- Will the move give the person more impact?

Don't over-index on one or two unhappy responses, especially during a period of change, but a consistent pattern of voluntary departures ("bad mobility") calls for investigating root causes (pp. 217 to 218).

In fast growth, some people will churn through several managers in a short time. A short, standard manager-to-manager transition process reduces the disruption: a three-step guide (preview the move with the person, hold a joint transition meeting with the person and the new manager, have the new manager follow up on action items), with a sample meeting agenda covering current work, strengths and development areas, how to support the person's ambitions, and preferred ways of working (p. 218, exercise at p. 247, reproduced below).

## New hire onboarding: the working-with-me document

Her "Working with Claire" document, written first in 2010 and later published in Elad Gil's *High Growth Handbook*, describes how she works: operating approach (1:1 cadence, team meetings, quarterly planning, personal goal reviews), management style (collaborative, hands-off but able to get hands-on early in a new initiative, accountable, data-driven yet intuitive about people, strategic, user-oriented), and communication preferences (1:1s, email response norms, chat, a preference for documented plans). It closes on a personal note: she likes a good laugh at work, a reminder from a colleague that the mechanics of working together aren't the whole story (pp. 218 to 219, full text at pp. 253 to 257).

She recommends every manager write their own version and use it in the first meeting with a new report, a meeting devoted to setting mutual expectations. That first meeting should cover (pp. 218 to 219):

- Onboarding: additional team context.
- Operating approach: how and when you'll work together.
- Management style: what kind of manager you are, shared through your working-with-me guide, with an invitation for the report to write their own.
- Communication preferences: how you each like to communicate and expected response times.
- Initial priorities: for the team and the individual's role.
- The upcoming career conversation: a heads-up that a longer conversation about their career arc and ambitions will follow in a few months (see p. 283, the career-conversations section).

Not every new hire will be ready to share their own working style right away, and that's fine; let them think about it and write their own document when ready. The goal is to set a tone of openness by sharing first (p. 219).

## New leader onboarding

Leadership onboarding should meet the same objectives as standard onboarding, with more invested because the rejection risk is higher and the impact of a new leader, positive or negative, is felt immediately (p. 219). At Stripe this is the New Leader Experience (NLE): welcome emails, prescheduled meetings and pre-reads, the Hogan Personality Inventory as a leadership assessment, a coach, a set of first-month actions, and a 90-day 360-degree feedback process. Success depends on the commitment of everyone involved: the leader, their manager, their spin-up buddy (a team member providing onboarding support), their guide (a peer mentor for "how does this place really work" questions), their HR partner, and their new direct reports (p. 219).

A table of NLE objectives and tactics (p. 220):

| Objective | Tactic |
|---|---|
| Get the division excited about the new leader | Standard onboarding, plus a welcome email from the manager with personal context |
| Give business and company context fast | Core onboarding, plus pre-start reading and key first-weeks 1:1s |
| Forge connections with manager, colleagues, stakeholders | Critical 1:1s scheduled for the first few weeks |
| Balance listening and learning with early decisions and action | A spin-up buddy and a guide as sounding boards |
| Reinforce strengths and alignment with principles | Hogan assessment, HR partner time, 90-day feedback |
| Course-correct quickly when friction arises | Same tools, used for early self-awareness and feedback |

For especially critical roles, a daily or every-other-day short standup with the leader's manager helps surface questions and impressions early. The hardest part of leader onboarding is knowing when to shift from gathering information to taking action: too fast risks poor decisions, too slow risks paralyzing the division (pp. 220 to 221).

### A direct report's perspective on onboarding a new leader

A sidebar from former Stripe employee Jorge Ortiz, who onboarded many leaders, offers guidance from the other side of the relationship (pp. 221 to 224):

- Commit to their success. A new leader's failure is expensive for the whole division.
- Deliver information fast but at a digestible pace, learn how they best absorb it, and triage what they need to know now versus later.
- Give facts, not your pre-processed conclusions; let them reach their own.
- Use shadow and reverse-shadow handoffs for responsibilities that are moving to them.
- Model company values, since new leaders often can't see the unwritten parts of culture and few people will tell them directly.
- Give feedback quickly and often; if trust isn't built yet, route sensitive feedback through their manager or a trusted peer.
- Help them adapt old playbooks to new constraints rather than assuming what worked before will work here.
- Map the org, formal and informal: decision-makers, domain experts, incentives, high and low performers, forums and cadences.
- Loan your social capital: broker introductions, talk them up, set them up for early wins.
- In some cases, transfer your social capital outright, including your relationship with star performers who might follow you.
- Share the stories and history behind how the company got here; current state is often path-dependent and hard to explain without it.
