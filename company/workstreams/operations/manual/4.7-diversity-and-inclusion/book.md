# 4.7 Diversity and inclusion: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 4, pp. 354 to 360. Full text: `sources/extraction/build/s11/book.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## Why this is a manager's job

She frames diversity, equity, inclusion, and belonging as part of a manager's role, offered from her own vantage point as a manager, an ally working to keep improving, and a woman in leadership others look to as an example, not as an outside expert (p. 354).

Her starting claim: a great team and company come not only from hiring well but from building an environment that fosters and retains talent, and the work of diversity and inclusion is fundamentally the work of building a better environment for everyone. Research shows diversity affects team performance; homogeneous teams may execute faster, but diverse teams reach outsize results once they have the psychological safety, Amy Edmondson's term, that lets people actually speak up. The underlying logic: most problem spaces are too large to hold in one set of experiences, so more varied backgrounds and perspectives let a team take on problems more multidimensionally, but only when everyone feels able to fully participate (pp. 354 to 355).

She widens diversity past race and gender to include socioeconomic background, education, sexual orientation, gender identity, political and religious belief, and upbringing. A named failure mode: as a company grows, referral-based hiring quietly clones the existing team, since people refer others like themselves; referrals are valuable, but the cycle needs watching and breaking if it becomes the only hiring channel (p. 355).

She names three areas where a manager's influence on diversity and inclusion concentrates, with a shared instruction to broadcast opportunities widely, both inside and outside the company (pp. 355 to 356):

- Hiring
- Performance assessment, reward, and recognition
- Running teams

Before going further in any of these, she recommends educating yourself on the lived experience of historically underrepresented groups in your country and company; her own example as a US-based leader is reading Coqual's "Being Black in Corporate America" (p. 356).

## Hiring

Assess your team's current portfolio of backgrounds and viewpoints, and name what's missing; if you don't know, sit in on another manager's team meeting to see what a different mix looks like (pp. 356 to 357). Build a pipeline wide enough that you're exposed to many different candidates before you start interviewing. Add an interviewer with a different background and different strengths from your own, someone who complements your intuition and can push back on your assumptions about a candidate (pp. 356 to 357).

A caution: hiring alone is not enough. Companies that treat hiring as the whole answer neglect the harder work of developing and retaining a diverse leadership pipeline. She cites a BCG study of its own consultants: people from underrepresented groups, matched on GMAT and GPA, advanced more slowly for two reasons, a weaker sense of belonging and more difficulty navigating the professional environment. The conclusion: once a team is more diverse, running it equitably and inclusively is what makes the investment pay off; without that, the company will neither retain the team nor attract more diverse candidates later (p. 357).

## Performance assessment, reward, and recognition

Her named failure modes are tactical: picking someone for a project through a quick, informal process with no explicit criteria; deciding promotions without objective data; adding someone to a team because you used to work together. Each has consequences (pp. 357 to 358).

Her fix: run a lightweight but explicit process. Make new opportunities visible and let people volunteer, rather than filling roles by tapping the person who already looks like you on the shoulder, since that excludes people who would have raised a hand, or who never spoke up but would perform well given the chance. If someone you'd expect to volunteer doesn't, ask why and help them find the path (pp. 357 to 358). Have a common, agreed way to evaluate who gets promoted, gets a project, or gets a leadership opportunity, with clear criteria (see Chapter 3), plus equity checks such as calibration in performance management and pay parity analysis in compensation. These don't need to be heavy audits; she likens them to the simple checklists the best hospitals use to prevent errors (p. 358).

## Running teams

The way you run a team should give everyone an equal chance to prove themselves, starting with onboarding: solicit feedback to confirm every new team member, regardless of background, feels prepared to contribute after their first month. Nearly every team interaction after that is a chance to be more inclusive, how agenda items get collected, how an offsite gets planned, how a meeting runs, how a controversial decision gets made. Watch airtime, and draw out anyone who hasn't spoken. Assume your own management can be more inclusive than it is, and ask someone you trust to observe and tell you where (pp. 358 to 359). She recommends the book *Unleashed* by Frances Frei and Anne Morriss for concrete inclusion frameworks (p. 359).

## Two closing frames

David Foster Wallace's 2005 Kenyon College commencement speech, on attention, awareness, discipline, and the discipline of caring about other people in small, unglamorous ways every day, is offered as one of the more impactful things she has read on empathy and compassion (p. 359).

She closes by holding two things together: real sensitivity to the fact that you can never fully know someone else's experience, particularly a person from a historically underrepresented group if you are not part of it, alongside a belief in uniting a diverse team around shared qualities and a common vision, human experience, and a desire for love and respect (pp. 359 to 360).

Chloé Valdary's theory of enchantment supplies three tenets she treats as meaningful for managers too (p. 360):

- Treat people like human beings, not political abstractions.
- Criticize to uplift and empower, never to tear down.
- Root everything in love and compassion.

Her instruction to managers: adopt these, build a shared vision the whole team belongs to, set goals, measure progress, and hold yourself and other leaders accountable not just for team composition but for the environment each manager creates, not as a checklist exercise but because it produces a tighter company fabric, a stronger team, and better results (p. 360).
