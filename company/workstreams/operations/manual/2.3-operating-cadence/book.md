# 2.3 Operating cadence: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 2, pp. 135 to 143, with the chapter's quarterly business review guidelines and outline at pp. 156 to 165. Full text: `extraction/build/s04/book.md` and `extraction/build/s04/exercises.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## Where this section sits

Chapter 2 builds the planning and accountability framework as a house: founding documents are the foundations (2.1), the operating system is the structural beams (2.2), and the operating cadence is the mechanical components. This section is the last of the three, and she opens it by saying the cadence is how you operationalize the operating system (p. 135).

## What an operating cadence is

The schedule or rhythm with which a company, its divisions, or its teams provide progress reports and make decisions (p. 136). Her reason for having one is blunt: people are creatures of habit, and a rhythm they can follow naturally is what turns the operating system into something that runs. People should be able to break with it in an emergency or for another good reason, but most of the time the goal is stability and predictability (p. 136).

The cost of not having one: if different teams set goals at different times and measure results with different definitions of the data, execution turns chaotic and morale suffers (p. 136).

Her rules for the cadence itself (p. 136):

- Cadences vary by manager, team, and individual, but they are agreed up front by everyone involved.
- They very likely mirror the company cadence, which is often driven by milestones in the calendar year.
- There is no secret to designing one; it depends on the type of team, business, and company.
- "The key is to have a cadence but not get stuck in it." Rhythms need iteration and should be revisited to stay relevant.

## Her examples

The list of cadences she gives (p. 136): annual planning processes; quarterly business reviews; monthly all-hands meetings; biweekly 1:1s; weekly snippets and team meetings, with metrics reviews; daily standups. ## Starting from what already happens

If there is no set cadence, observe the interactions and reviews that are happening organically. Some teams will already be using a process or a communication mechanism. Figure out why it is happening and what is and is not useful about it, then test the approach with a broader part of the company, or the whole company, for a month or a quarter, ask for feedback, and iterate (p. 137).

## What each cadence produces

Within each rhythm, teams and individuals produce reports: written updates, automated reports, and other measures of progress. Her examples: a support team opens each week by reviewing customer satisfaction, feedback, and response rates, then makes a plan to improve them; a product leadership team reviews the launch calendar and shares status on each launch (p. 137).

The point of all of it: predictable, common, consistent structures that serve as touchstones for every person in the division or team, so that people develop a shared understanding of why their work matters, how they will do it, what success looks like, and how it is measured (p. 137).

## The feedback questions

Ask for frequent feedback on the operating system and cadence. Her questions (pp. 138 to 139):

- Does everyone know the mission and objectives for the company, the division, and the team, and the work they are individually expected to do?
- Is there a clear timeline for planning and execution, with established measures of progress against targets?
- Are projects with dependencies aligned and agreed across the relevant teams and leaders, so ownership and priorities are clear?
- Does everyone know how success is measured and who is accountable for each part of the work, and when plans or results are due?
- Are there mechanisms to monitor progress, make decisions, and unblock teams?
- Are there communication structures to update on goals and notify relevant teams of changes?
- Is success recognized and celebrated? Are mistakes shared and learned from? Do internal communications reinforce the values and operating approach?

## Ownership and the leaders who run it

Design and delegate the cadence to suit the preferences and competencies of the leadership team. Only spin up a process once you are certain leaders will make a dedicated effort to implement it. Ideally there is one owner for each key structure, and every other leader agrees to honor it (p. 139).

Strong operators are dedicated and consistent in honoring the run-the-company structures; great operations are repetition and consistent improvement over time. Young companies are full of people who love inventing new things and need leaders who recognize and invest in repeatable processes; the mix should marry creativity with operational rigor. Be clear which leader owns which structure and match structures to each leader's strengths. The example a leader sets gives a structure its cultural and executional weight; lack of ownership, or leaders not adhering to the company's operating approach, undermines the whole architecture (p. 139).

## A brief note on process (pp. 140 to 142)

Process has a bad name, but bad processes cause bloat while good processes provide clarity, which leads to faster execution (p. 140). Her four notes:

**Watch out for defensive processes.** In environments without clear owners and decision-makers, something goes wrong and, instead of naming an owner to prevent it next time, someone creates a process. Her Google example: the launch approval swelled from about three people to over twenty as each function wanted official notice; it was cut back by making ownership clear, so the product manager consulted the affected functions and then decided readiness themselves. Good processes are lightweight checks that solidify alignment; defensive processes exist because people are not aligned on who owns a decision, and they slow everything down (pp. 140 to 141).

**Beware of stale processes.** People ignore deadlines for materials, open laptops in meetings, stop submitting updates, and fall into roles. It does not mean the process was bad; it may have outgrown its purpose, or it was defensive and the confusion it was covering is gone. Do not change processes too quickly either. "Revisit your processes every six months or so." More often is destabilizing; less often lets people switch to autopilot (p. 141).

**Allow for experimentation.** People fear experimenting with process because it looks indecisive. If you experiment: state how long you will try it, determine when you will check back, and set the criteria for whether to continue or revisit it (p. 142).

**Process is not synonymous with meetings.** Meetings are useful for sharing information, discussing, deciding, and bonding. But ask whether a meeting's objective could have been achieved by a written update or by everyone contributing to a project tracker. "A good process should subtract from the number of meetings, not add more" (p. 142).

## Warning signs (pp. 142 to 143)

Signs that the operating system or cadence needs revisiting:

- Goals, timelines, or accountable owners are unclear; you do not know which team is doing what, when.
- Everything feels like it is moving too slowly.
- People flag problems but cannot suggest solutions.
- Simple information on the status of the work cannot be found.
- No one can name the decision-maker for a project, or the priorities for the company or their team.
- People stop participating or showing up when asked for key information or to attend meetings.
- It is not clear which updates or meetings matter, and you hate updating the tracker or attending your own meetings.

## What the cadence lays the groundwork for (p. 143)

A sound operating system on an efficient cadence is the foundation for the rest of the book: hiring (bringing in people who carry the highest-priority work and set the culture), team development (processes for communicating, deciding, collaborating, and measuring the team's impact), and feedback and improvement (developing and coaching people while they clarify their own development goals). Chapter 3 begins with hiring.

## Exercise: quarterly business reviews (pp. 156 to 165)

Written by Stripe's finance team with her input. The QBR is the quarterly member of her cadence, and the guidelines are specific enough to adapt.

**What should be true after a QBR** (p. 156): it provided clear long-term and short-term metrics with targets through year end; it explained which goals were executed, which were not, why, and how gaps close; it explained what is invested relative to what the area produces; participants left with clear mutual expectations for the coming periods; obstacles through year end are known; leadership understands what the team needs (pp. 156 to 157).

**Delivering one** (p. 157): focus on results, not winding discussion of plans; be candid, since the audience wants a candid assessment; be concise, a six-page narrative plus an appendix of tables; prepare in advance, sharing the memo at least 24 hours ahead with reading time in the meeting; be present, minimizing distractions, and do not link out to supplementary materials.

**Before** (p. 157): link the document in the calendar and guidebook 24 hours ahead; keep confidential topics in a separate restricted document; email materials to attendees; prepare an agenda of key topics.

**During** (p. 158): allow pre-read time and announce its end; gather top-of-mind questions and add them to the agenda; prioritize if needed; include metrics as a standard topic; the QBR leader drives the discussion; take comprehensive notes with action items and owners.

**After** (p. 158): share the notes with all participants; add action items to the tracker; reflect on the quality of the QBR, including the quality of the metrics; gather feedback; decide what to change next time.

**The outline** (pp. 158 to 165), with directional length limits:

1. Executive summary, 250 words or less: a candid assessment, not a summary of successes.
2. Narrative, two pages maximum, grounded in charter and operating metrics. If goals were missed, skew toward lowlights. Cover the quarter's results, the outlook for the rest of the year, and longer-term strategy, with at least half the discussion on strategy and outlook. Her example outline: current quarter reflection (performance against goals, how metrics moved and why, key ships, user feedback, velocity); outlook (top three goals in priority, which metrics they move, whether on track, what keeps you up at night); other sections at discretion (cross-functional asks, hiring progress, team morale).
3. Metrics performance, two pages maximum: changes to the metrics used, graphs, a roughly two-year horizon, original and revised targets where revised.
4. Product P&L, for product teams, as a table.
5. Cross-functional focus areas: company-wide focus areas (reliability, security, efficiency, operations, product quality) with a score per quarter and comments.
6. Progress on goals, one page maximum: the top five to ten goals, each shipped, on track, at risk, or off track, with totals.
7. Appendix A, required tables: prior QBR action items with status and owner; operating expenses (budget versus actuals) for non-product teams; headcount by sub-team; top asks from users with status.
8. Appendix B: additional supporting materials and links at discretion.
