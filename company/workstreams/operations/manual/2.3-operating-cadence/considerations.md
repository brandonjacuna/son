# 2.3 Operating cadence: considerations

What is worth knowing before doing the work in `tasks.md`. Topics follow the book's order. "WP p. N" is the white paper (`extraction/s01/record.md`). Nothing here is a decision; where the old synthesis argued a position it appears as an option with its reasoning and what it commits Sŏn to. Plain terms are defined at the top of `tasks.md`.

## 1. The cadence as a whole

### What the white paper holds

Four rhythms and no calendar. The white paper describes rhythms in four places without ever laying them on a calendar or naming who runs them:

- **The floor's day.** "The rhythm is simple. Beginning of shift, prepare for the customer. During service, serve them. End of shift, reflect and strengthen the relationship" (WP p. 26). The shift brief and the post-shift capture are named mechanisms, and their absence is a named failure: sequence corruption, "the shift brief is not happening, or the system is not surfacing preferences, or post-shift documentation is not captured" (WP p. 14). The close is already allocated: every server writes a thank-you to every customer they served, and closing cleaning goes to an overnight crew so "server labor gets redirected to relationship-building" (WP p. 26). The learning loop runs through the close: "the system captures the incident in the shift close, the appropriate lead reviews, the process that allowed the failure gets updated" (WP p. 13).
- **Development and review.** Reviews every three to six months, focused on development, under a rule of no surprises: "continuous communication means a formal review never contains new information" (WP p. 19). A ninety-day plan "with regular check-ins and course correction" (WP p. 18). Advancement self-paced and system-scheduled (WP pp. 11, 19). Peer recognition in the moment (WP p. 19). Feedback both ways, through a dedicated channel, with the loop closed (WP pp. 19, 22). Post-mortems private and process-focused (WP p. 17).
- **The brand's cadence.** "A quick daily pulse on engagement and growth. A weekly read on what performed by category and on partnership return. A monthly strategic review of platform shifts and where to move" (WP p. 31), with a three-tier approval framework beside it. This is the only complete cadence in the white paper: three frequencies, a decision rule, an owner. It runs now and continues in service.
- **The phasing.** Dinner first, alone for at least a quarter; the other core periods once dinner is steady, "most likely the early morning coffee first and then late night"; lunch and brunch not before a full year, and lunch a three-partner decision that can come out of the plan (WP p. 36). "A daypart added as an afterthought, without its own reason to exist and its own operating logic, becomes a worse version of dinner" (WP pp. 33 to 34).

Two constraints govern every rhythm. Bandwidth is first: "Bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results... This is mechanical, not aspirational" (WP pp. 12, 17), and cooling failure is the failure a cadence can cause, an organization that "runs people at full capacity until they stop, and calls it dedication until it becomes turnover" (WP p. 13). And the company runs on its systems: it is "built so it does not depend on either founder's memory" (WP pp. 05 to 06), and the technology stack "absorbs that work" of integration "a person would otherwise hold from memory" (WP p. 21).

Where the white paper is silent: how the partners meet and decide; any weekly rhythm for the house; the form, placement, or pay of anything like her 1:1; the interval of the ninety-day plan's check-ins; a closed day, a closed week, or a season; when goals are written and scored; when the rhythms themselves are revisited; who runs the brief.

### Considerations

**Her unit is the quarter; a restaurant's is the day.** Her cadences "mirror the company cadence, which is often driven by milestones in the calendar year" (p. 136), and her quarterly review is the centerpiece. In a company that ships software the quarter is the unit of work and the day is its detail. In a restaurant the day is the unit of work and the white paper says so (WP p. 26). A quarterly cycle imposed on a restaurant inverts its own unit; a restaurant with only a daily rhythm never lifts its head. The cadence needs both, and the question is which is the plan and which is the read.

**The build does not end at opening.** Dinner opens and the early morning is still being built; the early morning opens and late night is still being built; late night opens and the first full year and the lunch ruling are ahead (WP p. 36). So "pre-opening" is not a phase that ends; the build runs alongside the room for a year or more. Whatever shape the cadence takes has to hold both at once for a long time.

**The hazard of one calendar.** The founders will have run a build at speed for a year and then walk into a room that runs on the table and the service. A single calendar carries its habits across that seam by construction: the same meeting, the same people, now pointed at the room. The old work argued for two named rhythms with a seam rule precisely so the protection is mechanical rather than a matter of self-restraint. The counter-argument: two rhythms is more machinery for a company of two founders and eight leadership lines (WP p. 22), and her own advice is to start from what is already happening (p. 137).

**Start from what already happens.** Her advice for a company with no cadence is to observe the organic reviews and build from those (p. 137). Sŏn already runs the brand cadence weekly and monthly (WP p. 31), and the founders already meet about the build. The cheapest first step is to write down what those meetings actually do and give them a fixed day and a record, before inventing anything.

**Her feedback questions are a readiness check in disguise.** The seven questions at pp. 138 to 139 (does everyone know the mission and their own work; is there a timeline and measures; are dependencies agreed; who is accountable and when are results due; are there mechanisms to monitor, decide, and unblock; are there communication structures for changes; is success recognized and are mistakes shared) are the test chunk 2.2's readiness rows already run, and the mechanism reset is where they are asked again.

### Options on shape (2.3.1)

**Option A: two rhythms that coexist.** A build rhythm (weekly partners' review, quarterly gate review, the brand cadence, onboarding per arrival; no daily unit) and a service rhythm (the day, the week's leads' review, the month's check-ins and reads, the season, the quarter's house review, the year's reset), sharing named members and keeping the rest apart, with a seam rule between them (section 2). Commits Sŏn to naming what is shared and what is not, and to a rule that keeps build meetings out of service hours.

**Option B: one calendar with two phases.** The same meetings before and after opening, with the agenda changing at the gate. Commits Sŏn to less machinery and to the founders' self-restraint as the protection for the room.

**Option C: start with what runs now, decide the shape at the first reset.** Write down the brand cadence and the founders' build meeting, give them a day and a record, and defer the service rhythm's design until the leads are seated. Commits Sŏn to designing the service rhythm with the leads, at the cost of the candidate sheet (WP p. 18) carrying fewer communication expectations at the first interviews.

## 2. The seam between the build and the room (2.3.2)

### What the white paper holds

"Dinner is what everything protects" (WP p. 33) and the metabolic sequence is mechanical (WP p. 17). Nothing on how the founders' build work coexists with the running room.

### Considerations

Four candidate rules, from the old work, each with its cost:

- **No build meeting in the building during service hours** (from the first brief of the day to the last close). Cheap, mechanical, and easy to check. Cost: the founders lose the building as a meeting place for most of the day.
- **The room's rhythms held fixed through dinner's first quarter**, except anything that protects safety, a non-negotiable, or a promise already made to customers, and except the shift close's continuous process updates (WP p. 13), which never stop. The argument is her own: do not change processes too quickly (p. 141), and a crew cannot learn a cadence that changes monthly. Cost: a rhythm that is visibly wrong in week three waits until the reset, which the reset must then be early enough to catch (section 9).
- **First-quarter goals as baselines, not targets.** Chunk 2.2's rule, applied here: a build habit of speed needs an improvement target to run at, and in dinner's first quarter the room has none. Cost: none beyond what 2.2 already decided.
- **The founders' floor presence on the service's tempo, not the build's.** This is a founder's own working rule and belongs to 6.1; it is listed here because the old work counted it as part of the seam. It is not decided in this chunk.

Whether the protection repeats at each later opening (the early morning's first quarter, late night's) is a separate question the founders can answer once or defer to the reset.

## 3. The day: the shift brief and the shift close (2.3.7, 2.3.8, 2.3.20)

### What the white paper holds

Cited in section 1: the rhythm (WP p. 26); the brief's absence as sequence corruption (WP p. 14); the pre-shift briefing as equal in importance to the signature dish (WP p. 20); customer recognition as a leading indicator (WP p. 22); the capture in the close (WP p. 13); the thank-you note and the overnight crew (WP p. 26); post-mortems off the floor (WP p. 17); the OS surface as the live home of the system's views (WP p. 21).

### Considerations

**The inversion.** Her cadence's problem is usually too few meetings. A restaurant's is the opposite: the pre-shift meeting already happens every day, and family meal often does too. The problem is that the daily meeting carries no structure, so it runs on whoever is holding it. The brief is the one place her "daily standup" already exists at Sŏn, and the work is to give it a spine, not to add it.

**Two objects hide in one meeting.** The Realist's read: the brief holds operational readiness (a service cannot open without it) and team-making content (what makes the gathering a team). Only the first is the cadence's; the second is chunk 4.5's. The operational block has to be a checklist the rush cannot delete, separate from the team content, so that a thirty-second brief on a short-crewed night still moves the 86s and the allergies. A candidate spine: the readiness block (the 86 list; allergies and dietary notes routed to the stations that need them; recognized customers on the book, surfaced by the system; staffing gaps; station checks), the period's goal in one sentence from its charter and goal page, the one thing from this week's leads' review (the same sentence at every period's brief that week), recognitions since the last brief read aloud, then team content bounded by the time left.

**Two demands on one close window.** The white paper allocates the close to the thank-you note (WP p. 26) and to the capture (WP p. 13) and does not say how long the window is. Both are right. The question is which yields on a short night. The old work's answer: the capture must take less of the window than the note, and a capture that takes longer is a system failure, not a server failure. A candidate close: the capture (incidents, preferences learned, what the next server should inherit), the notes, the lead's one line on the night (with one line on how hard the night ran on the team, since cooling failure is the failure the white paper says no restaurant measures, WP p. 13), and the station close checklist.

**What the close refuses.** Feedback about a person goes to the check-in; a post-mortem is private and off the floor (WP p. 17); cleaning is the overnight crew's (WP p. 26).

**One arc, and each period's own logic.** The white paper says each period needs its own reason to exist and operating logic (WP pp. 33 to 34) and describes the day as one restaurant met at different hours (WP p. 33). For the daily rhythm the reconciliation is simple: each open period has its own brief and close because each has its own charter, and the close of one period hands to the brief of the next through the capture. Where two periods run back to back with the same crew, the second brief can be a short readiness insert rather than a second gathering. The transition between periods in the room is the room's own and is not scheduled by the cadence.

**Who runs the brief** is the owner of the room for that period, and which seat that is (the Maitre d, a floor manager, the Lead Host at the early morning) is chunk 4.1's. The spine is decided here; the runner is named there.

**Languages.** The white paper promises the interview in the candidate's language (WP p. 17). The brief spoken only in English to a roster that is not all English-speaking has not been communicated. Chunk 4.8 sets the language list; the brief's rule that it is spoken in every roster language is decided here.

### Options on the brief's spine (2.3.7)

**Option A: the five-part spine above, fixed in order, with the readiness block on the OS surface too.** Commits Sŏn to a brief that survives compression and to building the readiness block into the surface.

**Option B: readiness block only, with everything else at the lead's discretion.** Commits Sŏn to a brief that is always short and never carries the week's one thing or the period's goal.

**Option C: the spine as a default the owner of the room may reorder.** Commits Sŏn to variation by period and by lead, which her rule on common structures (p. 137) warns against.

## 4. The check-in: Sŏn's form of the 1:1 (2.3.9 to 2.3.11, 2.3.17, 2.3.18)

### What the white paper holds

The ninety-day plan with "regular check-ins and course correction, so early success is planned rather than hoped for" (WP p. 18). Reviews every three to six months under no surprises (WP p. 19). The Maitre d owns team development, training, and the invisible advocate function for the floor (WP p. 10). Advancement never waits on a manager's attention (WP pp. 11, 19). The person can open the system and see what they hold and what they need (WP p. 11). Team members weigh in on management effectiveness (WP p. 19). Feedback runs through the dedicated channel (WP p. 22). Nothing on the form, the interval after the plan, the pay, or the record of the check-in.

### Considerations

**The function is not optional; the form is.** Her 1:1 is the load-bearing member of her cadence: weekly or biweekly, rarely rescheduled, a shared agenda document, notes, action items, quarterly goals tracked inside it. Every one of those assumptions is written for salaried people with desks. The alternative to a shift-native form is what almost every restaurant does, which is nothing.

**Why her form does not transfer.** The clock: hourly staff are on the clock when the room is open and are paid for the shift; a meeting outside the shift is unpaid labor, and a meeting inside the shift competes with service and loses. The frequency: a lead who is on the floor every service has one pre-service window per day; weekly per person for a full roster does not fit those windows. The document: a shared agenda assumes a desk and an hour between meetings; a server reads on a phone between services, in their own language. The unit: a quarter is a knowledge worker's planning unit; a restaurant person's units are the shift, the schedule, the ninety-day plan, and the season, and turnover in this labor market means a quarterly cycle can outlast a tenure. The relationship: her 1:1 is manager and report; at Sŏn the lead is a systems function at the center, not a rank (WP p. 09).

**Four of her six functions are already carried.** Advancement is the system's (WP pp. 11, 19). The formal read is the review's (WP p. 19). Upward feedback about the house has the channel (WP p. 22). Recognition has the peer platform (WP p. 19). Two are not carried by anything in the white paper: the standing, paid, two-way conversation about how the person is doing and what is in their way; and the record of it that the person can read and the review can rely on. Without the second, "no surprises" is a wish. Those two are what the check-in is for, and naming what it does not carry is what makes it small enough to fund.

**The old work's proposal, in full, as an option.** Fifteen minutes, on the clock as the first fifteen minutes of a scheduled shift (the system schedules the person's shift to begin early on their check-in day, so it is paid because it is shift), in the pre-service window (never mid-service, never at the close, whose labor is already the customer's, WP p. 26), placed on the schedule by the system when the interval elapses (the lead does not remember to call it; the person sees it on their schedule like a shift), with the lead of the person's domain (the Maitre d for the floor; the chef partner's to assign for the kitchen), with a fixed spoken spine: what is working; what is in the way (heard as a system question, "what allowed this," WP p. 17); what changed (the person's one goal, their progression state, schedule needs, anything for the lead); then the person's read on the lead and the house (WP p. 19); then one line written on the person page, read back, and the next date. It refuses a verdict, a scheduling negotiation, task assignment, a pay conversation (5.5), discipline (5.8, 5.10), and a post-mortem.

**Range and triggers.** The lead may move a check-in to the person's next shared shift when the window is short-crewed, and may extend it when the window has slack; may not cancel one, run one mid-service or at the close, write on the page outside the check-in, or carry its content elsewhere except through the review the person is part of. A check-in moved twice is a load reading on the lead at the leads' review; a check-in that surfaces a serious breach goes to both leads that day, and what follows is 5.8's and 5.10's. Later sessions proposed one extension: a lead may ask the system to place a check-in ahead of the interval, once per interval, on the next shared shift, counted as a load reading, with the interval reset from it. That is an option to add to the range, not a change to the form.

**The intervals.** Inside the ninety-day plan: every two weeks (six check-ins across the plan, which is what "planned rather than hoped for" means in practice). After the plan: monthly, squeezed from both sides. Weekly does not fit the windows; quarterly fails no surprises, because a review every three months with a check-in every three months has nothing between them. The Realist's arithmetic, without figures: a monthly rotation fits only if the number of people whose check-ins a lead holds is not larger than the number of pre-service windows that lead works in a month. If the roster exceeds the windows, the stretch is reported at the leads' review as a load reading on the lead, and the fix is structural (4.1), not a longer interval quietly accepted.

**The person page.** What replaces the shared document for a person who does not open documents is a card on the team's internal home, readable on a phone in the person's language: the person's one goal and its state; their progression state, written by the system from the competency record, not by the lead; the last three check-in lines, dated; the next date; the ninety-day plan while active. Written only at the check-in, by the lead, read back before saving, readable by the person at any time. The review opens on it, which is what makes no surprises a mechanism. The lead does not write on it between check-ins; anything said between is said in person and written at the next one. Later chunks put other records beside it (the review record, 5.4; the pay statement, 5.5; the coaching form, 5.1); none of those adds a field a lead edits.

**The worst realistic night.** A Friday, a full book, a green runner, a station short, and one check-in due. The lead reads the window and moves it, in range. The system re-places it on the next shared shift. The person is paid for the minutes they were scheduled early, because the schedule was the schedule. Nothing broke, because the thing the night took was the thing the range let it take.

**The interpreter question.** Where a person and their lead share no language, a check-in needs a third body or does not happen. That changes who is in the room, which the old form fixed at two. Chunk 4.8 holds the language list and the interpreter designation; 2.3.9 depends on it and does not decide it.

### Options on the form (2.3.9)

**Option A: the on-the-clock check-in as proposed.** Commits Sŏn to scheduling early starts for check-in days, to a lead's pre-service windows carrying the rotation, and to a page the person can read.

**Option B: the check-in folded into the shift brief.** A standing minute per person at the brief. Commits Sŏn to a public conversation where a private one was needed, and to a brief that grows with the roster.

**Option C: no standing check-in; the ninety-day plan's check-ins only, then the review.** Commits Sŏn to the review being the first time a person hears how they are doing after their first ninety days, which contradicts WP p. 19.

## 5. The week: the leads' review, the closed day, and the partners (2.3.4 to 2.3.6, 2.3.12 to 2.3.14, 2.3.19)

### What the white paper holds

The two leads are "parallel. Neither reports to the other. They are co-leads of different systems serving the same center" (WP p. 10). The Operations Lead oversees the systems and is accountable to their outputs; the Maitre d owns the how of the experience and team development (WP p. 10). The founders own non-overlapping domains (WP pp. 03, 05) and "the mark of these founders is a company built to run without them" (WP p. 06). Leading indicators before the X-ray (WP p. 12). The candidate's sheet names "their direct lead" and "communication expectations" (WP p. 18). Nothing on a weekly meeting of the leads, a meeting of the partners, a closed day, or which partner each lead talks to.

### Considerations

**The leads' review is the first place "runs without its founders" is tested.** It is the one cross-period, whole-house read. If the founders chair it, the company has a general manager with two heads. If the founders never read it, they are flying on the X-ray. The old work's answer: the founders read the record and attend by standing invitation for a stated reason, not by right.

**Placement is the whole problem.** The review sits in a building that is either about to open or has just closed, never inside service. While dinner runs alone, the morning of the week's first service day, ending before that day's brief, is the obvious slot. Once the early morning opens, that morning no longer exists, and the review moves to the interval between the early morning's close and dinner's pre-service window, on the lowest-tempo day of the week (known only from the reservation record once there is one). If the house has a closed day, both this review and the house review sit on it at no cost in service, which is why the closed day is decided first (2.3.12).

**The hard stop.** A leads' review that runs into the pre-service window takes the brief's time, and the brief is the mechanism the white paper says cannot be skipped (WP p. 14). The review ends before the brief begins, every week, and a review that cannot fit its agenda shortens the agenda rather than the brief. Her "be present" rule (p. 157) has a sharper form here: no service radio in the room, no screen open beyond the agenda's reads, a room that is not the floor, and the chair's clock the only clock.

**The chair.** Her rule is one owner per structure (p. 139). The review has two co-leads and no one above them. The old work argued for alternation by week, because a permanent chair is the first step back toward a general manager, which the white paper names as the load-bearing model's final failure (WP p. 10). The counter-argument: the Operations Lead "oversees those systems and is accountable to their outputs," so the run-the-house structures are closer to that seat, and a fixed chair is simpler. The chair's only added duty in either case is the clock and the record.

**The agenda, as a candidate.** Every item names what it reads and where the reading comes from. The leading indicators from the system, read first and every week (customer recognition for the week; the employee NPS pulse in the week it is taken; the number of shift-close captures and the process updates they produced, because "the process that allowed the failure gets updated" (WP p. 13) is a rate the leads can read). The week's captures: what the system learned, which process changed, which capture is waiting on a lead past a week. Goal state changes only, not a tour of every page. The interface between service periods once two are open: what the closing period handed on and what broke at the handoff, because bus failure (WP p. 13) at Sŏn is an interface failure between periods. Feedback items past the window, each with an owner. Load readings: check-ins moved twice, the closes' lines on how hard the week ran, any sign of node overload (WP p. 14). Decisions in the leads' range, taken and recorded; escalations with a named destination. The one thing: the single sentence each lead carries into every brief this week, written on the internal home before the first brief. Lagging indicators are not on it; they are the Operations Lead's on their own surface and are read once a quarter at the house review. Recruiting's standing reads (3.1) take a line under load readings or as a ninth item.

**The partners' review.** Equal partners with non-overlapping domains do not need a meeting to assign each other work. They need one to integrate, to take the reserved-class decisions (2.1.13), and to read each other's domains so a decision in one does not surprise the other. Pre-opening it is the build's working meeting, weekly, fixed day and length; in service it moves to a closed hour and reads the leads' review's record rather than repeating it. The monthly domain read is how equals review each other without either standing above the other: in writing, on the page, with the domain owner answering. The record is the partners' page: a decision log by dated, versioned entry; open cross-domain asks with owners and dates; the readiness state; the domain-read questions. When the chef partner is seated, the weekly review gains the culinary domain's page, the domain read runs three ways, and the reserved classes run on the three-partner rule (2.1.13).

**The lead and a partner, monthly.** The white paper puts "their direct lead" on every candidate's sheet (WP p. 18). For the two leads that is a partner. A monthly conversation in the check-in's form, on the clock, at a closed hour, on the lead's own page, is the pattern the old work proposed; it is not a report. The Maitre d, who owns "the how of the experience," pairs naturally with Brandon, who owns the experience (WP pp. 03, 10). The Operations Lead manages "the architecture of the business" (WP p. 10), which the white paper splits between Brandon's operating systems and Dominic's financial and technology build (WP p. 03), so that pairing is a real choice. The old work recommended Dominic so each partner holds one lead and the center is read from both sides; later sessions noted the pairing also decides who is the second reader when a lead is the subject of a serious concern (5.9).

**Construction and hiring.** The white paper assigns capital and the technology build to Dominic and the operating systems and experience to Brandon (WP pp. 03, 05) and is silent on construction and on hiring. The old work recommended hiring to Brandon's seat (the hires run the operating systems and the experience) and construction to Dominic's (capital in built form). Chunk 3.1 cannot start until the hiring owner exists; whether recruiting help is engaged for the pre-opening burst is 3.1's question and a cost question this manual does not price.

### Options on the closed day (2.3.12)

**Option A: one fixed closed day a week.** Commits Sŏn to a day with no revenue and to the reviews, the gathering if approved, and the reset sitting on it at no cost in service; the kitchen's deep clean and the menu turn's training find a home too.

**Option B: no closed day; every review at a closed hour.** Commits Sŏn to reviews in the morning before the first brief, which the early morning's opening later removes, and to finding the lowest-tempo day from the reservation record.

**Option C: a closed day by season.** Commits Sŏn to a schedule that changes with the seasonal shape 2.1.15 puts on dinner's charter, and to the reviews moving with it.

### Options on the chair (2.3.13)

**Option A: alternating by week.** Commits the leads to equal standing and to a record that any chair can pick up. **Option B: the Operations Lead, fixed.** Commits Sŏn to a simpler rule and to watching for the drift toward one lead above the other. **Option C: the Maitre d, fixed.** The mirror of B, with the argument that the review's first item is people and customers.

## 6. The month, the quarter, and the season (2.3.15, 2.3.16, 2.3.22 to 2.3.29)

### What the white paper holds

Employee NPS "tracked regularly rather than annually" (WP p. 22). The feedback channel with the loop closed (WP p. 22); employees as the users, with continuous feedback loops (WP p. 17). The failure mode reference as a diagnostic (WP p. 14). Cultural measurement "tracked with the same seriousness as cost": retention, internal promotion, engagement, participation in development; and the qualitative side, stories, exit interviews read for what could have been better, customer feedback about the team's experience (WP p. 23). The phased opening and its gates (WP p. 36). Nothing on a quarterly review, a gathering, the pulse's rhythm, the loop's window, or a season.

### Considerations

**The pulse.** The indicator can move within a month, so it is read within a month. Weekly over-samples a question whose answer does not change weekly and trains people to ignore it; annually is what the white paper rejects. One question, on the OS surface, off the floor, never on paper mid-service, anonymous; read at the leads' review in the week it is taken and as a composite at the house review. The anonymity matters: the feedback channel closes its loop to a named person, so the pulse cannot share the channel's carrier (4.8 confirms the carriers).

**The loop window.** The white paper sets the rule and not the number. A candidate: every item acknowledged with an owner by the next leads' review (within seven days); resolved, scheduled with a date, or declined with a written reason the submitter can read within thirty; older items a standing line at the house review with the owner named. Both numbers are parameters for the reset.

**The house review is her QBR, translated for a team that does not sit at desks.** What transfers whole: a memo shared ahead, reading time in the room announced closed, a candid executive summary, a narrative skewed to lowlights when goals were missed, top-of-mind topics gathered from the room, metrics as a standing topic, notes with action items and owners, and a written reflection on the review's own quality afterward (pp. 157 to 158). What changes: the memo is per domain, integrated by the partners into one house memo; it is shared to the partners and the leads, who read documents, and the house receives the outcome through the three channels afterward; her six pages becomes a reading-time rule (readable in a fixed block, or shortened by its author); "no links out" survives with a Sŏn reason, links rot and a memo that needs one is not finished.

**Her outline, section by section.** Executive summary: kept, as the first paragraph. Narrative: kept, as each unit's goal results with a flag on any goal met at a cost to bandwidth. Metrics performance: replaced by the leading indicators over the quarter, then the lagging indicators once as the X-ray (WP p. 12), with no figure in this manual. Product P&L, operating expenses, headcount: dropped; financials are not a source for this work, and whether any of it enters the house memo is the transparency line 2.2 draws. Cross-functional focus areas with scores: replaced by the five failure modes (tension slack, node overload, sequence corruption, skill invisibility, cooling failure, WP p. 14), one line each with the evidence from the quarter's captures and closes. Progress on goals: kept, as the goal state table across the units 2.2 defined. Prior QBR action items: kept and moved to the front, so the review opens on them. Top asks from users: replaced by customer feedback about the team's experience (WP p. 23). Team morale: replaced by the pulse composite and the exit interviews read for what could have been better (WP p. 23). Supporting materials: dropped.

**The gathering.** Her monthly all-hands has no form at a restaurant whose team never co-occurs by design; it needs a closed service or a shared window. If held: on the house review's closed day, after the review, so the outcome reaches the house spoken by the founders in one room on the day it was decided; paid as a scheduled shift; carrying the review's one thing, the quarter's goal states in a sentence per unit, the quarter's recognitions, and the next quarter's goals; nothing about a person. Its team-making content is 4.5's. If declined: the outcome reaches the house through the three channels only (spoken at every brief, written on the internal home, carried by the lead or mentor), and the fact of the decision is recorded. The cost is real and no figure is stated here.

**The gate review.** "Dates are not gates." A calendar quarter is when a gate is reviewed, not what opens it. The gate opens when chunk 2.2's readiness test holds at the review, checked by the named checker rather than reported met. Pre-opening it is its own meeting; in service it is a standing section of the house review, because the people are the same and a second quarterly meeting is one more than needed. Two refinements from the old work: an off-cycle review may be called by all partners together when every checker has reported every row met; and no review shortens a minimum duration the white paper set ("at least a quarter," WP p. 36). The white paper states the early morning's gate twice, as a duration and as a condition ("once dinner is steady"); the duration is a floor no review shortens and the condition is the gate, whose definition is 2.2's.

**The charters on the calendar.** For any period the order is fixed: charter drafted, charter ruled, the period's build, the readiness test, the opening. The ruling comes before the build so nothing is built for a period until its reason to exist is on a page. Dinner's is drafted first, before the pre-opening gate, because every other charter's "what would make it a worse dinner" field needs dinner's to point at. The early morning's is drafted during dinner's first quarter, by the partners with the leads, once there is a quarter of dinner to describe the handoff from, and ruled at the review that finds dinner steady. Late night's after the early morning opens. Lunch and brunch not before the first full year: the charter attempt is the form the three-partner decision takes, and a lunch charter that cannot be written without describing a worse dinner is the white paper's "no good answer for lunch" (WP p. 36), reached on paper. No lunch cadence exists before that ruling.

**The season.** The menu's change cadence is the chef partner's. What the founders can place without them: a turn reaches every open period's brief, the internal home, and the domain lead before its first service; its training sits in the pre-service windows ahead of it (every new item is a tempo tax, and the windows before a turn carry the training); and the season is not forced to align with the quarter. The house review reads the season whenever it falls.

**The goal cycle's placement.** Chunk 2.2 holds the goal pages, the counts, the states, and the change record. What this chunk places: pre-opening, the readiness test is the goal cycle, and failed rows become the next quarter's page for the unit that owns them; dinner's first quarter carries baselines only; from the second quarter, pages are written in the two weeks before the quarter and confirmed as the house review's last item, with the person's goal set at their check-in in that window; mid-period scoring at a designated week (the seventh, so the score is on the page before the memo is written); end-period scoring at the review. A person who arrives mid-quarter starts on their ninety-day plan (WP p. 18), not the quarter.

### Options on the house review's writer and chair (2.3.22)

**Option A: a memo per domain, integrated by the partners, who chair.** Commits the partners to integrating every quarter and keeps the whole-house read at the center. **Option B: one memo by the Operations Lead, the partners reading.** Commits Sŏn to one lead's view of the whole and to the Maitre d's domain being described by someone else. **Option C: the leads write and chair; the partners attend.** Commits Sŏn to a review that runs without its founders from the first quarter, at the cost of the founders' integration role.

## 7. The year: the mechanism reset (2.3.30, 2.3.34)

### What the white paper holds

The continuous loop through the close (WP p. 13); "when something goes wrong, the first question is not who failed. It is what allowed this to happen" (WP p. 17); the gates at dinner's first quarter and the first full year (WP p. 36); the tools evolving on real usage (WP p. 17). Nothing on revisiting the rhythms themselves.

### Considerations

**Her six months versus the gates.** Her rule is to revisit every six months or so, because more often destabilizes and less often produces autopilot (p. 141). The old work split the two risks: autopilot is answered by continuous care through the close, which updates a process the night it fails; destabilization is answered by making the large correction rare. On that reading the reset runs after dinner's first quarter (the moment the seam freeze lifts), after the first full year, and annually after, at the house review nearest dinner's anniversary. Her six months is the alternative, and it may be the better one for the first year, when most rhythms are untested.

**The method is hers, run in reverse.** State how long a process was tried, when it was checked, and the criteria (p. 142), applied against the record each rhythm produced: its cost and buy, her warning signs (pp. 142 to 143), and one Sŏn addition, a rhythm no one would miss is the argument to end it. Each rhythm continues, changes by a dated versioned entry, or ends. The parameters this chunk marks (the check-in's intervals, the pulse's rhythm, the loop window, the brief's ceiling, the leads' review's day) are re-set here and nowhere else, except for anything that protects safety or a promise to customers.

**One annual block.** Later work proposed holding the reset and the principles revision (2.1.18) as one block at a closed hour, in a room that is not the floor, with the leads attending for the reset and the founders' working-with-me documents on the table (6.1). It is her offsite reduced to its decision content: it closes no service, reads no team state it does not already have, carries no opener or feedback form, and produces versioned decisions.

**What the reset reads, as counts.** Later sessions listed the counts a reset can read without naming a person: briefs that ran the full spine against briefs that dropped to the readiness block; closes where the capture displaced the note or the reverse; the capture and process-update rates; the channel's closure rate and items past the window; check-ins moved and placed; the pulse composite. Each is a count of services or reviews, never a figure in money and never a read of one person from a count.

**Recruiting and the reset.** Chunk 3.1 hands the reset its own parameters (the response window, the founder's interview stage, the source mix) and names the reset as the revision rhythm for its instruments. The reset's agenda holds a line for them.

### Options (2.3.30)

**Option A: at the two gates, then annually, as one block with the principles revision.** Commits Sŏn to a first reset early (after dinner's first quarter) and then a slow rhythm. **Option B: her six months, standing.** Commits Sŏn to a second reset in the first year regardless of the gates. **Option C: at every house review, lightly, with a full reset annually.** Commits Sŏn to the quarterly reflection paragraph doing the light work and the annual block the heavy.

## 8. The calendar as one table (2.3.3, 2.3.31 to 2.3.33)

### What the white paper holds

The brand cadence with its owner (WP p. 31); bandwidth as the first measure (WP p. 12); "employees are the users" (WP p. 17); the system a person can open and read (WP p. 11). Nothing that lays every rhythm side by side.

### Considerations

**The entry test.** Every rhythm on the calendar costs bandwidth, and bandwidth is first. A candidate test any proposed meeting, report, or check passes before it enters: it states its cost and what it buys; it has one owner (p. 139); it runs on the system rather than on someone's memory (WP pp. 05 to 06, 21); it sits in the pre-service window or after the close, never mid-service (the Realist's rule); it fixes a process, not a person (WP pp. 13, 17); and it enters as an experiment with a stated duration, check-in date, and criteria (p. 142). Her question for every meeting applies too: could this have been a written update or a line in the tracker (p. 142)?

**The frequency rule.** A leading indicator is read by the rhythm matched to how fast it can move: within a week, at the leads' review; within a month, monthly; a quarter's composite, at the house review. A lagging indicator is read once a quarter as the X-ray. Reading a rate faster than it can move trains the reader to ignore it; slower, and the damage is done (WP p. 22).

**The bandwidth ledger, in time and not in figures.** Per hourly person per month: the brief and the close, which already exist and are already funded (WP p. 26); fifteen minutes of check-in on the clock; one pulse question; a recognition when they choose; the review every three to six months; one paid shift per quarter if the gathering is approved. That is less than her cadence asks of anyone: no weekly team meeting beyond the brief, no biweekly 1:1, no monthly all-hands, no daily standup separate from the brief. Per lead per week: the leads' review with its hard stop, a slice of most pre-service windows for check-ins, the capture reviews the white paper already assigns (WP p. 13); per month, one conversation with a partner; per quarter, one memo and one review. Per partner: the weekly review, the monthly domain read and one lead conversation, the quarterly review, the annual block. The one cost that can exceed its budget is the check-in rotation on the Maitre d's windows, which is why the stretch is reported rather than absorbed.

**The Realist's closing read.** The crew that runs this cadence on a Friday is the green, short one. The brief survives a thirty-second version. The check-in's range lets the night move it. The leads' review has a hard stop. The house review sits at a closed hour. Nothing asks a cook or server to record anything at tempo that the system does not already capture. Whether it holds at month six is the reset's to read.

**Ownership, confirmed by the owners.** Her strongest warning is that lack of ownership, or leaders not honoring the structures, undermines the whole architecture (p. 139), and her advice is to spin up a process only once the leaders will implement it. The calendar's owner column is a proposal until each lead has read it and agreed, which is why 2.3.32 exists as its own action.

**The page for the team.** The calendar is a working table for the leads and the partners. The team needs one page, in every roster language, saying what happens daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, and yearly, who runs it, and where its record is. That page is the written form of the cadence's third channel, and its test is whether someone asked at a brief can say what happens this week.

## 9. Where Sŏn may diverge from the book

- **Two rhythms, not one.** She describes a single company cadence driven by the calendar year. Sŏn may run a build rhythm and a service rhythm that coexist for a year or more (section 1).
- **The day, not the quarter, as the unit.** Her cadence's centerpiece is the quarter. Sŏn's is the service, and the quarter is a read (sections 1, 6).
- **The check-in instead of the 1:1.** On the clock, in the pre-service window, placed by the system, with a page instead of a document (section 4).
- **No monthly all-hands by default.** A gathering costs a closed service and is decided, not assumed (section 6).
- **The reset at the gates rather than every six months** is an option, not a rule; her six months may win for the first year (section 7).
- **The QBR's financial sections are dropped.** Financials are not a source for this work; the lagging indicators are read on the Operations Lead's own surface (section 6).
- **One owner per structure, but two co-leads with no one above them.** Her rule assumes a leadership team with a top; the leads' review chair is where Sŏn's structure meets her rule (section 5).
- **Continuous care through the close.** Her process revisit is periodic; the white paper's learning loop updates a process the night it fails (WP p. 13), which carries part of what her six-month revisit does.
