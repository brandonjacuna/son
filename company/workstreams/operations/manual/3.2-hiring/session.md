# 3.2 Hiring: working-session guide

How to run the session on this chunk. The decisions are in `tasks.md`; the reasoning is in `considerations.md` ("cons. N" below). Nothing here is a verdict. This chunk is large enough that it will likely take two sittings: the loop and the decision (conversations 1 to 4), then the practical, references, offer, leadership, internal hiring, and calibration (5 to 9).

## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** Everything between a candidate applying and a person accepting an offer: what an interview reads, the stages by seat class, who interviews and how they are trained, what a scorecard holds, who decides and how fast, the paid practical, references, the offer, the two lead seats and the chef seat, hiring from inside, and the read that keeps interviewers consistent. It does not cover the pipeline, the seat description, or the applicant system (3.1), onboarding and the ninety-day plan (3.3), or what happens when a hire does not work out (3.4).

**What is already decided upstream.** Read before the session: 2.1 `decisions.md` (the principles, if version one exists; the chef seat's standing, 2.1.10; the partners' deadlock rule, 2.1.13), 2.2 `decisions.md` (the decision-rights rule beneath the partners, 2.2.2; the readiness test, 2.2.35 to 2.2.38), 2.3 `decisions.md` (the leads' review, the check-in, the person page), 4.1 `decisions.md` (the leadership-line list, levels, what a lead enters at, the salaried test, who scores an advancement assessment, the seat inventory), and 3.1's task list for what the seat description and applicant system settle. Where those are not yet decided, the brief says so; the decision here can still be made and marked as depending on them.

**Who else must agree.** Dominic on anything that binds both founders: how they decide a lead hire when they split (3.2.10), the compensation order (3.2.17), negotiation for the lead seats (3.2.19), the chef seat's process (3.2.31). The chef partner, once seated, on the kitchen's kit, rows, and stages; until then the kitchen entries are marked waiting. Counsel on the paid practical's status, the decline wording, reference questions, and the written offer (0.1).

**Why it sits here.** The book puts hiring after recruiting and before onboarding. At Sŏn the first hires are the two leads, and everything a lead candidate meets (the reads, the stages, the sheet, the offer rule) has to exist before their first interview, which is why most of this chunk is phased before the first hire even though the cohort's practicals come later.

### 3.2.3 Decide how a candidate's outside experience counts

**The question:** may a seat description ask for years or credentials, and may an interviewer count experience for or against a candidate?

**Why it matters now:** the anchors (3.2.26) are written differently if experience is evidence than if it is neutral, and the seat descriptions in 3.1 need the rule before the first posting.

**Openers:**
- Two candidates for the same floor seat: one has three years in a fine-dining room, one has run a busy family shop and never worked a restaurant. What do you want the interviewer to be able to conclude from that fact alone?
- Sideways: you entered coffee with no coffee background and asked a different question than the people around you (WP p. 3). What would a seat description written to exclude you have looked like, and who else does it exclude?
- The book warns that experienced candidates interview better (p. 215). Where have you seen polish beat capacity?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Requirement, evidence, or neutral? If evidence, at which read?
- Does the rule differ for the lead seats and the chef seat, which are hired into the center?
- Does fine-dining experience specifically get probed differently (the experience-trap questions), and does the anchor say so?

**What the book says:** overweighting experience relative to trajectory and aptitude to learn is a common mistake (p. 195); years of experience is a red herring for level (p. 199); probe experienced candidates for what they specifically did and whether it worked (p. 215).

**White-paper default:** entry is at the outside edge against documented competency requirements (WP p. 9, p. 11). No experience requirement appears anywhere; the white paper is silent on whether experience earns anything.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Skills-based hiring, dropping degree and years requirements from postings in favor of demonstrated ability, has spread across large employers over the last decade; the restaurant industry mostly never had the requirements to drop, but reads experience informally at the interview instead.

**Options:** cons. 2: (a) no requirement, experience neutral, probed where present; (b) no requirement, experience admissible as evidence at the competency read; (c) requirements on the lead and domain-owning seats only.

**Watch for:** an anchor that quietly rewards having seen a fine-dining room; a lead-seat rule that contradicts the edge rule without saying why.

**A finished answer:** one sentence for the edge seats and one for the center seats, and the anchor writers know what to do with a résumé.

**Needs agreement from:** no one else; the chef partner for the kitchen later.

### 3.2.4 Decide the stages each seat class runs, and who runs each

**The question:** who does a candidate for each kind of seat meet, in what order, for how long, and what is the smallest packet a decision may rest on?

**Why it matters now:** the stage notes (3.2.26), the scorecard set (3.2.27), the calendar (3.2.32), and the founders' hours all follow from this table. The lead candidates go through it first.

**Openers:**
- Walk a line cook candidate from application to answer, out loud, naming each person they meet by seat. Now do a Maitre d candidate. Where do the two paths part?
- Sideways: the white paper says format choice is a filter (WP p. 17): the serious candidate chooses in person. What does your loop do with the candidate who chose the phone screen and turned out to be the best one?
- The book caps the number of interviewers at eight. Your problem is the opposite: how many reads is too few for a hire that will hold a station alone?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Is there a second interviewer for seats that own a domain, and from where (the adjacent domain, the other lead)?
- Is a packet of one scorecard ever enough? If not, what is added for a seat with no practical and no dinner?
- How long is each stage, and does the house commit to that length on the candidate note?
- Does 3.1 keep the phone screen, and if so what may it read (must-haves and availability only, or more)?

**What the book says:** a clear description, a decided set of interviewers, an eight-person cap (four or five for well-understood roles), trained interviewers, a shared rubric split across interviewers, a narrowing first round, a committee or review, references, the offer; process weight flexes with volume (pp. 193 to 194). Infrequent roles do better with the same people interviewing every candidate, plus a cross-section from beyond the team (pp. 196 to 197).

**White-paper default:** a phone screen for high-volume roles; a first interview for everyone with the transparency sheet; a paid practical as the second interview for physical-skill roles; a dinner in the room as the final round for leadership and salaried roles; self-scheduling, language and format choice (WP p. 17 to 18, all stated outright).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Google's research, cited by the book, found diminishing returns past four interviewers (p. 193). Most restaurants run one interview and a tryout shift.

**Options:** cons. 1: (a) the seat-class table with an adjacent-domain second interviewer and a packet floor; (b) one loop for all seats; (c) the white paper's four stages and nothing more until the cohort shows what is missing.

**Watch for:** a stage added because it feels thorough rather than because it carries a read no other stage does; a loop the two founders cannot staff for a whole cohort.

**A finished answer:** a table (seat class, stages, runner by seat, reads per stage, length) and the packet floor, and Brandon can say who a runner candidate meets and why.

**Needs agreement from:** no one else now; the chef partner for the kitchen classes later.

### 3.2.5 Decide what the dinner in the room reads, and whether it is scored

**The question:** is the dinner an evaluation with a scorecard, a recruitment conversation, or both, and what does an evaluation of a person eating dinner actually read?

**Why it matters now:** the two lead candidates will sit this dinner before Sŏn has a room; what it is for has to be told to them beforehand (3.2.26), and if it is scored it needs a form (3.2.27).

**Openers:**
- Picture the dinner with the Maitre d candidate. Halfway through, what have you noticed that you could not have learned in the interview? Say it out loud. Is it something you would write on a scorecard?
- Sideways: the white paper draws a line between the visitor who owes the host grace and the customer the house owes (WP p. 25). At this dinner, which is the candidate?
- Where does the dinner happen before there is a room, and does the borrowed room change what you can read?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Scored or unscored? If scored, on which reads, with what small documented list (their treatment of the server, their read of the room, whether they hold the table without performing)?
- Who sits at the table: both founders, one, the other lead once hired?
- What is the candidate told the dinner is for?

**What the book says:** a sell chat late in the process, with someone central to the role or an executive, signals the candidate matters; candidates want to hear how the leader sees the role, the company's prospects, what excites the leader, and the challenges (p. 209).

**White-paper default:** for leadership and salaried roles the final round is a dinner in the room, evaluation and recruitment at once (WP p. 18, stated outright). Silent on what is evaluated.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Hospitality groups commonly take a senior candidate to dinner as part of the close; treating it as a scored stage with a documented list is uncommon, and the earlier work's version (a few open questions and one deliberate silence) is closer to a structured observation than an interview.

**Options:** (a) scored on a short documented list, reading the candidate as a customer, with a scorecard like any stage. Commits Sŏn to telling the candidate it is scored and to anchors for it. (b) Unscored recruitment, the last conversation before the offer. Commits Sŏn to the dinner carrying no read, and the decision resting on the other stages. (c) Both, split: the first half scored, the offer conversation after. Commits Sŏn to a clear line the candidate can see.

**Watch for:** the dinner becoming the place presence and charm get scored under another name; the dinner running after the decision is already made in everyone's head.

**A finished answer:** scored or not, what it reads if so, who sits, what the candidate is told, and where it happens before the room exists.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic, since he sits at it.

### 3.2.6 Decide who makes the hire decision, on what, and how fast

**The question:** for each seat class, who says yes or no, what do they say it on, and by when?

**Why it matters now:** the book's first rule of hiring at any scale is to settle this before interviewing (p. 198). The applicant system (3.1) has to be configured to it, and the decision-rights register (3.2.33) records it.

**Openers:**
- The packet for a server candidate completes at 4 p.m. on a Tuesday. Who opens it, what do they see first, and when does the candidate hear? Say the whole chain.
- Sideways: the white paper's answer to a table problem is that the server resolves it with authorized judgment and the system captures it (WP p. 13). If hiring worked the same way, who is the server, and what is the capture?
- Where in your career did a hiring meeting change a decision for the better? For the worse? What did the meeting have that a set of independent scorecards would not?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The domain lead alone, the lead plus a named second reader, or a small group?
- On what: a complete packet of independently submitted scorecards, or a discussion?
- What is the deadline from packet completion to decision, and from decision to the candidate's answer? Is "same day" a rule or an aim?
- Is there any approval above the decider? If not, what stops a hire made against every scorecard (3.2.39)?
- What is a packet "incomplete by content" (a stage that marked every read not assessed), and is the remedy a re-run or a send-back?

**What the book says:** a consultative process, run consistently, with the hiring manager as primary decider because they are accountable for the team's results, plus checks and balances such as a committee or executive review (p. 198); a hiring committee or candidate review to finalize; anyone on a committee may veto (pp. 203 to 204); the steward guards the candidate experience, listens to interviewers, brings the organization along, and decides for the company (pp. 204 to 205).

**White-paper default:** authority lives in the domain and the domain owner is accountable to its outcomes (WP p. 7 to 8); the decision is made quickly and development starts immediately (WP p. 18). Silent on a committee or approval.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. The hiring manager deciding with some check beside or above them is the common shape at companies that formalize hiring; Amazon adds a bar raiser's veto; the book describes Stripe's committee for disagreement, newness, or senior roles and a lighter review for the rest (p. 203).

**Options:** cons. 4: (a) the lead alone on a complete packet, same day; (b) the lead on the packet with a named second reader who may dissent but not overturn; (c) a small standing group discusses before the lead decides.

**Watch for:** "the lead decides" becoming "the lead decides after a chat with the founders," which is (c) without the honesty; a deadline the founders cannot keep for the cohort.

**A finished answer:** one sentence per seat class naming the decider, the basis, the two deadlines, and whether anything sits above, and Brandon can say what happens when the packet is incomplete.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic for the lead and chef seats.

### 3.2.7 Decide who may end a candidacy outright, and on what grounds

**The question:** what finding ends a candidacy regardless of everything else, who may raise it, and can anyone override it?

**Why it matters now:** the scorecard needs the flag (3.2.8), the training has to teach it (3.2.23), and the answer to "can anyone stop a hire the lead wants" shapes trust in the whole loop.

**Openers:**
- An assessor watching a practical sees the candidate speak to the steward in a way that would be a conduct problem on day one. The rest of the practical was excellent. What happens next, and who decides?
- Sideways: the white paper says the kitchen steward is not a support role but the tension that holds the sequence together (WP p. 11). Which finding at an interview tells you a person will treat that seat as interchangeable?
- The book gives every committee member a veto and calls it rare and important (p. 203). Who at Sŏn should be able to say "not this person" and be unoverridable, including by you?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Is the only veto a conduct disqualifier, or may an interviewer veto on any read?
- Who may raise it: any interviewer with evidence, the decider, a founder?
- What evidence must it carry (who, when, the act)?
- Can a founder override it? Can the candidate answer it?
- Is competence disagreement ever a veto, or always dissent (3.2.9)?

**What the book says:** anyone on a hiring committee can veto, extremely rare but important (pp. 203 to 204); a bar raiser can single-handedly veto (pp. 197 to 198).

**White-paper default:** cultural alignment is a read (WP p. 18); the white paper names no disqualifier. The principles (2.1.5) hold the baseline conduct standard if one has been written.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies with a written conduct standard commonly treat a clear breach of it during the process as ending the process, whoever observed it; a general veto on competence is much rarer outside Amazon's model.

**Options:** cons. 4: (a) only a conduct disqualifier, raisable by anyone with evidence, overridable by no one; (b) any interviewer may veto on any read; (c) only the decider or a founder may end a loop.

**Watch for:** a flag with no evidence field, which becomes a veto on presence; a rule that a founder may override, which makes the founder the decider.

**A finished answer:** the grounds, who may raise it, the evidence it needs, and whether anyone may override, in three sentences.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic, since it binds the founders too.

### 3.2.8 Decide what a scorecard holds and what it refuses

**The question:** what fields does an interviewer fill in, what values may each hold, what is refused, and does an interviewer ever see another's card before submitting?

**Why it matters now:** the scorecard is the whole record of a candidate; the applicant system (3.1) is configured to it, the training teaches it, the calibration read scans it, and the decline letter is written from it.

**Openers:**
- Read a scorecard from a hiring process you were part of, from memory. What was on it? Which line on it would you have been embarrassed to have the candidate read?
- Sideways: the white paper's review rule is that a formal review never contains new information (WP p. 19). If a candidate could read their own scorecard, what would have to be true of every line for that to be fine?
- An interviewer writes "great energy, would fit right in." What do you want the form to do with that?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Fields: an evidence line per question, a mark per read, a disqualifier flag, a recommendation? Anything else?
- Mark values: does not meet, meets, meets with evidence beyond, not assessed with reason? Is there a "not sure"?
- Recommendation values: advance or decline only, or a scale? Must a decline name a read?
- Is a free-text verdict field allowed? A numeric score? A composite?
- Independent submission before any discussion? Does an interviewer ever see a prior interviewer's card?
- Does anything on a scorecard ever reach the person's employee record?

**What the book says:** the two most common failures are not using the rubric and assessing likability (pp. 194 to 195); Stripe's scorecards run strong no to strong yes and are verified for leniency, strictness, and subjectivity (pp. 250 to 252); send interviewers brief feedback but not so much that you create bias (p. 212).

**White-paper default:** interviews assess specific competencies rather than gut feeling (WP p. 18). Silent on the form.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most applicant systems ship with a numeric or four-point recommendation scale and a free-text box; structured-hiring programs commonly require evidence per attribute and submission before the debrief. The book's own recommendation scale is four points (p. 250).

**Options:** (a) evidence lines, a three-state mark plus "not assessed with reason," a flag, advance or decline with a read named, no verdict or numeric field, independent submission, no prior card visible. Commits Sŏn to configuring the vendor tool against its defaults and to training people to write evidence. (b) The book's four-point scale with evidence required. Commits Sŏn to a scale the calibration read has to calibrate. (c) The vendor default. Commits Sŏn to whatever the tool assumes.

**Watch for:** a "not sure" value, which is where an unassessed read hides; a verdict field under a friendlier name ("overall impression").

**A finished answer:** the field list with allowed values, the refused list with a reason each, the independence rule, and the employee-record rule.

**Needs agreement from:** no one else; Dominic should see it since the tool is his domain.

### 3.2.9 Decide how a disagreement between an interviewer and the decider is carried

**The question:** when the decider goes against a submitted scorecard, what is written, who is told, and can it be appealed?

**Why it matters now:** without a committee there is no room where dissent is heard; if it is not written down it is lost, and the interviewer learns that their read did not matter.

**Openers:**
- The practical assessor recommended decline on the load row; the lead hires anyway. What does the assessor hear, when, and from whom?
- Sideways: the white paper says management absorbs blame from staff and post-mortems are process-focused (WP p. 17). What is the process-focused version of "I overrode your scorecard"?
- Have you ever been overridden on a hire and found out later by seeing the person on the schedule?

**Narrowing questions:**
- A written dissent line (the read, the interviewer's evidence, the evidence read as outweighing it)? A conversation? A second decider?
- Is the overridden interviewer told the same day? By the system or in person?
- Is there an appeal? If not, is the preserved scorecard the appeal?
- Are repeated overrides of one interviewer, or by one decider, read anywhere (3.2.39), and in what order (anchors first, interviewer second, decider third)?

**What the book says:** when a leader and the organization disagree, do more homework, then decide, explain, and ask for commitment; where a committee gets it wrong it usually had inexperienced participants (pp. 215 to 216).

**White-paper default:** none; silent.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies with a debrief meeting carry dissent in the room; those without one rarely record it at all, which is the failure mode this decision is about. If there is nothing more useful to say, that is the point.

**Options:** (a) a dissent line in the packet, the interviewer told the same day through the system, no appeal, patterns read by the calibration read. (b) A required conversation between decider and interviewer before the decision stands. (c) A second decider on any override. Each commits Sŏn to a different cost per disagreement and a different signal to interviewers.

**Watch for:** a dissent line the interviewer never sees; an appeal route that reopens a candidate's decision after they have been told.

**A finished answer:** what is written, who is told and when, whether there is an appeal, and where patterns go.

**Needs agreement from:** no one else.

### 3.2.10 Decide how the two founders decide a lead-seat hire when they disagree

**The question:** when Brandon's and Dominic's scorecards on a lead candidate disagree, who decides, and how?

**Why it matters now:** the two lead seats are the first hires; a split with no rule is a split resolved by whoever pushes harder, which is the load-bearing habit the white paper is written against (WP p. 7).

**Openers:**
- You want the Maitre d candidate; Dominic's card says does not meet on the alignment read, with evidence. What happens in the next forty-eight hours?
- Sideways: the book says a leadership hire needs one accountable decider because a group does not know what leadership it needs (p. 210). Sŏn has two owners of everything outside the kitchen. Which of you is accountable for the Maitre d's results, and does that settle it?
- Has 2.1.13 given you a deadlock rule? Does a hire feel like the kind of decision it was written for?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The domain-owning founder decides and writes why, the other's card preserved as dissent? Both must agree? The 2.1.13 rule directly?
- Is "more homework first" (another reference, an outside reader) a required step before any rule applies?
- Does the same rule cover the chef seat under 2.1.10's hire branch, where the founders are hiring an equal?

**What the book says:** the CEO or top executive should decide a leadership hire, on a consultative process, because the impact is large enough that one person must be accountable (pp. 210 to 211); disagreements get more references, an outside adviser meeting the candidate, and a clear summary for those with concerns (pp. 215 to 216).

**White-paper default:** two equal founders with distinct domains (WP p. 3, p. 5). The Maitre d and Operations Lead are parallel co-leads (WP p. 10). Silent on a split.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Two-founder companies commonly give the hiring decision to the founder the role reports to and require the other's consent only for the most senior roles; the book's own advice is one accountable decider with consultation (p. 211).

**Options:** cons. 9: (a) the domain-owning founder decides and explains; (b) both must agree, homework first, then 2.1.13; (c) 2.1.13 directly.

**Watch for:** a rule that only works if they never actually disagree; the Maitre d treated as Brandon's and the Operations Lead as Dominic's without saying so.

**A finished answer:** one sentence for the lead seats, one for the chef seat, recorded as pending Dominic's agreement.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic.

### 3.2.11 Decide whether the interview questions are public, and how far an interviewer may depart from them

**The question:** can a candidate see the questions before the interview, and what may an interviewer add, skip, or reword?

**Why it matters now:** the seat description (3.1) either carries the questions or does not, and the training (3.2.23) teaches whichever rule is chosen.

**Openers:**
- A candidate arrives having rehearsed answers to every published question. Is that a problem, or exactly the person who read the seat description?
- Sideways: the white paper says the absence of a clear path is where politics begin (WP p. 11). What is the interview version of a clear path?
- When you have departed from a script in an interview, what did you learn that the script would have missed? What did you lose?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Questions public, areas public, or nothing beyond the stage note? Anchors always private?
- May an interviewer skip a question? How is a skip recorded?
- Are probes scripted, free, or a fixed set the interviewer chooses from?
- Does the candidate's chosen language change anything beyond the language?

**What the book says:** prep candidates, don't coach them; explain the process without telegraphing questions (p. 193); the same questions of every candidate, with room to probe (p. 194).

**White-paper default:** the system tells candidates exactly what to expect at each step (WP p. 17); every position's competency requirements are visible to anyone (WP p. 11). The white paper does not say the questions themselves are public.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. A few employers publish their interview questions on the posting on the ground that it favors preparation over improvisation; most publish the areas. The book's line is the majority practice.

**Options:** cons. 2: (a) questions public, anchors private; (b) areas public; (c) nothing beyond the stage note.

**Watch for:** publishing questions and then scoring candidates down for prepared answers; "room to probe" becoming a different interview for every candidate.

**A finished answer:** the publication rule, the skip rule, the probe rule, in three sentences.

**Needs agreement from:** no one else.

### 3.2.12 Decide how the founders become trained interviewers before a training platform exists

**The question:** the white paper requires trained interviewers and a platform that does not exist yet; how do the first two interviewers get trained, and what is the record of it?

**Why it matters now:** the lead candidates' first interviews cannot honestly run under the white paper's own rule until this is answered, and the readiness test (3.2.45) will check for a dated record. The first leads inherit the same gap: the platform that would train them is built by the team they have not hired. 3.1 asked this question separately and it was merged here in the cross-chunk pass; 3.1's framing of the minimum is in the narrowing questions below.

**Openers:**
- Say the first three things you would want a new interviewer at Sŏn to unlearn from how the industry interviews. Now: who teaches you those?
- Name the last interview you ran that you would be embarrassed to have recorded. What would training have changed?
- Sideways: you were trained directly by Scott Rao and later trained others (WP p. 3). What did the training you received have that a manual would not? Can two people give each other that?
- Sideways: the book's Stripe recruiters were themselves interviewed on a rubric for structured thinking (pp. 229 to 245). Would you pass your own first stage?
- If you bought an interviewing course tomorrow, what would it teach that you would have to undo?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Documents first and the founders train each other; an outside trainer who signs off both founders; the first lead brings the skill?
- The minimum a founder completes before running any stage: the reads (3.1.5), the stage's questions, the rubric, and a practice interview scored by the other founder? Anything less, anything more?
- What are the practice runs on (consenting people who are not candidates)? How many? Who scores them: Dominic, an adviser, someone who has hired for these seats?

- Never; with the candidate's consent; only if the candidate is not filmed?
- Is creator scheduling checked against practical scheduling before either is committed?

**What the book says:** candidate experience matters independent of outcome (p. 196).

**White-paper default:** creators may be offered a seat at a training day (WP p. 31).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Nothing useful; most houses have neither a documented practical nor a creator program.

**Options:** never; with written consent; observer present but the candidate not filmed. Each commits Sŏn to a different check between the two calendars.

**Watch for:** consent asked of a candidate who wants the job.

**A finished answer:** one sentence and the scheduling check.

**Needs agreement from:** no one else.

### 3.2.39 Decide the calibration read: whether it runs, who runs it, what it reads, and what it may produce

**The question:** is there a standing read of completed packets that keeps interviewers consistent, and what may it do?

**Why it matters now:** without a committee, this is the only place a pattern across many packets can show: a lead drifting toward people who resemble them, one stage declining one kind of candidate, an anchor that is not working. It is also what 3.4 reads when a hire does not work out.

**Openers:**
- Six months after opening, the floor's later service period is hiring. Every hire is outgoing and quick. Nobody did that on purpose. What would have shown it, and when?
- Sideways: the white paper says lagging indicators are the X-ray after the fracture and the nervous system reads before the damage compounds (WP p. 12). What is hiring's leading indicator?
- Who reads every packet once a month, and what are they forbidden to do with what they see?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Monthly, or per N packets? One named owner, or read at the leads' review?
- What it scans for: presence language, unasked questions, misfiled evidence, off-description decline reasons, per-interviewer leniency or strictness as a pattern, hires against a "does not meet," declines contradicting every scorecard, whether any stage or source declines one kind of candidate at a different rate?
- What it produces: an anonymized record and a per-interviewer note? What it refuses: a candidate's name, a reopened decision, a numeric target, a verdict on an interviewer?
- How many consecutive periods must it have run on real packets before dinner's gate (3.2.45)?
- The outcome-side read (a hire against their packet at ninety days, and on any first-year departure) goes to 3.4; does this read's record exist in a form 3.4 can use?

**What the book says:** Elevate selected interviewers by reading their history against outcomes (p. 197); candidate review's mission is a consistent, efficient, transparent framework that controls for bias, with scorecards verified and feedback looped to interviewers (pp. 204 to 207, 250 to 252).

**White-paper default:** leading indicators (WP p. 12, p. 22); blame the process, fix the process (WP p. 17). Silent on hiring calibration.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Periodic calibration sessions where interviewers read the same feedback and compare marks are common in structured-hiring programs; at Sŏn's volume the same function has to be done on the packets that exist rather than a statistically meaningful sample.

**Options:** cons. 11: (a) a monthly read, one owner, anonymized; (b) findings at the leads' review, no separate owner; (c) no standing read.

**Watch for:** a read that produces a number per interviewer; a read that names a candidate; a read nobody has the hours for.

**A finished answer:** rhythm, owner before and after opening, the scan list, what it produces and refuses, the pre-gate run requirement, and the hand-off to 3.4.

**Needs agreement from:** no one else.

### 3.2.40 Decide the outside-domain check on entry-level packets after the founder stage ends

**The question:** once a founder no longer meets every candidate, what read from outside the domain, if any, sits on an entry-level packet?

**Why it matters now:** it is the gap the earlier work named and did not close: the book's bar raiser, committee, and executive review all put someone outside the team on every hire; if Sŏn has none of them, the outside check is monthly and after the fact. 3.1 asked the same question with a different timing, decided at the first mechanism reset (2.3.34) with the pipeline report (3.1.37) in hand; the two were merged here in the cross-chunk pass, and the timing is now one of the things to choose.

**Openers:**
- A year in, a floor lead hires four servers in a month. Who outside the floor has read any of those packets before the offers went out?
- If you learned in month four that the floor's hires were all the same shape, what would you want to have been recording since month one?
- Sideways: the white paper says the center is where the most connections intersect, not a rank (WP p. 9). Is an outside read on a packet a rank thing or a connections thing?
- Sideways: Amazon's bar raiser exists because a hiring manager under pressure to fill a seat lowers the bar without meaning to. Who at Sŏn feels that pressure most?
- What does it cost, in a trained person's hours per hire, to put a second domain on every packet?

**Narrowing questions:**
- None per packet, relying on the assessor's separation, the scored practical, the calibration read, and a hire-against-a-flag trigger? The founder stage kept indefinitely? The other lead or an adjacent-domain interviewer on every packet?
- Does the answer differ by seat class?
- When is it decided: now, before the first frontline packets, and reread at the reset; or held open until the reset, with the data 3.1.37 must hold for it named today (declines by stage and source, hires against a flag)?
- Either way, the decision-rights entry in 3.1.32 is updated when the answer lands.

**What the book says:** a bar raiser from outside the hiring team on every panel preserves the standard once leadership can no longer meet every candidate (pp. 197 to 198); Google's founders reviewed every packet, then delegated (pp. 198 to 199).

**White-paper default:** a company built to run without the founders (WP p. 6); two parallel leads (WP p. 10).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Amazon's answer is the bar raiser; most small companies' honest answer is that no one outside the team reads the packet, and they rely on the manager.

**Options:** cons. 11 on the check itself. On timing: (a) decide now and reread at the reset. Commits Sŏn to an answer before any data, revisable. (b) Hold until the reset. Commits Sŏn to naming today what 3.1.37 records, and to the founder stage or nothing standing in until then.

**Watch for:** keeping the founder stage "for now" with no end condition, which is (b) unadmitted; deciding now without data while calling it final; keeping the founders' stage by default because nobody raised it at the reset.

**A finished answer:** one sentence, the cost in hours, whether it differs by seat class, and when it is decided; if held to the reset, the data 3.1.37 must hold for it and the reset date it is on.

**Needs agreement from:** Dominic if a founder stays in.

### 3.2.42 Decide how an internal candidate is hired, and what those not chosen are owed

**The question:** when a seat opens and the ready-now pool holds someone, what do they run, is the seat also posted outside, and what do those not chosen receive?

**Why it matters now:** the advancement path is on the transparency sheet at the first interview; the internal loop is the promise behind it, and internal promotion rate is a tracked measure (WP p. 23).

**Openers:**
- A server has completed the modules, passed the assessment, and sat in the pool for two months. A seat opens. What is the next thing that happens to them, and who makes it happen?
- Sideways: the book says never run a sham process (p. 213). If the pool holds one person and you interview them anyway, what is the interview for?
- Two people in the pool for one seat. Both take the practical on the same rows. One is chosen. What does the other hear, the same day, and what changes on their record?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Pool read before any external search? What must be on record for an outside lead hire once the pool could hold someone?
- What an internal candidate runs: the seat's practical on the same rows as an outside candidate; for a lead seat, the lead block and the dinner too; references skipped because the record serves?
- Outside candidates ever met to validate an internal choice?
- Who sees who is in a pool? Is the seat named on any public schedule?
- Those not chosen: rows in writing the same day, pool standing kept with a next assessment date, nothing else on their record?

**What the book says:** decide first whether there is one internal candidate or several; never a sham process; a lightweight loop for several, with feedback to those not chosen and confidentiality; meeting a few outside candidates can validate the choice (pp. 213 to 215).

**White-paper default:** the next skill set unlocks after a set period, the assessment auto-schedules, a ready person with no seat enters a ready-now pool (WP p. 11, p. 14); the goal is internal succession to the point where the building rarely hires a manager from outside (WP p. 19). The white paper's two accounts of what triggers advancement (the system alone, or a lead's sign-off, WP p. 11 and p. 19) are 4.1.11's to reconcile.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies that publish a promotion-from-within rate usually pair it with internal visibility of open roles before external posting; interviewing an internal candidate for form's sake is widely recognized as corrosive.

**Options:** cons. 10: (a) pool first, practical as the test, no outside candidates, feedback by rows; (b) the book's lightweight loop with a few outside candidates; (c) the seat is always posted inside and out and everyone runs the same loop.

**Watch for:** "we'll just see who else is out there" on a seat the pool already holds a person for; a not-chosen person's rows landing on their employee record.

**A finished answer:** the pool rule, what an internal candidate runs by seat class, the outside-candidate rule, the visibility rule, and the owed list.

**Needs agreement from:** no one else; depends on 4.1.11.

### 3.2.43 Decide whether a domain's own team members ever interview for their lead's seat

**The question:** after opening, when a lead seat is filled, does anyone from that lead's own domain sit as an interviewer?

**Why it matters now:** the book says direct reports are stakeholders and should not be excluded even when they may react badly (pp. 211 to 212); the earlier work kept them off by structural rule and filed the question.

**Openers:**
- The Maitre d is leaving. Two servers have worked under them for a year and will work under the next one. Do they meet the candidates? What would they read?
- Sideways: the white paper says the outside edge feels the cultural temperature of the building before anyone else (WP p. 9). Is that a reason to have them on the loop, or a reason to protect them from it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Never; one trained team member on one stage; the team meets finalists unscored?
- What would they read that a lead from another domain would not?

**What the book says:** identify the main stakeholders, including direct reports; don't exclude people likely to react negatively (pp. 211 to 212); quietly gauge reactions before an internal promotion (p. 214).

**White-paper default:** silent.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Team interviews of a prospective manager are common in technology companies and rare in restaurants.

**Options:** never; one trained member on one stage with a read named; an unscored meeting with finalists. Each commits Sŏn to a different answer to whether a team chooses its lead.

**Watch for:** a team interview that is really a popularity read.

**A finished answer:** one sentence with the stage and the read, or "never" with the reason.

**Needs agreement from:** no one else.
