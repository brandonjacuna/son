# 4.3 Team changes and restructuring: considerations

What the white paper holds, what the earlier work found worth keeping, the options and what each commits Sŏn to, and where Sŏn may diverge from the book. Topics follow the book's order (pp. 286 to 294). "WP p. N" is the white paper; "cons. N" is a section of this file. Nothing here is a decision.

Vocabulary, in plain words: a **structure change** is any change to seats, lines, domains, or reporting, recorded on the structure change entry (4.1.33). A **departure read** is what the house reads in the week someone leaves. The **ready-now pool** is the white paper's list of people who have completed the next track and are waiting for a seat (WP p. 11). A **designation** is a per-service assignment of a live function (WP p. 10). The failure modes and the evidence line are as 4.2 defines them.

## 1. After the diagnosis: sequencing

**Book.** The plan is ten percent, execution ninety. Decide whether new leaders come first or vision and inspiration come first; sometimes both, and the order matters. An effort-impact matrix helps. Manage stakeholders' expectations while the change runs (pp. 287 to 288).

**White paper.** The stack is the nervous system; a degraded one cannot feel the rest (WP p. 12). Sequence corruption's fix is system maintenance (WP p. 14). Silent on an order among fixes.

**From the earlier work.** A causal order among the modes: sequence corruption first, because a broken information flow corrupts every other read; node overload second; skill invisibility and cooling failure next; tension slack last, because it is the only mode whose read reaches a person and it should be read after the others are known. The effort-impact matrix was absorbed as the reset's existing habit of reading every mechanism against its cost and benefit (2.3.30).

**Options.**
- (a) A fixed order among the modes, written on the change guide. Commits Sŏn to fixing the record before fixing the people, even when the people problem is louder.
- (b) An effort-against-impact read at the reset, case by case. Commits Sŏn to the book's tool and to the reset carrying the ordering each time.
- (c) Case by case, with the reason recorded on the change entry. Commits Sŏn to leads' judgment and to the entry showing the reasoning.

## 2. What triggers a change, and what never does

**Book.** Two legitimate triggers: structure no longer matches strategy (a new product, new priority segments, often at planning time), and a talent change (a leader leaves, is let go, or changes roles). A third is commonly misused: an underperforming team, where a reorg masks a talent problem that should be addressed directly. Reorgs are not a catchall (pp. 288 to 290). Restructuring can signal growth, and normalizing that it will recur is part of leading through growth; too much has real costs (p. 288).

**White paper.** The two leads are parallel; bundling them is the load-bearing model's final failure mode (WP p. 10). Growth is outward, not upward; the web never acquires altitude (WP p. 09). Growth in practice is adding service periods, each opened on a test (WP p. 36); roughly eight leadership lines run the whole day (WP p. 22). Universal problems are design problems (WP p. 17).

**From the earlier work.** There is no org chart to reorganize in the book's sense. The first trigger's real form is a service period chartered or closed (2.1.15, 2.2.36) or a leadership line added under 4.1.35. The second is a departure, handled as tension re-establishment (cons. 6) with the lead change on the change entry, not a rethink of the domain. The third is the white paper's own rule: read the mode before treating a domain's trouble as a people problem. Merging two domains under one lead was declined as a step back toward the general manager; splitting a domain happens only through 4.1.35's trigger.

**Options.**
- (a) Triggers in Sŏn's terms only (a period, a line under 4.1.35, a lead's departure, a repeat finding that resolves to structure); no merges; no splits outside 4.1.35. Commits Sŏn to a structure that changes rarely and at known moments (4.1.32), and to living with a mediocre domain for a cycle while its mode is read.
- (b) The book's triggers as written, read by the founders at each reset. Commits Sŏn to the founders' judgment on "structure no longer matches strategy" without a test.
- (c) (a) plus a stated emergency trigger (a legal, safety, or partner-level event). Commits Sŏn to defining the emergency and who calls it (2.2.7 for the service; here for the structure).

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Restaurants that add a service period most often do it by stretching the dinner team's leads across it; the white paper's own case (WP p. 02) is that this is the structural error, so the trigger for structure here is the period's charter, not the period's opening.

## 3. Pace and telling: the ice cream on the counter

**Book.** Take time deciding whether a change is needed; once anyone beyond your manager and an HR partner knows, move fast, or misinformation and instability follow. Three phases: about a month to decide and design with a very small group, every person accounted for; one to two weeks of buy-in with each affected person, two or three conversations, listen then be firm, do not rethink the plan because one person reacts badly; one to two days to tell the most affected first, then everyone, then move into the new structure (pp. 290 to 294). Charles Phillips's subway announcer: say plainly that things are changing, offer the choice to come along, and the ones who stay commit more fully (pp. 297 to 298).

**White paper.** No surprises: a formal review never contains new information (WP p. 19). Silent on how a change is told.

**From the earlier work.** A change is proposed one week and decided the same or next; the reason is written before the proposal. The order of disclosure controls itself: the leads' review first, then the affected person at their own check-in before any record or brief, then the record, then the floor at the next brief when it becomes operationally relevant. A change reaching the floor before the affected person's check-in is a process failure. The conversations phase became the person's check-in plus a scheduled follow-up, with their reaction recorded in their words. 1.2.2 already decides what the team hears before a decision and after; 4.1.12 fixed the channels; 4.8 will hold the wider communication norms.

**Options.**
- (a) The earlier work's order: leads' review, the person's check-in, the record, the brief; a fixed window in reviews. Commits Sŏn to the check-in being able to carry hard news, which 2.3.9 says it refuses for discipline and verdicts; a change is neither, but the line needs drawing.
- (b) The book's three phases with their clocks, run in miniature. Commits Sŏn to a design phase that excludes the leads until the plan exists, which in a house of two leads means the founders design alone.
- (c) (a) for seat-level changes; (b) for a line or a lead. Commits Sŏn to two procedures and a rule for which applies.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Companies that handle restructuring well almost always tell the directly affected person one-to-one before any group hears it, and compress the gap between the first telling and the general one to a day or two; the ones that leak do so in the gap.

## 4. Structure versus stability: the first quarter

**Book.** A reorg never feels well timed; if aligned that it is needed, push through and accept short-term instability. But after frequent restructuring, delay for stability, especially if the contemplated structure is not itself a steady state and another change would follow (pp. 290 to 291). Be transparent about an interim structure (p. 292).

**White paper.** Dinner opens first and runs alone for at least a quarter "so the heart is solid before anything is added to it" (WP p. 36). Open items are named rather than hidden (WP pp. 15, 22).

**From the earlier work.** A first-quarter hold: the diagnostic reads everything and changes almost nothing on the room's mechanisms; anything it would change is logged with its date for the first reset, so the reset opens on a list rather than memory. Structural changes happen at gates and at the reset; seat and role corrections may happen between. Pre-opening structural gaps temporarily routed to a founder are already treated as a stated interim (4.1), which is the book's transparency advice in practice.

**Options.**
- (a) A hold on structure, mechanisms, and parameters for dinner's first quarter; corrections and safety changes proceed; the log opens the reset. Commits Sŏn to a quarter of living with what it built, and to a reset that arrives with a long list.
- (b) A hold on structure only; mechanisms and parameters may change by entry. Commits Sŏn to the tempo rules of 2.3.2 doing the protecting.
- (c) No hold; 4.1.32's occasions and the change entry are enough. Commits Sŏn to the founders' restraint.

## 5. Breaking the structure for one person

**Book.** Do not restructure around a single person, out of attachment or a retention threat; teams lose trust, and the person is set up to fail if the structure does not match strategy. Two exceptions: bridging toward a future structure when there are not yet enough leaders, transparently; and a genuine high performer who could take on more. The test is whether you can explain why (pp. 291 to 292).

**White paper.** Designations are not permanent elevations (WP p. 10). Progression develops connections, not altitude (WP p. 11). Titles are to be scale-independent and not concept-specific (WP p. 15). Compensation recognizes cross-strand fluency (WP p. 11). Silent on a retention threat.

**From the earlier work.** Declined entirely and by design: inventory rows carry no names; a seat is not invented or retitled for a person; a designation is never made permanent; a lead vacancy is filled from the pool, with a written justification if not. What survives from the book is its warning: a team loses trust when it senses structure serving one person. The pressure case (a valued person about to leave) was routed to the pool and to pay (5.5), never to structure.

**Options.**
- (a) Never. Commits Sŏn to losing a valued person rather than bend the structure, and to 5.5 carrying the whole answer to "what can we offer."
- (b) The book's two exceptions, each with a written why on the change entry. Commits Sŏn to a bridge rule (an interim seat, named as interim, with a date) and to the founders being able to explain the high-performer case to the floor.
- (c) A bridge rule only, no high-performer exception. Commits Sŏn to interim seats being the one way structure flexes for a person.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Retention counter-offers built on a new title are widely reported to buy a short stay and cost the team's trust; the pattern is common enough that many companies forbid title changes as retention tools and route the case to pay or to a real open seat.

## 6. A departure as tension re-establishment

**Book.** A talent change is a legitimate trigger; the more senior the person, the wider the ripple (pp. 288 to 289).

**White paper.** Thread removal is tension loss, not slot vacancy; adjacent nodes compensate at cost (WP pp. 8 to 9). When a key person leaves, the knowledge does not leave: it lives in documented processes, captured preferences, skill maps; the pool surfaces who is furthest along; the cost is "bounded, temporary, calculable" (WP p. 14). Also: belonging is to people, a server's regulars may follow them to another property (WP p. 25), and customer knowledge must not live in a server's memory because memory leaves when the server does (WP p. 27). Two accounts of what a departure costs sit side by side.

**From the earlier work.** Three passes. In the week of the departure: what the seat actually held (the row, the person's own records); which adjacent seats and designations now carry it (a leads' review note); the pool check before any outside posting; and whether what the person held was in the system or in their head, each "in their head" a documentation or process gap and a finding. Over following weeks: whether adjacent strain rises, showing where the tension went. Once refilled: whether adjacent strain falls back, which is how the read closes. This is how "calculable" becomes concrete without money: services of compensation and a count of gaps found. Extensions: a lead's departure gains a paid handoff block and pool-priority handling; a founder's departure is a governance event on the operating agreement (2.1.1) running on the same records.

**Options.**
- (a) The three-pass read, owned by the domain lead, closing at the leads' review. Commits Sŏn to a record per departure and to treating gaps found as findings on the system, not on the person who left.
- (b) A shorter read: pool check and coverage only. Commits Sŏn to not learning what the seat held until the next person struggles.
- (c) The book's approach: the manager assesses the gap and hires. Commits Sŏn to a backfill model the white paper argues against (WP p. 09).

**Divergence.** The book treats a departure as an event to manage; the white paper treats it as a measurement of the system. Both can be true; the departure read is where they meet.

## 7. What the team hears when someone leaves

**Book.** Tell the most affected first, then everyone within about a day; the leader's attitude toward the change spreads (pp. 293 to 294).

**White paper.** Post-mortems are private and process-focused (WP p. 17). Belonging is to people (WP p. 25), so a server's regulars will notice. Silent on what the floor is told.

**From the earlier work.** The floor hears a short factual coverage note at the next brief (which seat is open, who covers its functions this service), nothing about the person, no reason, no farewell, no judgment; treated as routine coverage information. This reconciled earlier wording that had drifted. 3.4.3 already decides what the team is told for a reversed hire in the first weeks.

**Options.**
- (a) The coverage note alone. Commits Sŏn to a house where people leave without being spoken of, which protects the person and may read as cold to the floor; 4.5's norms should say why.
- (b) The coverage note plus a farewell the departing person chose (a line they wrote, or nothing). Commits Sŏn to asking every departing person one question, including those leaving badly.
- (c) The book's announcement: the lead tells the team, with context. Commits Sŏn to leads deciding what context is safe to give, and to counsel's view (4.3.14).

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most employers' guidance to managers is a factual statement that the person has left and who to go to in the meantime, with no reason given; the reason is withheld for the departed person's privacy and the employer's exposure. Farewell messages are usually the person's own choice.

## 8. An open seat before it is a hiring problem

**Book.** Talent gaps in skill or will mean real work; sequence it (p. 287).

**White paper.** Bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results (WP p. 12). Austin's labor market is tight (WP p. 16). Hiring reads competency, not instinct, and speed is respect (WP p. 18).

**From the earlier work.** Before an unfilled seat is called a recruiting failure: read adjacent strain; whether others are double-covering; whether the row itself describes a job the labor pool supplies (entry horizon, shift shape, physical demands). Only then a hiring-quality question, answered by 3.4.17. The held-open case: a lead holds a seat open rather than lower the bar; if adjacent strain climbs across consecutive weekly reviews, the answer under "bandwidth before hiring corners" is to cut coverage or hours elsewhere before the bar drops, a call the earlier work placed above the lead. A domain that repeats (open seats, departures) is read up a level, in order: is the seat count wrong (a charter problem); is the row wrong; is the lead's own seat overloaded. A domain producing repeated departures is read as cooling failure first, never as a read on its lead's character. A period that keeps failing its metrics is read against its charter and the interface test (4.1.2, 2.2.34) before any people question.

**Options for who cuts coverage.**
- (a) The lead, on their own domain, recorded. Commits Sŏn to leads being able to reduce a service's coverage without asking.
- (b) The partners, on the leads' review's finding. Commits Sŏn to a week's delay and a founder decision on every such case.
- (c) The leads' review jointly, since a cut in one domain lands on the other. Commits Sŏn to the cross-domain rule (2.2.3) carrying it.

**How others have handled it.** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Restaurants short a server routinely close a section or cap covers for the night rather than seat the full room; the question here is only whether that instinct becomes a rule, and whose.

## 9. A designation held by one person for a quarter

**Book.** A structure bent around one person costs the team's trust (pp. 291 to 292).

**White paper.** Designations mark accountability in the moment and are not permanent elevations (WP p. 10).

**From the earlier work.** Three branches at the reset: the lead's own scheduling or hours; only one person is trained to hold it (a training gap, 3.3); the lead names the same person by preference (the rotation floor of 4.1.19 answers). Never grounds to make the designation a seat; never a performance read on the holder. Read from the brief's designation block.

**Options.** (a) the three branches at the reset as written; (b) the pattern surfaces weekly and the lead answers it at the leads' review; (c) no read, the rotation floor is enough. Each commits Sŏn to a different amount of attention on the designation block.

## 10. Where Sŏn may diverge from the book, in one place

- The book restructures teams; Sŏn may only charter periods, add lines under a trigger, and re-establish tension after departures (cons. 2).
- The book's design phase excludes the affected; Sŏn's leads' review may be where a change is first spoken (cons. 3).
- The book allows two exceptions for one person; Sŏn may allow none (cons. 5).
- The book manages a departure; Sŏn may measure it (cons. 6).
- The book announces; Sŏn may only cover (cons. 7).
