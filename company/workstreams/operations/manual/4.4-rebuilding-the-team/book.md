# 4.4 (Re)building the team: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 4, pp. 294 to 303, with the career conversations exercise at pp. 364 to 367. Full text: `sources/extraction/build/s09/book.md`, `sources/extraction/build/s09/exercises.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## The bedrock is the people

Whether a team is newly hired, inherited, or restructured, its bedrock is the people on it. The next step is to evaluate each person's skill and will, form a view of their contribution and potential, and take time to deeply understand each team member individually and collectively (p. 294).

## Career conversations

A career conversation is a scheduled, dedicated conversation with each team member about their career and development goals. She holds these with everyone she manages and finds they consistently surface motivations and how a person's aspirations do or do not fit the team's mission (pp. 294 to 295).

Timing: after a few months of working together, so there is enough trust to talk openly, and before any formal performance review. This is not an interview and not the place for "what's your five-year plan," a question that tends to shut people down. Instead, use it to help the person reflect on the choices they have made across their career and why, which tends to surface values and motivations relevant to the working relationship (Operating Principle 1: build self-awareness to build mutual awareness). Those insights become useful later, at moments like reorgs or performance reviews, or when deciding what to delegate to someone (pp. 295 to 296).

Three purposes (p. 296): showing the person you care about them and their career; understanding their broader narrative arc, their motivations, past choices, and development aspirations; and beginning to outline a longer-term career direction you can both return to.

**The exercise (pp. 364 to 367; a starter guide also appears in the book's chapter appendix at p. 335).** A 60-minute conversation (30 to 45 minutes for someone straight out of school). Pre-conversation: tell the team what a career conversation is and why you are having one with each person, then send a same-day-before reminder of the goals so people do not over-prepare. The conversation itself walks through: where they grew up; where they went to school and why; what they did right after school and why; their favorite and least favorite jobs and how they chose their roles; and, more speculatively, what kind of work and life they picture five years out, without pinning it to a title. Throughout, the guidance is to listen actively, take notes, and keep asking why. Wrap up by recapping, sharing your notes, and writing down two or three development goals to track together.

## A note on how change gets explained

She includes a short reflection from Charles Phillips on introducing change directly and early, using the image of a subway announcer redirecting a train: people are told plainly that things are changing, offered the choice to come along, and the ones who stay commit more fully (pp. 297 to 298).

## DRIs

A sidebar on the directly responsible individual, a term Apple coined for hardware programs to name who is accountable for a goal or ship date. A DRI can be a team leader or an individual contributor; the job is to make sure a project has what it needs and that decisions get made fast enough to keep executing. Stripe uses the term because it centers accountability rather than hierarchy: even a DRI who is not the decision-maker owns getting the decision made. DRI structures can nest, one DRI relying on another running a related project. Like the Code Yellow (p. 266), a shared term like this works when it is well understood, taught in onboarding, and used consistently (p. 298).

## Delegating

Delegating is central to a manager's own capacity, to developing the team, and to the team's overall impact. It takes two forms: ordinary work assignment, the day-to-day responsibilities that should already be covered in 1:1s, and one-off assignments outside the regular flow of work (pp. 296 to 297).

Under-delegating managers tend to be over-involved and to insist on reviewing everything before it leaves the team; a lasting team is hard to build this way because people never get to take on real ownership. Signs: employees bring problems but not solutions, most decisions need you personally, things fall apart when you are away, and you are too buried in firefighting to do strategic work (pp. 298 to 299).

Over-delegating managers make people feel trusted and empowered but drift too far from the work to notice when someone is in over their head, or hand out work people are not ready for without support. Signs: consistently low-quality output, projects going off the rails before you notice, reports feeling overwhelmed, and not being able to describe your team's recent, current, and upcoming work in a detailed conversation with your own manager or a peer (p. 299).

Delegating is inefficient up front and efficient later; it takes real time to develop people so they can produce quality work at scale, and it can frustrate task-oriented people in the short run, but the long-term payoff is the point (p. 299).

## When to delegate

Her framework echoes Jeff Bezos's Type 1 and Type 2 decisions, on two axes: how high or low the impact is, and whether the outcome is a trapdoor (hard to reverse) or adjustable. Examples: a ten-year office lease is high impact and a trapdoor; public website copy for a launch is high impact but adjustable; internal content or an offsite agenda is low impact and adjustable; a test email to a small group of prospects is low impact but a trapdoor once sent (pp. 299 to 301).

The rule of thumb: delegate everything that is not high impact and a trapdoor. Two kinds of exceptions exist. Sometimes a high-impact, trapdoor decision is worth delegating anyway, to grow a trusted high performer, with an explicit expectation that they keep you closely informed. And sometimes a manager should personally take on adjustable or low-impact work, for three reasons: modeling what good looks like (paired with letting reports later practice it themselves, in a "see one, do one, teach one" progression); genuinely urgent work that has not yet been set up to delegate, which should prompt fixing that gap afterward; and resource constraints, which is itself a signal that the team is not scaled adequately (pp. 301 to 302).

## How to delegate, and to whom

The skill-will matrix returns here: delegate most to high skill, high will people, and more selectively to high will, low skill people so they can build the missing skill. Discomfort delegating to someone on the team is itself a signal to examine, and to address through feedback and performance management (Chapter 5) (p. 302).

Her steps for a delegation conversation, which can double as a shared document for someone earlier in their career (pp. 302 to 303):

1. Bottom-line the assignment: the context, why it matters, and the goals.
2. Explain why the assignment fits this person, tying it to their skills or development goals and, where relevant, their career conversation or quarterly goals.
3. Set clear deliverables: what the finished work should look like, co-created with more senior people.
4. Discuss the timeline and whether it is realistic against their other work.
5. Get buy-in: ask whether they actually want this, with more conversation for a bigger ask.
6. Outline next steps together.
7. Agree how you will stay informed and when you will check back in.
