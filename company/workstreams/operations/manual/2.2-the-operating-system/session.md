# 2.2 The operating system: working session

The guide for the session (or sessions) that decides this chunk. Task numbers are `tasks.md`'s; "cons. N" is a section of `considerations.md`; "WP p. N" is the white paper. Nothing here is a decision. Every brief ends with what a finished answer looks like, so the conversation knows when to stop.

One vocabulary line to hold through the whole session: "the operating system" here is the human system the book describes (goals, metrics, owners, mechanisms, communication). The software is "the stack" or "the OS surface." When Brandon says "the system," ask which one he means.

## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** The book's Chapter 2 middle section (pp. 84 to 134): once the founding documents say why the company exists, the operating system says who owns what, what is measured, how progress is reviewed, and how information moves. The book assumes an org chart supplies the ownership structure; Sŏn has no chart of that kind, so this chunk also writes the decision-rights rule the book leaves unstated (cons. 2). It ends with a test: the conditions under which "the operating system is running" can be answered yes or no before dinner opens (cons. 10).

**Where it sits.** After 2.1 (the founding documents) and before 2.3 (the operating cadence). This chunk decides that a mechanism exists, who is in it, and what it reads first. Every rhythm, interval, and window is 2.3's. If the conversation drifts into "how often," park it for 2.3.

**What is already decided upstream.** Check `manual/2.1-founding-documents/decisions.md` before opening. This chunk leans on: the mission and its entity (2.1.2); the long-term goals, which every goal page and charter metric must name as a parent (2.1.3); whether "bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results" is a rule or a description (2.1.4), which changes 2.2.16 and 2.2.23; who owns the OS surface (2.1.7); the seat descriptions (2.1.8, 2.1.9, 2.1.11); how the partners decide and break deadlocks (2.1.13), which is the top of every escalation path here; the service period as the charter unit (2.1.14) and dinner's charter (2.1.15); the founding document and its versioning rule (2.1.17, 2.1.18); and how the founding documents reach the team (2.1.20). Where a 2.1 decision is still open, note it and go on; most briefs can be worked with the default assumption in place.

**Already decided inside this chunk.** Brandon ruled on 2026-09-13 that the floor manager and the Maitre d are one seat (`decisions.md`). Every "owner of the room" below means the Maitre d, or the room designation named in the shift brief on a service the Maitre d does not work. Invite him to restate his reasoning in his own words at the opening so `decisions.md` carries it.

**Who else must agree.** Nearly every decision here is "agreed by both seated founders": Dominic's domain holds the stack, the financial model, and every financial value inside a range or threshold. The chef partner, once seated, rewrites the kitchen's half of the register, the emergency authority, the metrics, and the goal pages; until then those parts are placeholders. 2.2.24 (the data hub of record) is Dominic's action outright, and 2.2.25, 2.2.33 cannot finish without it. Where Brandon has a position and Dominic has not yet weighed in, record the position and mark it "pending agreement from Dominic."

**How to run it.** Twenty-five decisions is more than one sitting. The agenda below groups them into four sittings that follow the phases in `tasks.md`; the first sitting is the one the first interviews depend on. Take them in any order he wants, but 2.2.1 and 2.2.2 shape everything after them and are worth doing first.

## 3. Briefs

### 2.2.1 Decide the units that carry goals, metrics, and review, and the direction goals ladder

**The question:** what are the things at Sŏn that get a goal page, a set of metrics, and a review, and do goals flow down from the center or up from the unit that holds the information?

**Why it matters now:** every later task in this chunk names a unit: the register is per position inside a domain (2.2.4), the goal pages are per unit (2.2.27), the mechanisms are placed at units (2.2.29), the team home has a page per unit (2.2.15). Get this wrong and the book's cascade gets rebuilt by default under the white paper's vocabulary (cons. 1).

**Openers:**
- The book says company, division, team, individual. Walk the building and name what you see instead. Where does the kitchen sit: inside dinner, beside dinner, or across every period?
- Sideways: the early morning has been open three months. Its lead writes a goal about the customer record traveling to dinner. Who told them to write it, and who would have known to?
- The white paper says "no top, no bottom," a center and an edge (WP p. 09). Say what that means for who sets a goal, in one sentence, without the word "web."
- Where would a person's development goal live: on their own page, on their domain's, or on their period's?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Four units (company, service period, domain, person), or fewer? Is the domain a unit that carries goals, or only ownership?
- Direction: set by the unit that holds the information and laddered up to a long-term goal (2.1.3), or set at the center and replicated down, or both with a rule about which goals go which way?
- The period and the domain intersect (the same kitchen serves every period). Whose page carries a goal that sits in both?
- Name the cost of the answer out loud: no division and no team means no manager above each person; the cascade means a manager above each person.

**What the book says:** the operating system replicates from company to division to team to individual; goals and metrics replicate down (pp. 84 to 86, 102 to 103, 115). A team charter cannot override a bad operating system (p. 85).

**White-paper default:** four intersecting units (company, service period, domain, person), goals laddering up, inferred from the structure and never stated (WP pp. 07 to 11, 33 to 34). Two caveats worth saying aloud: Sŏn is not self-managed; the founders own their domains and set the company's goals (cons. 1).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Google's OKRs, the book's own example, cascade from company to team but were always meant to be written partly bottom-up; the book credits the "set as close to the customer as possible" rule to the same source (pp. 104 to 111).
- Toyota-style production systems put the authority to stop the line with the person at the station, and set targets at the line, which is the nearest large-scale precedent for "the edge holds the information."

**Options:** (a) build on the web: four intersecting units, goals ladder up, ownership in the domain. Commits Sŏn to writing the decision-rights structure the chart would otherwise supply (2.2.2) and to testing the claim (2.2.38). (b) the book's cascade in Sŏn's vocabulary: leads as divisions, stations and sections as teams. Commits Sŏn to a manager above each person and to the parallel-leads structure existing only on paper. (c) a hybrid: cascade above the leads, web below. Commits the founders to being the integration layer, which is the memory failure the white paper names (WP p. 13). Depth: cons. 1.

**Watch for:** choosing (a) in words and (c) in practice, which shows up later as every escalation resolving to a founder (2.2.34 will catch it); "team" used loosely for the floor crew when he means the period.

**A finished answer:** the units named, the direction stated with its exception if any, the book's division and team ruled out or adopted on purpose with the cost said aloud, and Brandon can say where a person's development goal and a cross-period goal each live.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 2.2.2 Decide the decision-rights rule beneath the partner level

**The question:** what is the general form every grant of authority below the partners takes, and what rules govern writing one?

**Why it matters now:** the white paper hands out decision rights seven times and never states the rule (cons. 2). The candidate sheet promises every hire "their direct lead" and "communication expectations" at the first interview (WP p. 18); a lead's sheet cannot state their range until this rule exists, and the founders must write the leads' ranges before the leads are interviewed.

**Openers:**
- The server with the bottle (WP p. 26). Say out loud what they may do, what they may not do, and what makes them stop and find someone. That is the whole form; did anything feel missing?
- Sideways: at Coqodaq's opening, who could say yes to a comp, and who actually did? What did the gap between the two cost the table?
- The white paper says the absence of a clear path is where politics begin (WP p. 11). Where have you watched politics start in a room, and what was undefined that day?
- Who writes a Station Lead's range: the chef partner, the Operations Lead, or the founders?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The three parts: owner, range (including refusals), triggers each with a destination. Adopt, amend, or replace?
- The three operating rules: a trigger names its destination ("escalate" is not one); a range states what the owner may not do; ranges are written one level out. Which of these does he push back on?
- An entry missing a part: marked incomplete, or filled by analogy from a similar position?
- Timing: the leads' ranges written by the founders before the leads' interviews, or by the leads once seated?

**What the book says:** ownership must be defined early or finger-pointing and then politics follow; the book assumes the org chart supplies the structure (pp. 121 to 122).

**White-paper default:** authority sits with the closest competent owner; the range is stated in advance; triggers are named before the event, each with a destination (WP pp. 07 to 08, 10, 13, 26, 31). Any financial value inside a range belongs to Dominic's domain and is not carried in this manual.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Ritz-Carlton's long-standing rule lets any employee spend a pre-authorized amount per customer to resolve a problem without asking; the amount is public inside the company, which is what makes it a range and not a favor.
- Nordstrom's employee handbook was for years a single rule, use good judgment in all situations; the enabling-constraint form the architect's lens describes (cons. 2).
- Apple's "directly responsible individual" and Amazon's "single-threaded owner" are the same one-owner rule the book gives at p. 121.

**Options:** (a) the three-part form and the three rules for every entry, the founders writing the leads' ranges first. Commits every domain owner to writing ranges before positions are filled. (b) ranges for the floor only (generosity, recovery, the door), everything else decided by the leads as it arises. Commits Sŏn to the leads as the integration layer (node overload, WP p. 14). (c) the form, written after the leads are seated so they write their own. Commits Sŏn to hiring leads without being able to tell them their range. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** a range written as a list of prohibitions; "escalate to management" as a destination; a financial figure slipping into the conversation (note it as Dominic's and move on).

**A finished answer:** the form and the rules adopted or amended in writing, the incomplete-entry rule stated, the timing of the leads' ranges stated, and Brandon can write the server's entry from memory in the form.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 2.2.3 Decide how a decision that crosses domains is made, including when the two leads disagree

**The question:** when a decision in one domain changes what another domain must do, who decides, who is consulted, what holds the decision, and where does an unresolved disagreement go, including between the two leads who report to no one?

**Why it matters now:** the house meets this in its first quarter: a course format changes and the floor's choreography breaks; a schedule changes against demand and coverage breaks; a new recovery gesture lands in the kitchen's prep (cons. 2). Without a rule the answer is "ask Brandon," which is the failure the white paper diagnoses (WP p. 13). 2.2.5, 2.2.22, and 2.2.31 all route their escalations here.

**Openers:**
- The kitchen changes a course to a shared plate and the servers' timing no longer works. Play it forward: who finds out, when, and who gets to say no?
- Sideways: the two leads are both right, one about experience and one about cost, and they have been right at each other for a week. What happens on day eight?
- Dominic's team ships a change to the OS surface that changes what a server does mid-service. Who was asked first, and how?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Owning domain decides, affected domain consulted before the event, not informed after. Yes, or a different rule?
- What holds a decision: any objection, or an unresolved one after consultation?
- The destination: the founder whose domain holds the disputed outcome (experience to Brandon's seat, cost to Dominic's), the partners under 2.1.13 when it sits in both. Does he accept that split, and does it match 2.1.13?
- The stack-change case: consulted through the feedback channel the white paper already builds (WP p. 22), or a different path?

**What the book says:** tease ownership apart at the top so it models clear ownership all the way down; a pair of owners is risky (pp. 121 to 122).

**White-paper default:** none stated. The Operations Lead is accountable to cost and compliance and the Maitre d to experience and team health; neither reports to the other (WP p. 10); Brandon owns the operating systems and the experience and Dominic the financial and technology build (WP p. 03). The seam rule follows from those two pages and is the old work's option, not a white-paper statement.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Bain's RAPID and similar decision-rights frameworks separate "decides" from "input" and name both in advance; the point is that consultation is a named step, not a courtesy.
- Toyota's andon cord gives any worker the right to hold the line; the hold is answered within a fixed short time by a named responder. The "hold and escalate to a destination" shape is the same.

**Options:** (a) the seam rule as stated in `tasks.md`, with the stack-change case covered. Commits both founders to being the destination for one class of dispute each, and the partners for the rest. (b) the two leads resolve it between themselves with no destination named. Commits Sŏn to disputes that wait, which is where politics begin (WP p. 11). (c) one lead is first among equals for cross-domain calls. Commits Sŏn to a general manager in all but name, the failure the white paper names at WP p. 10. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** a rule that requires a founder to be reachable during service; a rule that contradicts 2.1.13; "we will work it out" as the answer for the lead-to-lead case.

**A finished answer:** consultation step, hold, and destination stated; which seat receives which kind of lead-to-lead dispute; the stack-change case covered; and nothing in it contradicts 2.1.13.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 2.2.4 Write the decision-rights register, version one (choices only)

### 2.2.5 Decide who holds each lead's check-in and review

**The question:** the two leads report to no one; who holds the Maitre d's check-in and review, who holds the Operations Lead's, and what happens when a lead's plan crosses both founders' domains?

**Why it matters now:** each lead's candidate sheet must name "their direct lead" (WP p. 18) at the first interview. This is the one decision in the chunk a candidate will ask about directly. It also decides who is the second reader when a lead is the subject of a serious concern (5.9), and 2.3.14 places the conversation on the calendar.

**Openers:**
- A strong Maitre d candidate asks at the dinner interview, "Who do I actually answer to?" Answer them now, in one sentence.
- Sideways: name a lead you have watched who had no one above them. What went unsaid for a year because there was no room to say it?
- The Operations Lead's outcomes sit partly in Dominic's domain (cost, the financial architecture) and partly in yours (the operating systems). Which half would you rather hear about monthly, and which would Dominic?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The Maitre d with Brandon's seat: is that as obvious as it looks (WP pp. 03, 10)?
- The Operations Lead: Dominic (each partner holds one lead; the center is read from both sides), Brandon (the operating systems are his), or both?
- The crossing case: both partners once at the point where the plan crosses both domains, or a standing pair?
- The alternatives argued and set aside in the old work (the co-lead, no one, both partners standing, a rotating partner): does any of them deserve a second look?
- The counsel question: is a founder who holds a lead's check-in, or works a service under a designation, acting as an employee for any purpose? Log it; do not answer it here.

**What the book says:** every individual has development goals and every outcome has an owner who tracks it (pp. 113 to 115, 121).

**White-paper default:** none stated. Every new person gets a ninety-day plan with check-ins and a review every three to six months (WP pp. 18, 19); no one is placed above the two leads (WP p. 10).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Companies with co-equal function heads (a CFO and a COO who do not report to each other) routinely have each report to a different founder or to the CEO alone; "both founders standing" is uncommon because it becomes a review by committee.

**Options:** (a) the Maitre d with Brandon's seat, the Operations Lead with Dominic, both partners where the plan crosses both. Commits each partner to one lead's conversation. (b) both leads with Brandon. Commits Brandon's seat to holding all of team development and leaves Dominic without a monthly read on the lead who runs his stack. (c) both leads with both partners. Commits Sŏn to a committee review of each lead. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** an answer that makes one lead the other's manager by the back door; deferring the whole thing until the leads exist, which leaves the candidate sheet blank.

**A finished answer:** each lead's holder named, the crossing case stated, the interval left to 3.3 and the form to 5.4, the counsel question logged, and the candidate sheet line can be written.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 2.2.6 Draw the transparency line

**The question:** in three lists, what is visible to everyone in the building, what is visible to the person and their lead only, and what is held by the founders and whoever they name?

**Why it matters now:** the candidate sheet already promises the org chart, compensation mechanics, and the advancement path to every candidate, "nothing withheld" (WP p. 18); the team home (2.2.15) cannot be built without knowing what goes on it; the communications policy (2.2.12) carries the transparency statement; and 5.5 needs to know whether the line leaves room for the pool's visibility either way (cons. 9).

**Openers:**
- A server on their fourth week opens the team home. What is on the page for their domain, and what did you decide they should not see?
- Sideways: a line cook computes last night's revenue from the pool and says it out loud at family meal. Is that a problem, and whose?
- Review content: does a Station Lead get to read the review of the cook on their station, or only the chef partner?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Goals, metrics, owners, ranges, competency paths, and the register visible to everyone: yes, or with exceptions?
- Lagging figures: none on any surface the house reads, some (covers, waste) because leads accountable to cost should read them with their teams, or all?
- Individual reviews: the person and their domain lead only, or wider?
- Does the chef partner read the operating system before the seat is filled?
- Does the line leave 5.5 room to decide the pool's visibility either way? Say what would break it.

**What the book says:** transparency is not sharing everything; it is everyone knowing what will be shared, when, and with whom, including which HR information goes to whom (p. 131). Internal communications are an equalizing or a discriminating force (p. 127).

**White-paper default:** the candidate-sheet list to every candidate (WP p. 18); every position's competencies visible to anyone (WP p. 11). Silent on operating figures, review content, and the financial model.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Open-book management (Jack Stack's SRC Holdings, "The Great Game of Business") teaches every employee to read the operating numbers, on the argument that people cannot own outcomes they cannot see.
- Buffer publishes every salary internally and externally; Bridgewater records most meetings for anyone inside to watch. Both are the far end of the line and worth naming as the boundary, not a model.
- Stripe made most internal email readable by anyone in the company, which the book's author ran; it is the transparency practice behind p. 131.

**Options:** (a) everything on the team home to everyone; reviews to the person and their lead; the financial model and lagging figures to the founders and whoever they name. Commits Sŏn to publishing ranges, goals, and owners to the whole house and to a stated held list. (b) as (a) plus selected lagging figures on the team home. Commits Sŏn to the 5.5 consequence (a visible pool at a known proportion reveals the day's revenue) or to a held pool. (c) the candidate sheet's items only, the rest to leads. Commits Sŏn to a house divided by role (p. 127). Depth: cons. 9.

**Watch for:** drawing the line by instinct about one figure and then generalizing; deciding 5.5's question here.

**A finished answer:** three lists, the lagging-figures ruling, the chef-partner question answered, and a sentence that says what 5.5 is still free to decide.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 2.2.7 Decide who holds emergency authority during a service and who may close a period or the building

**The question:** on any given service, who acts on a sudden emergency in the room and in the kitchen without asking, and who may close a period, or the building, at each scope?

**Why it matters now:** the shift brief must name emergency authority every service (2.2.31); the alert register routes a weapon-detection alert to whoever holds it (2.2.33); and the readiness test checks that it was named (2.2.38). It is phased hiring and training because the leads must know it at their first training service.

**Openers:**
- The camera layer flags a weapon at the door at eight on a Friday. The Maitre d is on the floor; you are at home. Who moves, and what are they allowed to do before anyone calls you?
- Sideways: a kitchen fire that is out in ninety seconds but the hood is down. Who decides whether the room finishes its tables or goes home, and does the answer change if the chef partner is not in the building?
- Closing the building for a week: whose call, and who has to be told first?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Room: the Maitre d (or the room designation on a service they do not work). Kitchen: the Chef Partner (or the kitchen's designation). Either may close their own side without asking. Adopt, or amend?
- Closing the building for one period: the Operations Lead with the Maitre d consulted, or the reverse, or either?
- Longer than one period: a founder decision under 2.1.13?
- Counsel questions to log: does the lease, the insurance, or the food-safety regime constrain any closure, and who must be notified?

**What the book says:** decide ahead of time who acts on a sudden change and how they report it (pp. 126 to 127).

**White-paper default:** none stated. Brandon's one-seat ruling makes the Maitre d the room's owner; the rest is the old work's option (WP pp. 10, 21).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- The FDA Food Code requires a designated person in charge present during all hours of operation; most state codes adopt it. Whoever holds the room on a service is, in practice, that person, and counsel can confirm what it obliges.
- Incident command systems name a single incident commander per event and hand off explicitly; the "named per service, spoken at the brief" form is the same idea.

**Options:** (a) the split as stated, closure authority widening with scope. Commits the leads to holding the emergency call and the founders to being told rather than asked. (b) all closure authority with a founder. Commits Sŏn to a founder being reachable every service, which the readiness test's absence row is written against. (c) the Operations Lead holds both room and kitchen emergency authority. Commits the person who "reads the systems" (WP p. 10) to being on the floor every service. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** an answer that is right for the night he imagines and wrong for a service neither lead works; skipping the counsel questions.

**A finished answer:** emergency authority for room and kitchen named per service, closure authority at each scope with its consultation, the counsel questions logged, and the entries ready for the register.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner rewrites the kitchen's half once seated.

### 2.2.9 Write the strategy's trade-offs and the what-must-be-true list (choices only)

**The question:** which trade-offs are on the list, which must-be-true items, and what reads each?

**What Brandon supplies in the session:** which of the white paper's trade-offs he accepts as decided (dinner alone for a quarter; lunch may come out; compensation in the price; no marketing spend; no espresso machine on the front bar; an overnight crew instead of server close-clean; comps fully used; a paid practical; company-paid uniforms and lockers; no future brand named, cons. 3), any he thinks are not actually decided, and the cost of each in a sentence; which must-be-true claims go on the list with the metric or test that reads each (cons. 3 lists nine candidates); and that the financial model's existence and owner are pointed to without content.

**Openers:**

- Of the nine must-be-true candidates, which one would you least like to see measured?

**A finished answer:** both lists chosen with a source and a cost or a test per line, no figure, no future brand name, and a reversal recognizable as a decision under 2.1.13 or 2.2.3.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 2.2.10 Decide whether the horizon beyond the building appears in the internal operating system

**The question:** does the team home say anything about what the building is proving beyond itself, and if so in what terms, and who may say it aloud?

**Why it matters now:** the team home is read by every hire (2.2.15); the long-term goals (2.1.3) either carry the horizon or do not; the mission sentence (2.1.2) may have to carry it alone if nothing else does (cons. 3).

**Openers:**
- A new server asks at orientation why the morning customer's record has to travel to dinner. What is the true answer, and can you give it without naming a future concept?
- Sideways: which is worse, a team that thinks it works at a restaurant, or a team that has heard about a membership tier it will never see?
- The white paper says the future concepts "never appear externally" (WP p. 38). The team is not external. Does that settle it, or open it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- On the team home: not at all, by category only, or as a sentence inside the long-term goals?
- Who may say it aloud: the founders only, the leads, anyone?
- Does the answer change once the early morning opens and the layer is visibly doing its work?

**What the book says:** three horizons; plan differently for emerging and mature parts (pp. 90 to 92).

**White-paper default:** future concepts held by category, never external; the parent stays in the background (WP pp. 36, 38). Silent on internal appearance.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
