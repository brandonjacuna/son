# 2.2 The operating system: considerations

What is worth knowing before doing the work in `tasks.md`. Topics follow the book's order, with one addition up front (decision rights) that the book assumes and never states. "WP p. N" is the white paper (`sources/extraction/s01/record.md`). Nothing here is a decision; where the old synthesis argued a position it appears as an option with its reasoning and what it commits Sŏn to.

One vocabulary line, because the white paper uses the same words for two things. In this chunk "the operating system" means the human system the book describes: strategy, goals, metrics, ownership, accountability, and communication. The software is "the stack" (WP p. 21) or "the OS surface" (WP p. 22). The stack is what the human system's signals travel on; it is not the system.

## 1. What an operating system is at Sŏn, and when it is written

### What the white paper holds

The white paper describes an organization that holds its knowledge in the system rather than in people (WP pp. 07 to 08), and it names most of the mechanisms an operating system would run on: the shift brief and the shift close (WP pp. 13 to 14), the structured feedback channel that "syncs to the execution system" (WP p. 22), the development review every three to six months with the no-surprises rule (WP p. 19), the competency conversation and the ready-now pool (WP pp. 11, 14), the ninety-day plan with check-ins (WP p. 18), peer recognition (WP p. 19), the private, process-focused post-mortem (WP p. 17), the exit interview read for what could have been better (WP p. 23), and the content engine's daily, weekly, and monthly rhythm (WP p. 31). It names three leading indicators (WP p. 22) and a set of cultural measures (WP p. 23). It never calls any of this an operating system, never says who reviews what on which rhythm, and never states how a decision moves when it crosses a domain.

It also argues, in its first pages, against the book's frame. The book's operating system is a house's mechanical system, replicating from company to division to team to individual (book pp. 84 to 85). The white paper: "The restaurant industry builds teams like buildings. Weight flows downward" (WP p. 07); "No top. No bottom. The web has a center and an outside edge" (WP p. 09); below the leads, "strands, not levels" (WP p. 10); and "the organization is not built like a building. It is built like a body and engineered like a machine" (WP p. 14).

On timing the white paper is silent, but it commits to outputs that only an operating system can supply, at the first hire: every candidate receives at the first interview "the org chart, their direct lead, compensation and benefits, communication expectations, the training plan with pay dates, the compensation mechanics, and the advancement path" (WP p. 18); onboarding delivers the mission, values, and behavioral standard before operational training (WP p. 18); and "every position has documented competency requirements visible to anyone" (WP p. 11).

### Considerations

**Her trigger does not fire; a different one does.** The book says write it down once the company no longer fits in one room (p. 86). Sŏn's team never fits in one room, by hour rather than by headcount: the people who run the early morning are not the people who run late night (WP pp. 33, 35), and the white paper's name for what happens when information depends on people being in the same place is bus failure (WP p. 13). Three further reasons the old synthesis kept: the candidate sheet promises operating-system artifacts before the first hire (WP p. 18); the opening is phased, so each added period is run by people who did not run dinner and must inherit a system rather than reconstruct one (WP p. 36); and the chef partner arrives into an operating company (WP p. 29). The book's "too early" caution has a real Sŏn form, but it attaches to service periods, not to the company: the restaurant is not a product-market-fit experiment ("late to the cuisine and late to the category, deliberately," WP p. 35); the layer is (WP p. 35). No operating logic is written for a period before its charter exists (2.1.14, 2.1.15), and none for lunch before the three-partner decision (WP p. 36).

**The book's cascade against the white paper's web.** The old synthesis went through her apparatus mechanism by mechanism and sorted it three ways: survives intact, survives with its justification replaced, does not survive. The pattern that came out is worth holding because it decides most of this chunk. Her mechanisms that assume direction (goals replicate down, a manager assigns ownership, a leadership team allocates between claimants, communications push context down) do not fit an organization where "authority lives in the domain" (WP p. 07) and the edge "feel[s] the customer, the room, and the cultural temperature of the building before anyone else" (WP p. 09). Her mechanisms that are indifferent to direction (the tests for a good goal, shared definitions, the named-owner rule, the frequency rule, the four tests of communication, the named actor for an alert, the annual reset) fit, and are often stricter at Sŏn because the white paper treats undefined ownership and undocumented paths as structural failures (WP pp. 11, 14) rather than management lapses.

**The units.** The book's units nest: company, division, team, individual. Sŏn has no division and no team in her sense. It has the company; the domain, where ownership is held (WP pp. 07 to 10); the service period, where "reason to exist" and "operating logic" are answered (WP pp. 33 to 34) and which 2.1.14 makes the charter unit; and the person, where development is held (WP p. 11). The same kitchen serves every period and the same Maitre d holds the floor across them, so the period and the domain intersect rather than nest. A goal set at the center and replicated to the edge is set by the node with the least information about the thing the goal is about (WP p. 09); laddering up from the unit that holds the information to a long-term goal (2.1.3) keeps her throughline and reverses her direction.

**Two caveats the old synthesis stated, kept.** Sŏn is not self-managed. The founders own their domains outright and set the company's goals; the leads own theirs. What is absent is altitude, not authority. And the web is a claim about how Sŏn is built; several of its mechanisms are not yet running because the building has not opened. The architect's rule applies: a diagram is not a system. Section 10 is how the claim gets tested.

**A lead who produces stability by hand is the diagnosis.** The book's manager unblocks, course-corrects, tracks, and escalates. The white paper's failure-mode reference calls a manager absorbing adjacent-domain load "node overload" and says the fix is restoring the separation, not praising the lead (WP p. 14). Every mechanism in this chunk is worth testing against one question: does it run without a lead's memory?

### Options on the frame

- **Option A: build on the web.** Four intersecting units; goals ladder up; ownership in the domain; escalation on pre-named triggers; communication lateral and system-carried. Commits Sŏn to writing the decision-rights structure the book leaves to the org chart (section 2), and to testing the claim (section 10). The old synthesis argued this.
- **Option B: adopt the book's cascade with Sŏn's vocabulary.** Company, the two leads as divisions, stations and sections as teams. Commits Sŏn to a manager above each person, which the white paper's Part I is written against (WP pp. 07 to 11), and to keeping the leads' parallel structure only on paper.
- **Option C: a hybrid, the web below the leads and the book's cascade above.** Founders set goals and allocate; leads and below hold domains. Commits Sŏn to the founders as the integration layer, which is the memory failure the white paper diagnoses (WP p. 13) and the opposite of a company "built to run without them" (WP p. 06).

## 2. Decision rights: what the book assumes and never states

### What the white paper holds

The book's ownership and accountability material assumes a decision-rights structure and never states one, because in a hierarchy the structure is the chart. Sŏn has no chart of that kind, so the rule has to be written. The white paper assigns decision rights several times and never states the rule it applies:

- Lunch: "an equal decision among all three partners, the chef partner included" (WP p. 36). Decided in 2.1.13.
- The server: "owns the table relationship and resolves it using their authorized judgment and generosity range" (WP p. 13); "every server carries a bottle to gift as they see fit," managers "carry a nice spirit to pour tableside," and comps are "a line item to be fully used, not minimized" (WP p. 26); recovery is "whatever it takes, and do not negotiate, lead with more than expected" (WP p. 26).
- The two leads: the Operations Lead is accountable to "labor efficiency, cost targets, compliance, vendor performance, and facility integrity"; the Maitre d to "experience quality and team health"; "neither reports to the other" (WP p. 10).
- Per-service designations: "the Lead Host owns the door," "the Chef Partner owns culinary execution during service," "the Head of Beverage owns beverage program execution for this shift," and these "mark accountability in the moment, in the domain. They are not permanent elevations above anyone" (WP p. 10).
- Content: most pieces need no approval; a smaller share goes to a founder consultation; a small share (crisis response, strategic announcements, sensitive topics) goes to "leadership review" (WP p. 31). "Leadership" is not named.
- Advancement: "no one's advancement waits on a manager's attention or judgment. The system is the advocate" (WP p. 11); the domain lead signs off at the competency conversation (WP p. 19).
- The stack: Dominic owns "the technology build" (WP p. 03), and "employees are the users" of the systems (WP p. 17).

The white paper is silent on: what happens when a decision in one domain changes what another domain must do; what happens when the two parallel leads disagree; who holds emergency authority during a service and who may close the room; who holds a lead's check-in and review when no one is above the leads; and who sets the flags and reads the book before a service. Its one statement about escalation is that the absence of a clear path "is where politics begin" (WP p. 11).

### Considerations

**The shape all the instances share.** Authority sits with the closest competent owner; the range inside which that owner acts without asking is stated in advance; the triggers that escalate are named before the event, each with a destination. Session 2 found the shape and 2.1's considerations note it (section 6); this chunk is where it becomes the general rule beneath the partner level that 2.1.13 sets. Three operating rules follow from the form, kept from the old work: a trigger names its destination ("escalate" is not a destination); a range states what the owner may not do as well as what they may; ranges are set one level out from the position that holds them (a domain owner sets ranges inside the domain; the founders set the leads'; the partners set each other's, which is 2.1.13). Any value inside a range that is a financial figure (a recovery threshold, the bottle's worth) is set in Dominic's domain, and financials are not a source for this work.

**Enabling, not governing.** The architect's distinction: a governing constraint treats performance as compliance; an enabling constraint gives coherence and lets people use judgment inside it, and holds when no lead is present. A range written as an enabling constraint is what the white paper's generosity range already is (WP p. 26). A range written as a list of prohibitions is a traffic light where a roundabout would work.

**The realist's caution.** A range is only real if the person holding it has the slack to use it on a full night. A pre-authorized gesture still costs a person and a moment; if the labor model has no one who can leave a station to run a recovery, the range is a promise the floor cannot keep. The labor model is 4.1's; the range is written here, and it is worth stating in each entry what the range assumes about who is free.

**Cross-domain decisions (the book's Class B).** The house will meet these in its first quarter: the kitchen changes a course format and the floor's choreography no longer fits; the Operations Lead changes the schedule against demand and the Maitre d's coverage pattern breaks; the Maitre d authorizes a new recovery gesture and the kitchen's prep absorbs it. The old synthesis's rule, now an option: the owning domain decides; any domain whose outcomes are affected is consulted before the event, not informed after; an unresolved objection holds the decision and escalates to the founder whose domain holds the disputed outcome; if the outcome sits in both founders' domains, it is a partner decision under 2.1.13. The Operations Lead's outcomes sit partly in Dominic's domain (cost, the financial architecture) and partly in Brandon's (the operating systems); the Maitre d's sit in Brandon's (WP pp. 03, 10). So a disagreement about experience goes to Brandon's seat, about cost to Dominic's, about both to the partners. A change to the stack that alters what a person does during service is the same class: Dominic's domain decides, the affected domain is consulted first, through the feedback channel the white paper already builds (WP p. 22), because "employees are the users" (WP p. 17).

**The two leads have no one above them.** The white paper gives every new person a ninety-day plan with regular check-ins (WP p. 18) and a development review every three to six months (WP p. 19), and says feedback runs both directions (WP p. 19). For the two leads, who report to no one, it names no holder. Later sessions argued that the holder follows the same seam rule: the Maitre d's check-in and review with Brandon's seat, the Operations Lead's with the partner whose domain holds the outcome, and both partners where the plan crosses both. Alternatives argued and set aside in the old work: the co-lead (which makes one lead the other's manager), no one (which leaves the one topic with no destination, a concern about a lead), both partners standing (which is a review by committee), a rotating partner. This is worth deciding before the leads are hired, because the candidate sheet names "their direct lead" (WP p. 18) and a lead's sheet has to say whom they answer to. A counsel question sits beside it, kept from the old work: whether a founder who holds a lead's check-in, or works a service under a designation, is acting as an employee for any purpose that matters.

**Emergency authority and closure.** Silent in the white paper. Brandon ruled in the extraction program (2026-09-13) that the floor manager and the Maitre d are one seat, the Maitre d holding the room; the old synthesis then read emergency authority as the Maitre d for the room and the Chef Partner for the kitchen during service, either able to close their side without asking; closing the building for a service period as the Operations Lead's call with the Maitre d consulted; closing for longer as a founder decision. The ruling stands; the authority assignments are still Brandon's to decide, and the kitchen's half is the chef partner's once seated. Counsel questions: whether any closure authority is constrained by the lease, insurance, or the food-safety regime, and who must be notified.

**What the register does not cover, and should say so.** The old synthesis's rule that an entry missing a part is marked incomplete rather than filled by analogy is kept. Version one will have incomplete entries: the content framework's "leadership review" owner; the recovery threshold's value (Dominic's); the kitchen's entries (the chef partner's). A register that shows its gaps is the white paper's own practice: "a settled-looking answer to an open question is the tell" (WP p. 22).

### Options on the rule

- **Option A: the three-part form for every entry, with the three operating rules.** Commits every domain owner to writing ranges for the positions inside their domain before those positions are filled, and commits the founders to writing the leads' ranges before the leads' interviews.
- **Option B: ranges for the floor only (the generosity range, recovery, the door), with everything else decided by the leads as it arises.** Commits Sŏn to the leads as the integration layer, which is node overload by the white paper's own definition (WP p. 14).
- **Option C: the form, but written after the leads are seated so they write their own.** Commits Sŏn to hiring leads without being able to tell them their range, against WP p. 18's "nothing withheld."

## 3. Strategic and financial planning

### What the white paper holds

The strategy is in the white paper and it already meets her test that "a strategy should hurt" (book p. 88). The trade-offs, each with its cost visible: dinner runs alone for at least a quarter (WP p. 36); lunch may never be built and comes out of the plan if there is no good answer (WP p. 36); compensation is built into the menu price, never a tip or a service charge, which raises the printed price against every peer that hides the same cost on the check (WP p. 20); no marketing spend; attention is earned by documenting the build, setbacks included (WP pp. 28 to 29); no espresso machine on the front bar, so the room reads as a dining room at dinner (WP pp. 04 to 05); servers do not close-clean; an overnight crew does, so server labor goes to the thank-you note (WP p. 26); comps are "a line item to be fully used, not minimized" (WP p. 26); a paid practical replaces the unpaid stage (WP p. 18); uniforms, shoes, and lockers are paid for by the company (WP pp. 19 to 20); no future brand is named (WP p. 36).

The three horizons are there without the name. Horizon one is dinner, "the heart, the fine dining room everything else protects" (WP p. 35). Horizon two is the early morning and late night, in that likely order, "once dinner is steady" (WP p. 36). Horizon three is two things held apart: lunch and brunch "held in reserve, not part of the core and not assumed" (WP pp. 35 to 36), and the platform ceiling, concepts by category and a membership tier, "gated on the proof, never a promise made before the building earns it" (WP p. 38).

Her first artifact exists and is not this program's: "Where the model's numbers belong they live in the financial model" (WP p. 02), and Dominic "owns the financial model and the capital architecture" (WP p. 05). Her second artifact, the shorter-term plan with a year-end P&L, has an owner (Dominic) and no content here. Financials are not a source for this work.

### Considerations

**The trade-offs are decided and scattered.** Each lives in a different part of the white paper, which is what makes any one of them re-decidable at a different hour by a person who arrived later. Held in one list beside the long-term goals (2.1.3), a proposal to reverse one (add a service charge, put a machine on the bar, buy a placement) is recognizable as a strategy change under 2.1.13 rather than an operating adjustment. The list carries no figure.

**The horizons are sequenced by proof, not run in parallel.** The book says start on horizons two and three early (p. 91). The white paper says the opposite: horizon two opens "once dinner is steady," and the lunch decision is not opened for a full year (WP p. 36). What connects the horizons is the layer, and the layer is tested from day one inside horizon one: "can the layer take a customer who entered in the morning and bring them to late night" (WP p. 35). The early morning is both horizon two's first period and horizon three's first experiment, which is why it opens before late night in the white paper's likely sequence.

**What must be true, drawn from the white paper's claims.** Her list of what must be true for the numbers is the strategy half of her first artifact and it is in scope. The candidates, each with the metric or test that would read it: dinner is steady within its first quarter (undefined in the white paper; section 10); the layer moves a customer across the day (WP p. 35; a charter metric candidate, section 6); the company runs without either founder's memory (WP p. 06; the absence rule, section 10); the leadership structure holds at roughly eight lines across the day (WP p. 22; the requisite-variety test, section 4); compensation built into price is accepted by customers (WP p. 20; the customer's word, read in the qualitative side of section 6); the team stays (WP p. 23; retention and internal promotion); the morning enrolls the customer on the first visit and the record travels to dinner (WP p. 36); sound is solved at the source (WP p. 21); the documented build produces a cohort whose first visit begins where a fifth visit would (WP p. 30).

**"Steady" is undefined.** The gate that opens the second period is the most consequential undefined word in the white paper. Section 10.

**The horizon beyond the building.** The white paper says the future concepts "never appear externally" (WP p. 38) and that the parent "stays in the background" (WP p. 36). It does not say whether they appear internally. The team home (section 9) is read by every hire. Whether the horizon is on it at all, even by category, is a founders' call: on it, and every hire knows what the building is proving; off it, and the mission sentence (2.1.2) has to carry that alone.

**Where Sŏn diverges from the book.** The book's near-term plan runs on calendar periods and a year-end P&L. Sŏn's near-term plan runs on gates: pre-opening, dinner alone, dinner steady and the early morning added, late night added, the first full year, the lunch ruling. Each gate opens on a test, not a date. A calendar quarter is the rhythm on which the gates are reviewed (2.3), not the thing that opens them. The book's maturity split still applies, inverted: the dining room executes a proven category and its goals are execution states with baselines to establish; the layer is the experiment and its goals are her emerging-business form, run the test and read the result.

### Options on the near-term plan

- **Option A: proof gates with a readiness test each, reviewed quarterly.** Commits the founders to holding a period closed until its test passes, including when a lease, a stream announcement, or cash argues for a date.
- **Option B: dates, with the tests as a checklist.** Commits Sŏn to opening on the calendar and to the white paper's "worse version of dinner" risk (WP pp. 33 to 34) if the test was not met.
- **Option C: gates for the core periods, dates for lunch and beyond.** Commits the three partners to a calendar for the one decision the white paper says may remove the period entirely.

## 4. Resource allocation

### What the white paper holds

Her two resources are people and money (book p. 93). At Sŏn, people are near-fixed by design: the leadership structure is set at roughly eight lines across the day (WP p. 22), and the argument is that software does the integration work that a peer's fourteen positions did by hand. Money is Dominic's domain and not a source here. The white paper names what is actually scarce: "Bandwidth is the organizational metabolic rate. It is what the system has available after the work of survival is complete" (WP p. 12), and it ranks it first: "Bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results" (WP p. 12), "mechanical, not aspirational" (WP p. 17). Beside it: the room's character, since "experiencing the same dining room at every hour would cost dinner its magic" and "dinner is what everything protects" (WP p. 33); the day's hours, one room and one kitchen transforming between periods (WP p. 33); and founder attention, committed at once to the floor, the build, and a content engine producing many pieces a day "without burning the founders out" (WP p. 30).

The white paper names the risks the book says leadership must own because no one else will (book p. 101): the chef dependency ("a restaurant built around a specific chef is a dependency, not a business," WP p. 37); undercapitalization and weak controls ("the controllable set," WP p. 05); food safety, which the defensive stack exists to prevent (WP p. 21); key-person loss, answered structurally (WP p. 14); customer acceptance of compensation in the price (WP p. 20); sound as an Austin failing (WP p. 21); and the stack itself ("Software breaks: true," WP p. 22). It does not list them as a register or give them owners.

### Considerations

**Allocation is an ordering, not a ratio.** The book's tools are a fixed split across horizons and Stripe's ordered list with a reserve (pp. 100 to 101). The white paper's ordering already exists: dinner's protection first; then the protection order, bandwidth before results; then the phased sequence of periods. Whether "bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results" is a rule that decides a trade-off or a description of how health produces results is 2.1.4, and this component depends on it. If it is a rule, a period that makes money and consumes the team is restructured before it is defended on results, and covers come down before the floor is thinned. No ratio is carried.

**The leadership delta is a claim, and her own critique applies.** "Roughly eight leadership lines running the full span of the day against fourteen running one dinner service" (WP p. 22) is a benchmark comparison, and the book's own warning is that "other companies' benchmarks don't reflect your own company and business model" (p. 93). The architect's test is requisite variety: a regulator must hold at least the variety of what it regulates, and the white paper's argument for why eight can hold what fourteen held is attenuation, "manual integration work that software now does" (WP p. 22). That holds only while the stack is actually absorbing it, which is why "sequence corruption" (WP p. 14) is the failure that would expose the structure. The realist's test is the worst realistic night: staffing to the theoretical minimum looks identical to discipline on a calm night and is deferred collapse on a Friday. The old synthesis named eight disturbance classes the house must absorb, kept here without counts: customer-side (occasions read without being announced, a known customer whose record did not travel, allergies, recognition failures, recovery); kitchen-side (a station failure across sections, live-fire variability, a delivery that does not arrive, prep short at a period boundary because one kitchen serves every period); floor-side (no-shows and walk-ins, the last turn at a period boundary, the room's transition between periods); people-side (call-outs, a node leaving mid-quarter, an empty ready-now pool); system-side (a stack outage given the point-of-sale spine is open, WP p. 22; a sensor failure; preferences not surfacing at the brief); transitions (one room reconfiguring, with an owner per transition); external (weather on the patio, the sound ordinance, regulatory timelines); brand-side (a public crisis, a setback told honestly at content speed, a review that names the compensation model). The test: for each class, name the regulator (the stack, a pre-stated range, a lead, a founder) and check that no class lands on a lead's memory or a founder by default. Where a class resolves to a founder, the structure is under-built for that class and the fix is a range or a system function. Where it resolves to nothing, there is a hole. It runs against the actual leadership-line list (4.1), before opening and again against the shift-close log after the first quarter, and again at each period's gate.

**The work no one prioritizes is system maintenance.** The white paper says so four times: "the fix is system maintenance, not people management"; "the fix is documentation and system access"; "the fix is removing friction from the work" (WP p. 14). None of it produces a cover, all of it keeps the eight-line structure sound, and it has an owner: the Operations Lead "oversees those systems and is accountable to their outputs" (WP p. 10), with Dominic's seat owning the build. The old synthesis's device, kept as an option: every company and period goal set reserves one line for system maintenance and one for a named existential risk, so the work has a named owner before the quarter begins. A line, not a share.

**Interface complexity counts in periods, not people.** The book's Figure 5 has interfaces rising with headcount (p. 94). At Sŏn they rise with service periods and the hours at which handoffs occur: the same kitchen, the same room, the customer record traveling from the morning to the dinner book. The charter's "what it hands on" and "what it draws from" sections (2.1.15) are the interface specification, and each added period adds interfaces to every period already open. That is the structural reason the white paper adds periods one at a time (WP p. 36).

**What does not survive.** Celebrating managers who give headcount back (book p. 99): there is none to give back, and the white paper's recognition runs peer to peer for excellence in the moment (WP p. 19). The Sŏn form of an efficiency gain is friction removed, read as bandwidth through the leading indicators. Her three phases of scale (pp. 94 to 95): Sŏn is built in her third phase from the first day, domains with documented interfaces on a shared-services stack, and that is a claim to test, not a phase to reach.

## 5. Annual and quarterly goals

### What the white paper holds

No goals in her sense, and no goal-setting process. It holds the grammar goals would be written in: states the house is in ("no one touches a table until they are actually ready," WP p. 19; "a formal review never contains new information," WP p. 19), the test for the layer written as a state (WP p. 35), and a rule that forbids the book's advice to guess a target: "the discipline is to never assert the unproven, to a customer or to a reader" (WP p. 36). For development it is specific: "after a set period in any role, the next skill set unlocks automatically. It is self-paced" (WP p. 11); cross-strand fluency is "the real development marker" (WP p. 11); the domain lead signs off at the competency conversation (WP p. 19). On the cost of a goal met by exhausting the team it is direct: the organization "runs people at full capacity until they stop, and calls it dedication until it becomes turnover" (WP p. 13), and "when something goes wrong, the first question is not who failed. It is what allowed this to happen" (WP p. 17).

### Considerations

**Her good-goals guidance survives nearly whole.** States, not activities; three to five per unit on one page; objectively assessable, not necessarily quantitative; challenging but possible; never organized by function; set as close to the customer as possible, which at Sŏn means set at the edge; her heuristics for testing (a verb means an action, reframe it; if you do not wince at what was left out you have not focused; if two people could disagree on whether it was achieved it is not specific). Grove's test stands: did I do that or did I not. Her OKR structure, an objective as a state and key results that are binary or metric, fits; neither acronym needs adopting as a name. One thing changes: the unit. A service period's goals are one set across both strands and the kitchen, which is her "never organize team goals by function" (p. 106) with the unit translated, and they are set by the Maitre d, the Operations Lead, and the kitchen's owner of execution together, because the period is where their domains intersect.

**Committed and aspirational, declared up front.** Her conditions for a committed goal (p. 112) each have a Sŏn form: an existential threat is the risk register (section 4); a blocked team is a period whose opening waits on another being steady; and "a customer has been told" fires unusually often at Sŏn, because the documented build tells the pre-opening community what is coming in real time (WP pp. 29 to 30). An opening date said on a stream is a commitment to a customer in her exact sense. "Never assert the unproven" (WP p. 36) is what keeps the committed class small: an announcement is made once the gate behind it has passed, not before.

**Targets before a baseline.** The book says guess and update (p. 110). The white paper forbids the guess. Pre-opening, Sŏn has no operating history and cannot set a target on any rate. The old synthesis's resolution, kept as an option: pre-opening goals are the readiness test's rows (section 10), states checkable yes or no; first-quarter goals are binary states and baselining goals of the form "a baseline exists for X, computed by the stack, reviewed at the leads' review"; targets on rates appear from the second quarter, stated as relative movement from the baseline, which the workbook asks for anyway (p. 152). The alternative is the book's: name a number, call it a guess, and move it, which is honest inside the building and contradicts the white paper's discipline if it ever reaches a candidate or a customer.

**Development goals are system-surfaced, not manager-set.** The book's one or two personal development goals per quarter survive; the mechanism improves. The person's development goals are the skill sets the system has unlocked and the person has chosen, deeper in the domain or across into the adjacent strand, with cross-strand fluency as a named class because the white paper pays for it (WP p. 11). The lead's part is the competency conversation and the sign-off, not the assignment. A development goal that waits on a lead's attention to exist is skill invisibility (WP p. 14). The ninety-day plan (3.3) is where a new person's first goals live; the learning platform (WP pp. 18 to 19) is what the goals draw from and belongs to the learning and development work.

**The "how," strengthened into a rule.** "Performance = results × behaviors" (book p. 115) is a review note in the book. ## 6. Metrics that matter

### What the white paper holds

The constraint is absolute on the primary read: "Revenue, labor percentage, food cost: these exist because they have always existed. All lagging indicators. By the time they signal a problem, the damage is done. Sŏn measures what predicts problems before they compound" (WP p. 22). Three leading indicators, each given one line: "Employee NPS: would you recommend working here, tracked regularly rather than annually. Cultural labor score: beyond hours and cost, how the team is actually doing. Customer recognition rate: whether the building is actually recognizing and remembering customers" (WP p. 22). The cultural measures, "tracked with the same seriousness as cost": retention rate, internal promotion rate, engagement across psychological safety and values alignment, participation in development (WP p. 23). The qualitative side "most operators never gather": stories of how the culture shows up in daily problem-solving, exit interviews read for what could have been better, customer feedback about the team's experience, how people grow beyond the building (WP p. 23). Lagging indicators are "the X-ray after the fracture" (WP p. 12): read, not primary.

The test the building exists to run is a metric in waiting: "can the layer take a customer who entered in the morning and bring them to late night" (WP p. 35). The data the moat rests on is named: the demographic read at the door, plate-level and scrap-level waste, "one customer profile resolved across every channel and every daypart" (WP pp. 21, 27). Feedback runs through "a dedicated channel that syncs to the execution system" and "the loop gets closed" (WP p. 22). The word for the person at the table is "customer," deliberately (WP p. 25).

The white paper is silent on definitions, populations, rhythms, targets, counterweights, and owners. It names the data hub as a specific tool (WP p. 21) that this project's instructions say is no longer in use.

### Considerations

**Her lagging premise is inverted at Sŏn.** The book's long-term metrics tend to be lagging (p. 115). The white paper's long-horizon measures of the three engines are all leading in her sense: inputs that predict the lagging outcomes. So Sŏn's two layers differ by horizon, not by lag: charter metrics are the nervous system read as a trend over years; operating metrics are the same nervous system read at service tempo. A proposal to make a lagging figure a charter metric has to argue against WP p. 22 in writing.

**Candidate charter metrics, three to five, each with a parent in 2.1.3.** Employee NPS as a trend (parent: the organization that compounds, "labor as asset," WP p. 16). Customer recognition rate (parent: belonging), defined as the share of visits by a known customer at which held preferences were acted on before being asked. Cross-period movement (parent: the platform), defined as the share of known customers with visits in more than one service period, which is WP p. 35's test written as a metric. Retention and internal promotion (parent: the organization; "direct evidence the development commitments are real," WP p. 23). The cultural labor score (parent: the protection order), which has one line and no definition. The kitchen's contribution is the chef partner's.

**Candidate operating metrics, at service tempo.** Shift-close capture completeness: every incident, every recovery, every generosity use captured; its failure is sequence corruption (WP p. 14). The record travels: the share of known-customer reservations at which history and preferences were present at the brief before seating. Recognition delivered: given that the record traveled, the share at which the floor acted on it. Feedback loop closure: the share of items filed in the channel that reached a visible status inside the window, and the age of the oldest open item (WP p. 22). Generosity range utilization: the share of the pre-authorized range used per service, where under-use is the failure (WP p. 26). Employee NPS at its stated rhythm. Kitchen: waste at plate and scrap level (WP p. 21) and food-safety logging, the chef partner's to shape.

**Counterweights.** The workbook asks for them (p. 152). Recognition rate is counterweighted by recognition that becomes unsolicited instruction, which the white paper's belonging argument warns against (being seen, not shown off to, WP p. 25); cross-period movement by dinner's protection, so a morning customer moved to dinner in a way that costs dinner its register counts against (WP p. 33); generosity utilization by recovery frequency, so a range spent on recovery rather than belonging is a signal; employee NPS by bandwidth, so a high score at zero bandwidth is read as cooling failure in progress (WP p. 14).

**Zero targets.** Her instrument (p. 116) with white-paper candidates: food-safety critical events, read from the sensor logging (WP p. 21); a known customer treated as a first-time customer; a candidate ghosted ("no one is ghosted," WP p. 18); a feedback item open past the window with no status; and the word the white paper rejects for the person at the table appearing in any internal copy (WP p. 25).

**Shared definitions are the hardest part, and sharper here.** The white paper uses one word for things that must be counted differently. Customer: for counting, a person with a record in the identity spine (WP p. 21). Known customer: a customer whose record is "resolved across every channel and every daypart" (WP p. 27), which depends on the identity resolution actually running. Recognition: a held preference acted on without being asked, before or at the moment it matters; not a greeting by name alone. Return: a subsequent visit by a known customer in any period; the fifth visit is the white paper's arc marker (WP p. 25). Service period: a period with a charter (2.1.14). Incident: any event resolved inside or outside a range and captured at the close (WP p. 13). Closed loop: a feedback item with a visible status and, where acted on, a named change; the window is 2.3's. Employee NPS: the question is given (WP p. 22); the population and rhythm are not. Later sessions added vocabulary lines worth keeping: "feedback" names three different acts in the white paper (about tools, WP p. 17; upward and on the channel, WP pp. 19, 22; to a person about their work, WP p. 18) and the register should say which is meant; "disengaged" (WP p. 14) is the one word the white paper attaches to a person and is kept off any lead's mouth. The compensation vocabulary (a day, the pool, points) is 5.5's, and the register holds it once set.

**The cultural labor score is Brandon's to define.** He originated the concept; the white paper gives it one line. The team can say what it must do: read bandwidth rather than output, be captured at service tempo rather than annually, and not reduce to hours. It cannot say what it is: the question or observation it is built from, the population, and the rhythm. Later sessions noted that until it is defined, the read on the team's bandwidth has one source (the NPS question) and one "cannot read yet," and that whatever the score reads about the team it does not read the founders.

**The data hub.** The white paper names a specific tool as "the hub for everything that is not CRM" (WP p. 21). The project's instructions exclude that tool as no longer in use. The metric calculations, the shift close, the feedback channel's sync, and the alert register all run on whatever holds the hub role. Either the white paper's stack description is stale at its center or the instruction is wrong about the business; the stack is Dominic's domain outright (WP p. 03), and confirming it is his. Until then, no operating document names a hub tool; every reference is to the function.

**The realist's caution on capture.** Every operating metric above is captured by someone at the end of a service. The shift close survives only if it takes minutes, is structured and fielded, and never asks a server to explain the same incident twice. A metric that requires a cook to watch a screen mid-service adds friction rather than removing it, and fails the floor.

### Options on the metric layer

- **Option A: two horizons of the same leading indicators, each with one owner, a parent, a counterweight, and a definitions entry, lagging figures owned in Dominic's and the Operations Lead's domains and never first in any mechanism.** Commits Sŏn to building the definitions register before any metric is published and to defending the primary read against every operator instinct to open on revenue.
- **Option B: the book's split, lagging charter metrics (revenue, labor percentage, food cost) with leading operating metrics beneath them.** Commits Sŏn to contradicting WP p. 22 in its own measurement and to a house review that opens on the X-ray.
- **Option C: leading indicators only, with lagging figures never shown to the operating system at all.** Commits Sŏn to leads accountable to "cost targets" (WP p. 10) who cannot see cost.

## 7. Ownership

### What the white paper holds

This is the component where the white paper needs no translating. "Authority lives in the domain. The person who owns the domain is accountable to its outcomes" (WP pp. 07 to 08). Domain ownership is "small, specific, explicitly defined, interlocking" (WP p. 09). Politics are structural: "the absence of a clear path is where politics begin... In a code-bearing structure there is no weight to position yourself under" (WP p. 11). Ownership runs on two clocks: standing ownership by domain (the leads, the founders, the Station Leads, the Head of Beverage, the Lead Host, each server for their tables and their book of regulars, WP pp. 10, 25) and per-service ownership by functional designation, "who owns what on a given night" (WP p. 10).

### Considerations

**One owner, never two.** The book allows a pair of owners as a risky last resort (p. 122). The white paper's rule is stricter by construction, and the book's better answer is the one it actually used: split the metric at the seam ("what's on the truck" revenue against new-product revenue, p. 121). At Sŏn the seam is between the two parallel leads. The Operations Lead owns whatever the stack does and is "accountable to their outputs, not their manual production" (WP p. 10); the Maitre d owns what people do with what the stack surfaces. So recognition rate splits into "the record travels" (Operations Lead) and "recognition delivered" (Maitre d). Cross-period movement splits into "the profile is one across periods" (Dominic's data architecture, executed through the Operations Lead) and "the customer was invited across" (the Maitre d and the servers' books). Retention splits into the invisible advocate's mechanics (Operations Lead) and the team's health (Maitre d). Where a metric cannot be split, its owner is the period charter's "held by" entry and its escalation is the cross-domain rule (section 2). The operating system as a whole has one owner, Brandon's seat (WP p. 03); the stack has one, Dominic's (WP p. 03); the OS surface's seam is 2.1.7.

**Per-service ownership is the shift brief's job.** The instrument that assigns it already exists in the white paper: the brief names tonight's designations. The book's "wing and a prayer" (p. 122), run at tempo, is a service that starts without the brief naming who owns the door, the pass, the beverage program, the room's transition, and the emergency call that night. The realist's version: the pass must have a named owner at peak, and an unowned pass buries the line. Three per-service owners the white paper leaves unassigned: who reads the book and sets the flags before service; who triggers the room's transition between periods once a second period opens; who holds emergency authority that service (section 2).

**A ruling already made.** Brandon ruled (2026-09-13, extraction program) that the floor manager and the Maitre d are one seat: the Maitre d as master of the house holding both roles rather than saturating the floor with two leaders. Every "owner of the room" in this chunk therefore names the Maitre d; on a service the Maitre d does not work, the room designation in the brief. The structure that follows is 4.1's.

## 8. Accountability mechanisms

### What the white paper holds

The mechanisms listed in section 1, placed at their units: the shift brief and shift close at the service period, daily (WP pp. 13 to 14, 26); the feedback channel at the company, continuous (WP p. 22); the development review, the competency conversation, and the ninety-day plan at the person (WP pp. 11, 18 to 19); the content rhythm in Brandon's domain (WP p. 31); peer recognition as reinforcement (WP p. 19); the post-mortem and the exit interview as learning mechanisms feeding the qualitative side (WP pp. 17, 23). The white paper also holds monitoring: the OS surface "where the software agents live, each view sourced from several systems plus camera AI, updating live" (WP p. 21), with sensor-based food-safety logging, predictive maintenance, and a weapon-detection layer among the defensive tools (WP p. 21), and the rule that AI is "never [used for] anything load-bearing" (WP p. 21).

It is silent on any mechanism that crosses service periods, on a review of the whole house, on who acts on an alert, and on rhythms.

### Considerations

**Her two minimums have no weekly team to attach to.** The service period's team meets every service, at the brief; so the brief is the period's team meeting and does her first job, keeping everyone on priorities, progress, and who owns what (p. 126). What the brief cannot do is cross periods, and crossing periods is where bus failure lives (WP p. 13). The old synthesis added three mechanisms the white paper implies and does not name, and nothing else at the company level: a leads' review, weekly and cross-period (the two leads, the kitchen's owner of execution, the founders as their domains require), reviewing the week's closes across every period, the channel's open items, the goal pages, and incidents whose process changes need an owner; a house review, quarterly, one for the whole company in place of her per-division QBR, each open period reviewed against its charter and each domain against its goal page; and a phase-gate review, once per opening, the house review's form applied to the readiness test and the "steady" condition. Her second minimum survives intact: the leads' review opens with the metrics, and at Sŏn "the metrics" means the leading indicators before anything else, lagging figures last if at all.

**Snippets, with direction reversed.** Her snippets are written by leaders on Sunday for Monday (p. 123). At Sŏn the edge writes daily: the shift close is the snippet, structured, short, captured at the end of service by the people who felt the room. The center writes weekly: each lead files a one-page note before the leads' review, and the agenda derives from the closes and the notes. Neither is long-form.

**The house review, translated section by section.** Her QBR outline maps with a few replacements: the candid summary and the narrative survive with the leading indicators as the spine and lagging figures as an appendix owned by Dominic's domain; "user feedback" becomes the feedback channel's top asks, because "employees are the users" (WP p. 17), plus customer feedback about the team; "what keeps you up at night" is answered against the risk register; the cross-functional focus areas become the white paper's five failure modes as a standing scorecard, tension slack, node overload, sequence corruption, skill invisibility, cooling failure (WP p. 14); the headcount table becomes a tension map (which nodes are compensating for which, the ready-now pool's state), since headcount is not a variable; prior action items survive; the six statements that should be true afterward survive in Sŏn's terms, with "investment relative to output" read as bandwidth relative to results. The realist's caution: the review's length must be what the leads can actually prepare between services, and the first instance will calibrate it.

**The frequency rule assigns metrics to mechanisms.** Recognition delivered and close completeness move per service and are read at the close and the brief; loop closure and generosity utilization move over days and are read at the leads' review; employee NPS moves over weeks and is read at its rhythm; retention, internal promotion, and cross-period movement move over quarters and months and are read at the house review. Reading a metric faster than it can move is "demoralizing" in her word (p. 126) and a bandwidth cost in the white paper's.

**Monitoring is not accountability, and the actor is never a founder by default.** Her rule: decide ahead of time who acts on a sudden change in the dashboard and how they report cause and fix (pp. 126 to 127). The stack monitors; it holds no one accountable. The white paper's structural rule sharpens hers: a dashboard whose alerts route to Brandon is a system that produces stability by a founder's hand, node overload at the top of the web (WP p. 14), and the opposite of "a company built to run without them" (WP p. 06). Alerts route to the domain owner whose outcome they touch: food safety to the kitchen's owner of execution with the Operations Lead as compliance owner; a stack fault to the Operations Lead; a customer-record fault to the Operations Lead before service and the Maitre d at the brief; a weapon-detection alert to whoever holds emergency authority that service. The founders are the destination of escalation, not of alerts. No alert is enabled without a register entry naming its actor.

**The reset comes early.** Her annual January reset (p. 125) survives; Sŏn's first one comes after dinner's first quarter, because the mechanisms are designed against no operating history, and 2.1.18 already puts the principles' first revision at the same gate. Placing it is 2.3's.

### Options on the mechanism set

- **Option A: the white paper's mechanisms placed at their units, plus the leads' review, the house review, and the phase-gate review, and nothing else at the company level.** Commits the leads to one weekly cross-period meeting with a written record and the house to one quarterly review, and commits the founders to attending as their domains require rather than by default.
- **Option B: the book's set as written: a weekly meeting per team, a QBR per division, an all-hands showcase.** Commits Sŏn to units it does not have and to meetings the floor cannot attend.
- **Option C: the brief and the close only, with the founders reading the closes and deciding.** Commits the founders to being the cross-period mechanism, which is the memory failure the white paper diagnoses (WP p. 13).

## 9. Internal communications

### What the white paper holds

The trigger is named in structural terms: "Bus failure. Information cannot move between domains because the communication infrastructure is verbal handoffs, tribal knowledge, and informal relationships between individuals" (WP p. 13). The commitments: every candidate receives a single sheet with "communication expectations" among its items (WP p. 18); "every position has documented competency requirements visible to anyone" (WP p. 11); the feedback channel closes its loop (WP p. 22); reviews never contain new information (WP p. 19); candidates may choose the interview language (WP p. 17), which implies a team for whom English is not everyone's first language. For crisis, the white paper's rule for the brand is "honesty about what happened, the real impact, and the fix" (WP p. 31) and for the table "be direct about what happened, acknowledge the real impact, and make it right" (WP p. 26); it does not state the inward version. The white paper is silent on transparency of operating figures, review content, and the financial model; on whether the future concepts appear internally; on the team's languages; on whether the house ever gathers in one room; and on where the operating system lives for the team.

### Considerations

**Her trigger is headcount; Sŏn's is the hour.** Dunbar's number will not be reached (p. 128). The line of discrimination she warns about, between those with context and those without (p. 127), would run at Sŏn between service periods: the people who were in the room when a decision was made and the people who work a different hour. The design target is that no period's team learns of a decision later, or differently, than another's.

**The four tests survive, with Sŏn's edges.** Complete: the information people need is unusually specific and already promised, the competency path, the range and triggers for their position, tonight's designations and flagged records, their period's and domain's goals. Accessible: her segments are tenure, geography, and language; Sŏn's are hour, strand, and language. Reliable: the brief happens every service, the loop closes inside its window, the leads' review runs every week; these are readiness rows (section 10). Transparent: what is shared with whom, which is the line only the founders can draw.

**The transparency line.** The white paper commits to sharing the org chart, the direct lead, compensation mechanics, and the advancement path with every candidate (WP p. 18) and says nothing about operating figures, individual review content, or the financial model. The old synthesis's option: goals, metrics, owners, competency paths, ranges, and the decision-rights register visible to everyone in the building; individual reviews to the person and their domain lead; the financial model and lagging figures to the founders and whoever they name. Later sessions closed what the team could: everything not on the held list is shared at the moment it is versioned, to every open period at once, in every house language. What stays for the founders: which lagging figures, if any, appear on any surface the house reads; review content beyond the person and their lead; the financial model; and whether the chef partner reads the operating system before the seat is filled. One case from 5.5 tests the line at a figure: a person can check their own pool share by arithmetic only if they can see the day's pool, and a person who can see the pool at a known proportion can compute the day's revenue. That decision is 5.5's; the line drawn here has to leave room for it either way.

**Three channels, for a different reason.** Her "at least three times" (p. 132) is about desensitization. At Sŏn the reason is that no single channel reaches everyone, because the people are not in the building at the same time. The three: spoken at the brief of every open service period, by the lead; written on the team home, versioned; carried by the domain lead or the peer mentor the white paper assigns to every new hire (WP p. 18). Anything that changes a range, a trigger, a goal, a designation, or a mechanism goes through all three, and through the brief of every open period, not only the one where the decision was made. A later session turned this into something the stack can prove: a change has reached the house when every open period's brief since the version marks it and the team home shows it in every house language; a change has reached a person when their check-in dates after it. Whether Sŏn builds that record is Dominic's; that "reached" has a definition is decided here.

**The writing culture, tested against a workforce that does not sit at desks.** Her three arguments (pp. 129 to 130): the equalizer argument survives strengthened, because the person not in the room is most of the team at any hour, and the third founding seat; the higher-quality-thought argument survives at the center, where the founders and leads write goal pages, definitions, the house review, and the argument for any reserved-class change in prose; it does not survive at the edge as long-form. The edge writes in structured capture, the shift close and the feedback channel, short and fielded, at the end of service. Her lexicon of company terms (p. 130) survives as the definitions register and the standing vocabulary. Her deprecation rule survives as 2.1.18's versioning rule extended to every operating document: a version, an owner, a last change recorded as a decision. The brand's voice rules, which the old synthesis applied inward, are out of scope; a plain style rule for internal documents can be set without them.

**Crisis and change, told inward.** Her rule (pp. 132 to 133) and the white paper's brand rule (WP p. 31) are the same logic. The inward form the old synthesis argued: what happened, the real impact, the fix, at the next brief of every open period, in person before in writing; a key-person departure announced with its tension map (which nodes are compensating), so the team hears the structural read rather than a rumor about a slot. Her Goldilocks case, an event not widely known but affecting the company (p. 133), has a Sŏn form worth planning for: a founder's absence, a lead's departure, a stack outage that changed what the floor could do.

**The company-wide meeting.** Sŏn cannot hold one in her form without closing a service or finding a window every period's team can attend. The brief is a period's all-hands, every service. What it cannot do is put the whole house in one room, and the white paper's account of great nights as "every element in relationship with every other" (WP p. 09) is an argument that a team which has never been in one room has fewer of those relationships across periods. Whether to hold a whole-house gathering, and at what cost in closed service, is a cadence decision that 2.1 already routed to 2.3; the old item filed here goes the same way.

**The team home is not the OS surface.** The book's intranet (p. 134) and the workbook's instruction to put the organizational foundations table on the team's internal home page (p. 150) have one Sŏn form, and it must not be confused with the software. It is what the candidate sheet points to and what onboarding delivers (WP p. 18), readable between services on a personal device, in the team's languages. Whether it is a view on the OS surface or a separate surface depends on 2.1.7's ruling; its contents do not. Counsel question: whether any employee-facing document must exist in a language other than English by law, as distinct from by the white paper's commitment.

### Options on the transparency line

## 10. The readiness test: "the operating system is running"

### What the white paper holds

The phased opening's gate is a condition, not a date: dinner runs alone "for at least a quarter, so the heart is solid before anything is added to it," and the next periods follow "once dinner is steady" (WP p. 36). "Steady" is not defined. The white paper's own diagnostic for whether the structure is working is the failure-mode reference (WP p. 14) and the walk-through of three events, a problem at a table, a person wanting to advance, a key person leaving (WP pp. 13 to 14), each resolving by system or by a person's memory.

### Considerations

**A running condition, not a document inventory.** The old synthesis wrote a twenty-row test, each row a condition a named person could check and get a yes or a no, none met by a document's existence alone. The rows worth keeping, grouped: ownership (every domain has exactly one named owner readable by any team member; neither lead reports to the other on paper or in practice); decision rights (the register exists with every entry complete or marked incomplete; three servers and the Maitre d can state their range and triggers unprompted); goals and metrics (the long-term goals are published and every goal names its parent; every metric has a definitions entry; the leading indicators are being captured, including a defined cultural labor score); mechanisms (the brief has happened before every service and named the designations, the flagged records, the transition owner, and emergency authority; the close has captured every incident the leads know of; the feedback loop's oldest open item is inside the window; every alert has a named non-founder actor; no mechanism opens on a lagging figure); the system (every position's competencies are visible from a floor account and at least one skill set has unlocked without a lead's action; the stack holds the integration work, so the schedule posts from the system and preferences surface at the brief without retyping; every operating document has a version and an owner); the founders (they can be absent for a full service period and no decision waits on their return); the house (open periods match charters; the trade-offs and must-be-true list are published; the risk register has an owner per risk; the team home is readable by three team members across strands and hours from a personal device, in their languages). 2.3 adds the cadence rows. The realist flags any row whose check method cannot be run at tempo.

**How it is used.** Before dinner opens, as the operating-system half of the gate. At each later gate, because a new period can break the brief, the stack, the period count, and the team home rows without breaking anything else. And its failures map to the failure modes: a failed brief, close, loop, or stack row is sequence corruption; a failed competency row is skill invisibility; a failed absence row is node overload at the top of the web; a range written and not delivered is an onboarding failure (3.3).

**"Dinner is steady," argued to the ceiling.** The founders decide which readiness rows and which leading indicators constitute the gate, and the chef partner shapes the kitchen's half once seated. Candidates from the old work: every readiness row holds; the shift close's capture rate and the loop's closure hold for a stated run of weeks; employee NPS has a baseline and is not falling; recognition delivered has a baseline; the founders have been absent for a full period without a deferred decision; the incident log's cluster by class (section 4) does not resolve to a founder. No target value on any rate is set before a baseline exists. What "steady" is not: a revenue level, a cover count, or a date.

### Options on the gate

- **Option A: the readiness test plus a stated set of leading indicators with baselines, run as a phase-gate review, opens the next period.** Commits the founders to holding the morning closed until the test passes even when the room is ready.
- **Option B: the readiness test alone, with the indicators reported but not gating.** Commits Sŏn to opening on structure without evidence the structure is producing bandwidth.
- **Option C: a date, with the test as a checklist to complete by then.** Commits Sŏn to the book's plan and against WP p. 36.
