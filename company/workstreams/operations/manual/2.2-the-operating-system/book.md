# 2.2 The operating system: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 2, pp. 84 to 134, with the chapter's templates at pp. 150 to 155 (organizational foundations, objectives and metrics checklist, writing good OKRs) and the QBR guidelines in the workbook (pp. 23 to 41). Full text: `sources/extraction/build/s03/book.md` and `sources/extraction/build/s03/exercises.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## Where this section sits

Chapter 2 builds a house in three parts: founding documents (the foundations, chunk 2.1), the operating system (the mechanical system, this chunk), and the operating cadence (the moving parts, chunk 2.3). If the founding documents are the support structure, the operating system is the wiring and plumbing: substructures that work together to make up the whole (p. 84).

## What an operating system is

"A set of norms and actions that are shared with everyone in the company" (p. 84). Keystones like an annual plan, quarterly goals, and regular communications let everyone track progress and common priorities at the highest level, and the same structures replicate down through divisions and teams to clarify priorities and resolve dependencies. At every level, a good operating system makes clear the desired results, how and when to communicate progress, and how to measure achievement (p. 84).

Why it matters (pp. 84 to 85):

- Trust. People grow uncomfortable when they are unsure what is expected of them; they need to know how they are expected to work together, what they are working on, and why. Her Google line: every day people choose where to put their time; the leader's job is to give them the information to make the best decision.
- A stable frame of reference when external forces hit priorities; a throughline that lets people across the company speak the same language. Google's OKRs are her example of a replicable structure, taken seriously at the top and flowing down. She liked it when company OKRs were late, because it meant the decisions were hard and real.
- A bad operating system shows as confusion about who is doing what, which goals a team is accountable for, and to whom. Only clear ownership and accountability relieve it. A team charter cannot override a bad operating system but provides the basis for a good one (p. 85).

Her advice: get a simple company-wide operating system in place quickly, then iterate. Do not send players onto the field with lots of equipment and no rules (pp. 85 to 86). The manager's job is to understand and participate in these structures at the right level, reflect the company's mission, goals, and principles in the team's own wiring and plumbing, and, if they disagree with an element, improve it with their own manager rather than ignore it (p. 86).

**When to write it down (p. 86).** Once a company can no longer fit into a single meeting room. Before that, while still looking for product-market fit, it is too early: no point articulating why you exist while figuring out whether you should exist at all. At the inflection point, nail down the mission and founding documents, then the operating system, and start asking who and what you need to realize the vision, how decisions get made and prioritized, and how you will know you are successful.

**Table 1 (pp. 87 to 88).** The operating structures and the leader's and manager's role in each: mission, long-term goals, principles (articulate why, what, how); strategic and financial planning (decide strategy and top priorities; set the financial plan and targets; determine the org structure; assign teams to the work); team charters, goals, metrics (set objectives per team; be clear what you measure and how you report); ownership (establish work and role assignments; plan to hire for unassigned work; be clear who is accountable for what; develop skills); accountability mechanisms (measurement and reporting; review progress and course-correct; feedback and development goals; rewards and recognition); internal communications (a consistent set of practices; provide context and follow every process yourself); operating cadence (align the planning cadence on which all of the above is set, reviewed, and communicated).

## Strategic and financial planning (pp. 87 to 92)

The Peanut Butter Manifesto (Yahoo, 2006): a thin layer of investment spread across everything means focus on nothing. What its author wanted was a strategy. Her line: "A strategy should hurt." The trade-offs, where you invest and where you do not, should be painful and disappointing, internally or to customers. No strong strategy prioritizes everything at once (pp. 88 to 89).

Alongside strategy, long-term planning: the strategy realized as a multiyear set of financials, an annual exercise of setting the desired year-end outcome and allocating money and people toward it, with the mission and long-term goals in mind (p. 89).

McKinsey's three growth horizons: the current source of growth, the next (nascent but promising), and investment in a yet-to-be-determined third. In tech you often start on horizons two and three early (pp. 90 to 91). Young companies resist planning honestly; pre-product-market-fit the plan is short-term milestones; once fit exists, think longer term. A mid-stage company needs enough structure to speed progress without overburdening teams just being established. Amazon took a lot of painful iteration to reach its operating-plan approach; Stripe too. Start as simply as you can, then adapt (p. 91).

Her two artifacts (pp. 91 to 92):

1. A financial model of the next three or so years, plus a list of what needs to be true to achieve those numbers. Producing the list forces the strategic conversations. Revisit yearly.
2. A shorter-term plan answering what the company is trying to get done in the next six and twelve months, and what the P&L looks like by year end, linking out to the long-term themes. The financial outcomes are not the plan and not the reason the company exists, but they are a critical means of discipline and measurement.

Distinguish maturity levels: mature parts of the business have clear metrics to move; emerging parts may have a goal as simple as launch and test fit. Plan differently for each (p. 92).

## Resource allocation (pp. 93 to 102)

The primary resources are people and money. The art is funding earlier-stage efforts enough to let them prove themselves while streamlining mature parts for efficiency without undermining growth (p. 93).

Ratios (engineers to product managers, HR partners to employees) are a gut check, not a method: other companies' benchmarks do not reflect your model, ignore efficiency gains over time, and reward team size rather than impact (p. 93). Do not starve the current cash cow; horizon one invariably needs more people and money than it looks (p. 93).

Founders ask why adding people slows product velocity. The answer is the harsh side of scale: coordination and interface complexity rise with headcount (Figure 5, p. 94). Her three phases of engineering scale: scrappy teams in a shared codebase; decoupling and tooling with more senior hires; a painful redo of the infrastructure into a platform, at which point the whole company becomes separate units led by experienced people, loosely coupled on dependencies and tightly aligned through planning and goals (pp. 94 to 95). The sidebar from Stripe's CTO (pp. 95 to 98): no single metric tracks engineer output; measure productivity objectively and subjectively anyway; invest real money and a dedicated group in making others productive; services with well-defined interfaces let teams change internals freely.

Google's early headcount planning felt absurdly ratio-based, but its point was building the planning muscle: stepping back yearly to think about goals and resources (p. 98). Build that muscle early so the person best at arguing does not get the most resources. Objective measures, six-month allocation cycles, and a held reserve all reduce lobbying, at some cost to recruiting forecasts (p. 99). Getting resources reads as reward; celebrate managers who come in under budget or give headcount back (p. 99).

Sidebar (pp. 99 to 100): a founder whose leadership team fractured at allocation time. Her advice: set expectations before the contentious decision, out loud, every time. Keep the big picture, collaborate to a final decision, show up as a team.

Eric Schmidt's split of resources across the core business, emerging products, and research (p. 100) quieted Google's priority fights. Stripe's one-page prioritization: existential risks, then the core product, then new products, with a reserve for foundational company work (p. 101).

The side note that matters most (p. 101): leadership must invest in critical work no one else will naturally prioritize. Plans, incentives, and metrics are rarely built to mitigate risk or stop and redo work. Security, technical debt, halting a project: these need a top-down voice. Reserve space in team goals for top-down priorities and other teams' needs. Eric Yuan: his number one priority as CEO is the risk factors, not culture, values, and product alone (p. 102).

## Annual and quarterly goals (pp. 102 to 115)

Unlike long-term goals, these are tactical and measurable on a discrete horizon, a contract between the accountable team and the rest of the division. Two kinds: binary tasks and ongoing metrics. Goals replicate down from company to division to team to individual; her Stripe security example runs all three levels (pp. 102 to 103). OKRs and SMART goals are two structures for the same outcome: a clear, measurable objective people can be held to (pp. 103 to 104).

**The good-goals sidebar (Michael Siliski, pp. 104 to 111).** Why set goals: define success (statements about end states, not plans of activities); focus (distinguish the most important things from all the other good ideas); allow for autonomy (commit to a definition of success without mandating activities). What good goals look like: focused, concise, comprehensible, memorable; people can remember three to five things; plain English, because jargon masks strategic gaps; objectively assessable (not necessarily quantitative, never subjective); challenging but possible, aiming at roughly a seventy percent success rate; user-oriented, never organized by function, and set as close to the customer as possible; states, not activities; sensitive and specific enough to admit success and rule out failure. Table 2 refocuses "refactor backend" into "backend supports five-plus teams adding features concurrently" (pp. 106 to 107).

Heuristics for testing: a goal that starts with a verb is an action, so reframe it as the outcome (ask why until the true goal appears); no engineering goals versus business goals; no more than a page, three to five objectives, three to five key results each; if you do not wince at what was left out, you have not focused; if two people could disagree on whether it was achieved, it is not specific; if achieved and still dissatisfied, something is missing; if you could succeed without it, it is over-specific (pp. 107 to 108).

The FAQ: manage against goals by framing every conversation on them; score lightly mid-quarter and end-quarter in green, yellow, red, and if scoring is heavy the goals were not internalized; if a goal changes, start the new one and score the old one with an asterisk, but if that happens constantly the goals were too specific; quantification helps but a concrete goal with no numbers is fine as long as everyone assesses it the same way (Grove: did I do that or did I not); if you cannot measure it, use a proxy rather than the wrong goal; if you do not know the target, guess, say it is a guess, and update; if you cannot agree, stop arguing and diagnose top-down (vision and strategy, priorities, interpretation, incomplete success statements, feasibility, capacity); OKRs are goals with a structure separating where I want to go from how I pace myself (pp. 108 to 111).

**Further guidance (pp. 112 to 115):**

- Say up front what share of goals you expect to hit. Committed goals (hit fully) for existential threats, blocked top-priority teams, and anything promised to a customer on a timeline; aspirational goals (hit most of the way) elsewhere, because a stretch changes how people think and forestalls coasting. Declare which is which.
- Every individual's quarterly goals include one or two personal development goals beside the work output; the skills that got Q1 done will not be the ones needed a year on; a manager who does not keep people on development goals is not coaching, whatever they say.
- The "how" is as important as the "what." Review how the work was done, not only whether goals were hit. Pyrrhic victories are common: launched on time, team barely survived, a month of poor work after. "Performance = results × behaviors. It's multiplicative." Say the thing you think you cannot say: acknowledge a project accomplished at too high a cost, including your own role, so the team can learn before you set aspirational objectives next time.

## Metrics that matter (pp. 115 to 121)

Core company metrics replicate down like goals, on long and short horizons. Long-term metrics tend to be lagging: the output of many operational input metrics. Stripe opens each year with company targets, some financial outcomes and some strategic inputs (monthly active businesses, adoption of new products), plus zero targets, where the desired outcome is no incidence at all (outages). It should be clear which leaders and teams own the inputs behind each outcome (pp. 115 to 116).

Teams with less measurable outcomes (benefits, HR, finance, legal) still get metrics: the engagement survey is often the best read for internal teams; inputs in time and outputs in units of work can show a return, and knowing the quantified contribution helps morale and resourcing (pp. 116 to 117).

**Metrics write-up sidebar (Stripe data science, pp. 117 to 120).** Objectives answer where do I want to go; metrics answer how will I know I am getting there; one objective may carry several metrics. Long-term metrics define success against the mission and three-year vision, tend to be lagging, and should still have milestones in annual plans; a team keeps the same three to five charter metrics for years. Short-term operating metrics are real-time or leading indicators of activities and intermediate outcomes, three to five, often tied to team goals. Other metrics cover results for the half; turn binary metrics into continuous ones where you can. Have a dashboard, and review metrics regularly: weekly or biweekly metrics meeting, Monday standup, biweekly email, monthly business review.

Shared definitions are more critical than you think. What is a customer? What is churn when users churn intermittently or seasonally? Agree on company-wide definitions and accept they will never be perfect. If every rung of the ladder defines a metric differently, accountability disappears and teams fight. "As you establish your operating systems, the measurement piece is often the most difficult and critical part" (pp. 120 to 121).

## Ownership (pp. 121 to 122)

Goals and metrics need owners ultimately responsible for the work, from meeting action items up to the outcomes of a division. Each company target has an owner, most likely on the executive team; even with dependencies, someone tracks progress and escalates or unblocks. Her Stripe case: sales would not own revenue that depended on unlaunched products, so revenue was split into "what's on the truck" (sales) and a new-product line (product). Tease these apart at the top and it models clear ownership all the way down (p. 121).

Undefined ownership shows as finger-pointing ("I thought the sales dev reps were delivering the leads") and, worse, politics, with people jockeying to own the most important work. Ownership is easier where outcomes are measurable; where teams must collaborate, a pair of owners is risky but possible, or get granular about tasks. The worst mistake: putting a task to the team on "a wing and a prayer," hoping someone volunteers, or not even saying so (p. 122).

## Accountability mechanisms (pp. 122 to 127)

Tools for reviewing progress toward goals and missions, at company, team, and individual levels: meetings to review plans and action items, metrics dashboards, written snippets. First step: decide who participates and on what cadence. Her Stripe example: each Sunday the leadership team shares snippets (key information from the past week, priorities for the coming week, progress on action items), and the Monday meeting's agenda derives from them (p. 123).

QBRs: common once a company reaches a certain complexity (more than two hundred people, many products and teams). Backward-looking assessment of the quarter with key metrics, forward-looking discussion of the next few quarters; an hour to an hour and a half; a roughly five-page pre-read. Benefits: keeps leaders on the same page about focus and progress; problem-solves persistent issues; aligns leadership on priorities and how progress will be measured, or surfaces a strategy reset; holds the unit's leader accountable. Not every division is reviewed; support functions can run their own version internally (pp. 123 to 124). The workbook's QBR guidelines (pp. 30 to 41) give the outline: candid executive summary, narrative on charter and operating metrics skewed toward lowlights, current-quarter reflection, outlook including what keeps you up at night, cross-functional focus areas, a goals table, P&L, headcount, prior action items, top asks from users, and the statements that should be true afterward.

Company-level: showcase metrics and goals at the all-hands, weekly, monthly, or quarterly. Beyond a few summary emails and the all-hands, too many company-wide review mechanisms are not useful; keep a few, consistently, and let divisions choose their own. Take stock at least once a year; Stripe resets some meetings and mechanisms every January (p. 125).

Team-level: annual, quarterly, monthly, weekly cycles reflecting the company cadence; a few core mechanisms adopted everywhere lower cognitive load. Her two minimums for every team: a weekly team meeting (update or decision forum; the one standing place for norms, priorities, progress, and who owns action items) and a weekly metrics review in its first fifteen minutes, so everyone looks at the same numbers at the same time and everyone has a stake (pp. 125 to 126).

Frequency should match how quickly the team can move the metric: support response times weekly, because staffing and product changes show within a week; more often is demoralizing, less often hides trends (p. 126).

Accountability mechanisms are not monitoring. You also want an automated, ideally real-time dashboard with alerts when numbers cross thresholds. The accountability piece is deciding ahead of time who is responsible for acting on a sudden change and how they communicate root cause and solution (pp. 126 to 127).

## Internal communications (pp. 127 to 134)

If the company doubles yearly, most employees soon were not there for the decisions of two years ago. Decide how to commit important information to company memory, and have a clear policy so there is no culture divided between those with context and those without. Internal communications can be an equalizing or a discriminating force (p. 127).

At its best, another trust mechanism; at its worst, propaganda. If you need a team to convince employees the plan is great, you have a big problem. Trust is inversely proportional to hypocrisy; people forgive mistakes but lose trust when information is hidden, false, or misleading, or when leadership does not follow through. Your division should know what will be communicated, when, and where; meeting notes with decisions and next steps give confidence and settle later disputes; people must be able to get the information they need without depending on who they know or how long they have been there (pp. 127 to 128).

When to invest: Dunbar's number, around 150 people, when names, teams, and responsibilities stop being rememberable. By then: an internal website, communication guidelines, policies for what gets stored, through what channels, who maintains what, and a way to deprecate stale content (p. 128). Company-wide communications weave the principles into the fabric; teams running their own playbooks breed subcultures. Stripe's bulwark is long-form writing (pp. 128 to 129).

**Stripe's writing culture sidebar (pp. 129 to 131).** Discussions are written up and sent as notes; reviews require a pre-read; presentations are memos; all stored on the wiki. Three reasons: writing is an equalizer for those not in the room, including future hires; long-form leads to higher-quality thought (gaps show when sentences must connect; it is hard to skim); writing is efficient because context already exists. The cost: many documents, so distinguish evergreen, work-in-progress, and one-time; build discovery and hierarchy; set guidelines for meeting notes; document the quirks and stories, not only the big decisions; say what good writing means, with a style guide, examples, feedback, classes. First step: write that down.

**The four tests (p. 131).** Is it complete (the information people need to do their jobs)? Accessible (across tenure, geography, language)? Reliable (accurate; meetings start on time; updates at a predictable cadence)? Transparent (not sharing everything, but everyone knows what will be shared, when, and with whom, including which HR information goes to whom)?

Communicate important information at least three times through different channels, because people desensitize and have different preferences: a biweekly meeting, a dashboard, a newsletter (p. 132).

Communicate more in crisis and change. Communication tends to taper in a crisis; do not wait for all the answers; a personal note of reflection buys time and trust. Her YouTube example: a short note saying the acquisition was smart and she would keep the team informed. When the event is not widely known but affects the company, there is a Goldilocks problem: too soon breeds anxiety, too late breeds anger. Consult peers and apply the golden rule of what you would want to know, and when (pp. 132 to 133).

Make company-wide meetings count. Schedule them sparingly; frequent when small, less effective as you scale. Reserve them for hearing from and emotionally connecting with leaders and highlighting critical results; model what should be celebrated. If they are poor, attendance and effect suffer; invest or hold them less often (p. 133).

Intranet: Stripe Home, built by the internal tools team, connects individuals and helps them discover information; such internal mechanisms are small, critical investments in future scale, not luxuries (pp. 134). The sidebar traces it from a people directory built at a hackathon into the company's information home.

## Templates and exercises

**Organizational foundations (pp. 150 to 151; workbook p. 23).** A table to fill for an individual report, a team, and a division: mission; objectives with owner (DRI); key metrics; accountability mechanisms; operating cadence. Is the information documented and easy to find? If you struggle, imagine how your teams feel. Put it on the team's and division's internal home pages, with the top objectives and metrics with targets for the year.

**Objectives and metrics checklist (pp. 151 to 152; workbook pp. 24 to 25).** For each objective: metric, baseline, target. Are the objectives inspirational, actionable by your team, related to the team's mission and vision, related to the company's priorities, and SMART? Is the metric a leading indicator? Does it have counterweight metrics (users up but revenue down)? Is there a calculation or query? Does it have an owner? Is it relative, not absolute? Does it account for existing growth?

**Writing good OKRs (pp. 153 to 155; workbook pp. 26 to 29).** Three to five objectives with one to five key results each per quarter; mark must-hit goals; get feedback from partner teams; publish by changing "Draft" to "On track." Ambition: most goals ambitious yet attainable, scoring in the seventy to eighty percent range overall; a small share of hard commitments marked must-hit at ninety-five percent or more, and other goals sacrificed to hit them. Objectives are outcomes, states of the world at quarter end, focused on the user problem, no more than three to five, long-term and nesting, ambitious, aspirational or committed and labeled. Key results are concrete definitions of success, outcomes not activities, measurable and binary, flowing from a plausible causal mechanism, compounding if possible. Pitfalls: activity as outcome; unmeasurable key result; roadmap turned into OKRs; too many. Mid-quarter scoring in green, yellow, red with on track, off track, at risk. Changes: document added or dropped key results and notify affected teams; document what an added objective deprioritized; dropping an objective should be rare and stays on the page for discussion.

**QBR guidelines (workbook pp. 30 to 41).** The outline summarized under accountability mechanisms above, with a section-by-section template and the statements that should be true after the meeting: metrics stated with trajectory; which goals were executed and which not, and why; investment relative to output; mutual expectations for the coming period; obstacles through year end; what the unit needs to accelerate.
