# 5.8 Managing low performers: working session

## 1. Orientation

This chunk covers what happens when someone's work is not holding: how the house knows, what it checks before the person is the subject, the conversation, what is written, the plan, who judges it, and the outcomes short of departure. It sits after coaching (5.1), hard feedback (5.2), and the formal review (5.4) because the book treats it as the point where those tools have not been enough, and before managing out (5.10), which receives whatever this chunk hands it. The book gives it half its performance material; the prior extraction built its largest single mechanism here and argued the steady middle (5.7) deserved the weight instead. Both are on the table.

Upstream, already decided or in progress: the repeated hypothesis and what is handed here (5.1.9), what a lead may read (5.1.3), the serious-breach boundary (5.2.2, 5.2.3), counsel on the record of a hard conversation (5.2.4), whether a hard conversation leaves a record (5.2.8), the hard conversation's form (5.2.5), the failure types (5.2.9), the ninety-day plan and its extension (3.3.10, 3.3.12), a minor early issue (3.4.5), the hiring retrospective (3.4.6, 3.4.10), the skill-will matrix (4.2.5), evidence and recurrence counts (4.2.6, 4.2.10), the domain repeat (4.3.12), how a person moves (4.3.7, 3.2.42, 3.3.29), what a lead may write about a person (4.4.2), and the assessor pattern (3.2.35, 4.1.11). Compensation (5.5) decides any pay effect. 5.10 decides everything after "not held."

Who else must agree: both seated founders on every decision; counsel on 5.8.2's questions before 5.8.9, 5.8.11, 5.8.14, and 5.8.15 close; the chef partner on the kitchen's assessors (5.8.13). The two leads read the policy and guide before they are final.

## 3. Briefs

### 5.8.3 Decide who holds the function the book calls HR

**The question:** with no HR seat and the back office in software, who is the custodian of employment records, who is the second person in the room on a performance process, and when is counsel called by rule?

**Why it matters now:** the first performance process may happen in training services before opening. The book tells HR at the start (p. 434). At Sŏn there is no one to tell unless this decision names them, and counsel's answer on who may lawfully hold records (5.8.2) needs a seat to land on.

**Openers:**
- A lead comes to you and says "I think this is becoming a pattern." Who is the second person who hears that sentence, and where does the first piece of paper live?
- Sideways: the white paper removed the back-office administrator by moving "cash, payroll, HR, compliance" into software (WP p. 22). Which of those four is a spreadsheet, and which is a person's judgment about another person?
- Have you ever watched a good manager get a termination wrong because nobody with experience was in the room? What would have changed it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Record custodian: the Operations Lead? A founder until the lead exists?
- Second reader: the other lead? The partner who holds the lead's monthly conversation?
- Counsel called by rule at which points?
- An outside HR advisory retained? (Check what the payroll platform's plan includes; do not assume.)
- Confirm or reverse: no HR, floor-manager, or assistant-manager seat is created for this.

**What the book says:** tell HR once you have identified a low performer; do not hand them the management of it; a retained lawyer can substitute for training and materials; an experienced HR person flags risk (pp. 433 to 434).

**White-paper default:** the Operations Lead holds compliance; the Maitre d holds team health (WP p. 10); the administrator is removed (WP p. 22). No custodian named.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Independent restaurants below a few dozen staff almost never have HR; the owner or GM holds the file and a lawyer is called late. Groups that use a PEO or a payroll platform's HR advisory get the "seen this before" function without a seat.

**Options:** (a) no seat; Operations Lead as custodian, the other lead or a partner as second reader, counsel by rule. (b) an outside advisory retained from the start. (c) a seat at a headcount threshold. Depth: cons. 2.

**Watch for:** a custodian who is also the person's lead; a second reader who is never actually in the room.

**A finished answer:** three names or seats (custodian, second reader, counsel trigger), yes or no on an advisory, and the seat rule.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.8.4 Decide what "not holding" means at Sŏn, and the order of reads before a person is the subject

**The question:** against what is a gap measured, and what must the house have checked about itself before a lead may say to a person, "this is about you"?

**Why it matters now:** this is the white paper's central claim (universal problems are design problems, WP p. 17) applied to one person. If the order is too long, real gaps go unaddressed for months and the book's "move deliberately" (p. 437) is lost. If it is too short, the house blames the person for its own missing module.

**Openers:**
- A server is slow on the sequence three Saturdays running. Walk me through what you would want to know before you said a word to them. Now tell me how long that took.
- Sideways: the white paper's tension-slack entry says the fix "is not replacing the person. It is diagnosing which adjacent nodes are overloaded" (WP p. 14). When is that true, and when is it an excuse?
- Where does individual accountability begin at Sŏn? The white paper's only "at fault" sentence is about customers (WP p. 26).
- The zero targets: a regular goes unrecognized. Whose miss is it, the first time? The third?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The standard: the seat row, the readiness rows, the plan's milestones?
- The order before the person is the subject: incident as a process; failure type; domain fix made and read back; seat definition realistic; supplied versus needed; the person's account. All of them, every time?
- Or the full order only past a recurrence count (4.2.10), and a short version before?
- A zero-target miss at the person level: process first, same recurrence path as anything else?

**What the book says:** a persistent gap between performance and the role's demands; diagnose skill, will, or wrong job; context matters (p. 432); two or three explicit conversations before any formal step (pp. 434 to 435).

**White-paper default:** failure-mode reference before the people level (WP p. 14); what allowed this, not who failed (WP p. 17).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Safety-critical industries (aviation, healthcare) run a "just culture" sequence that asks whether the system, the training, or the person failed, in that order, and reserve individual consequence for recklessness or repeated disregard after the system is fixed. It is the closest published pattern to what the white paper describes.

**Options:** (a) the full order every time. (b) the book's, with the failure-mode reference run once at the first repeat. (c) short before a count, full past it. Depth: cons. 3.

**Watch for:** an order so long that no lead runs it and the process goes underground; a standard that is the lead's expectation rather than a row.

**A finished answer:** the standard named, the steps in order, whether they run every time, and the zero-target sentence.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.8.5 Decide the triggers: what may open the performance track, who opens it, and what never may

**The question:** what starts this, who starts it, and what can never be the reason?

**Why it matters now:** 5.1.9 and 3.3.12 are already handing things here. The stack is being configured (4.2.18). And the refused list is the policy a candidate can trust on the sheet.

**Openers:**
- Which is worse: a lead who opens this on a person they do not like, or a lead who never opens it on a person they do? Which one does a record-opened trigger fix?
- Sideways: the white paper's "no one's advancement waits on a manager's attention or judgment" (WP p. 11) is about going up. Does the same sentence apply going the other way?
- A cook's colleagues tell you they are covering for them every night. Is that a trigger, or is it gossip?
- Read the refused list aloud: a rescheduled check-in, a recognition tally, a hiring scorecard, a customer rating, a designation count, a peer's account, a review, a founder's request. Which one do you want back?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The triggers: the repeated hypothesis (5.1.9); the absorbed-duties statement counted across check-ins; a basic failure recurring after a fix; the ninety-day goal not held after one extension; the attendance count (5.8.6). Add, drop?
- Who opens: the record at a count; the lead on the record; the lead on judgment?
- The counts as reset parameters.
- What never opens or is cited.

**What the book says:** the trigger is the manager's read that feedback has become a pattern; form a hypothesis and set a timeline (pp. 432, 438 to 439).

**White-paper default:** no surprises (WP p. 19). Silent on triggers.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Attendance and safety triggers are commonly rule-based in the trade; quality and conduct triggers are almost always a manager's judgment. Fully record-opened performance processes are rare outside heavily instrumented work.

**Options:** (a) the record opens it. (b) the lead opens it, citing a record, and cannot without one. (c) the lead opens it on judgment, as the book has it. Depth: cons. 4.

**Watch for:** a trigger the stack cannot actually read (5.8.22 has a manual fallback for a reason); a peer's account entering through the absorbed-duties trigger if 5.1.3 refused it as a source.

**A finished answer:** the trigger list, the opener, the parameters named, and the refused list.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.8.6 Decide the attendance states and how a lateness pattern is read

**The question:** what does the schedule record about showing up, what does the house ask itself before it reads the count against a person, and what does it refuse to record?

**Why it matters now:** attendance is the most common performance issue in the trade and a candidate will ask about it. The scheduling surface is being configured. 3.4.5 reads the same facts in the first weeks and needs this rule.

**Openers:**
- Someone is late three times in two weeks. Before you say anything, list what could be true that is not about them.
- Sideways: the book's example is "consistently late," and its advice is "give feedback quickly and strongly" (p. 435). Prior work read that as the one case where the person's account should come first. Who is right?
- A points policy: predictable, defensible, and the reason a transit failure and a no-call look the same. Do you want one?
- Should the person be able to see their own states any time, and should a lead be able to write a note next to them?

**Narrowing questions:**
- States: worked, missed, late against crew call with the time?
- Window; count as a reset parameter.
- The questions first: shift shape against stated availability, transit, the schedule's language and posting, crew call against the pre-service window.
- Refusals: no free-text reason, no lead's note, no read in a room, no per-person comparison, no read by the point system beyond days counted.
- May the person add a reason in their own words if they choose?
- 3.4.5's reading in the first weeks: the same facts?

**What the book says:** do not wait for the review cycle to say something about lateness (p. 435); behavioral issues are assessable in two to three weeks (pp. 445 to 446).

**White-paper default:** respect for time (WP p. 17), toward the candidate; communication expectations on the candidate sheet (WP p. 18). Silent on attendance.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Points-based policies dominate hospitality and retail; "no-fault" versions ignore the reason on purpose. The questions-first design is closer to what union grievance procedures ask after the fact, moved to before.

**Options:** (a) states, window, questions first, refusals, count as parameter. (b) a conventional published points policy with counsel. (c) (a) plus a reason in the person's own words if they choose. Depth: cons. 5.

**Watch for:** a "no reason field" that erases a hardship the person wanted known (5.8.15); a count read at the brief.

**A finished answer:** the states, the window, the questions, the refusals, the count, the person's view, and the first-weeks rule.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; counsel reviews.

### 5.8.7 Decide the pauses and the seat repeat

**The question:** which findings stop the process because the house broke its own promise, who clears a pause, and when is a seat the problem rather than the person in it?

**Why it matters now:** the first pause scenario (a hire made against a documented concern on promised support) is likely in an opening cohort hired fast. The rule needs to exist before the first plan closes.

**Openers:**
- You hired someone the scorecard flagged, because you promised yourself you would give them extra time. You did not. Ninety days later, whose window is this?
- Sideways: the third different person in the same seat is not holding. What does the seat row say that is not true?
- Who is the person a lead least wants to admit the unkept promise to? Should that person be the one who clears the pause?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Pauses: hired against a documented concern with support undelivered; extended on a promised fix unmade; a needed module or row did not exist. Add, drop?
- What "paused" means: the clock stops, the person is told, the promise is kept, then it resumes?
- Who clears: the second reader (5.8.3)?
- Seat repeat: a second different person in a stated window sends the seat to 4.3.12 before any person question.

**What the book says:** context matters; you cannot lower the bar but can adjust responsibilities (pp. 432 to 433). No pause.

**White-paper default:** management absorbs blame (WP p. 17); a universal problem is a design problem (WP p. 17).

**How others have handled it:** Nothing useful beyond the just-culture pattern in 5.8.4.

**Options:** (a) the three pauses, cleared by the second reader, and the seat repeat to 4.3.12. (b) no formal pause; the order of reads in 5.8.4 is expected to catch it. Depth: cons. 6.

**Watch for:** a pause that becomes a way for a lead to avoid the conversation indefinitely.

**A finished answer:** the pause list, the meaning, the clearer, and the seat-repeat sentence.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.8.8 Decide the conversation that names the pattern

**The question:** what does the lead say, against what, and what is the honest answer to "do you think I can succeed"?

**Why it matters now:** 5.2.5's form covers the hard conversation; this is the one conversation that changes a person's standing, and the two sentences the book requires must be in the guide before any lead holds it.

**Openers:**
- Say the two sentences out loud: "this is now a pattern," and "the seat's expectations are not being met." Which one is harder? Now say them so that nothing in them is new to the person.
- Sideways: prior work would open by reading back the person's own words from an earlier check-in. Is that kinder or crueler than the book's version?
- Someone asks you, "can I do this?" You think probably not. What do you say that is neither a lie nor a sentence?
- The person says the facts are wrong. What is your next move?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Opening: the trigger read back in the person's earlier words; the seat row or readiness rows as the standard.
- The two required sentences.
- The questions: what do you think is going on, do you like this work, is this the right seat.
- Is the lead's hypothesis about the outcome ever said aloud?
- The answer to "can I succeed."
- Disputed facts: more examples; move to observation; gather more within a week and restart or debrief honestly.
- Off the floor, on the clock, in the person's language, interpreter by 4.8.5.

**What the book says:** hard feedback plus two additions; name the pattern and the unmet expectation against an objective source; denial and relief; the three questions; no flat no on "can I succeed" but honesty if hard; multiple sessions if needed; own a misread (pp. 439 to 444).

**White-paper default:** no surprises (WP p. 19); never assert the unproven (WP p. 36).

**How others have handled it:** Nothing useful beyond the book, whose guidance here is specific and good.

**Options:** the book's conversation as written, with 5.2.5's form; the same with the record-read-back opening; the same with the hypothesis never spoken. Depth: cons. 7.

**Watch for:** a conversation that ends without the second sentence said; a "can I succeed" answer that is a prediction either way.

**A finished answer:** the opening, the two sentences, the questions, the hypothesis rule, the "can I succeed" answer, the dispute move, and the setting.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.8.9 Decide what is written, in whose words, and who reads it

**The question:** after the conversation, what exists on paper, who wrote it, and who may read it?

**Why it matters now:** counsel's answer (5.8.2, 5.2.4) may make some options unavailable. The person page's fields are being built (3.3.41). And this is what a person will trust or not trust the house on.

**Openers:**
- The book says documentation builds trust because it is a shared record (p. 436). Prior work said a document in the lead's voice is a case file. Both are describing the same email. What makes the difference?
- Sideways: imagine reading your own file, written by a manager, three years later. Now imagine reading a file that holds only what you said. Which one is truer?
- If counsel says a manager's record is what defends a separation, does that settle it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The record: the book's recap after each conversation; the stack's citations plus the person's words; a joint recap read back and signed.
- Readers: the person and lead; plus the second reader; plus counsel.
- The four letters: exist at Sŏn in any form?
- Kept how long (counsel).
- In the person's language.

**What the book says:** deliver in person, follow up in writing; a line for a one-off, a document for a pattern, shared with HR if escalated; the shared record builds trust (pp. 435 to 436, 443 to 444); four templates (pp. 474 to 477).

**White-paper default:** no surprises (WP p. 19); one line per check-in in the person's words (2.3.11); what a lead may write (4.4.2).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Manager-authored write-ups are the norm and are what most employment counsel expect to see; jointly signed recaps are common in unionized settings; records in the employee's own words alone are rare.

**Options:** (a) the manager's recap. (b) citations plus the person's words, no lead's voice. (c) a joint recap, signed. Depth: cons. 8.

**Watch for:** deciding before counsel has answered; a record the person cannot read because of its language.

**A finished answer:** what is written and by whom, the readers, the letters yes or no, retention, and language.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; counsel's answer in hand.

### 5.8.10 Decide whether a person's performance situation is ever a field, a list, or a count

**The question:** does any record or room ever hold which situation a person is in, and how does leadership see the process without seeing names?

**Why it matters now:** the person page and the leads' review record are being built. 5.7.2's language and 5.4's review designations both depend on this.

**Openers:**
- A window has an open date and a close date on the person page. Is that a status? Who can see it?
- Sideways: the book says the shift to a formal designation should never be implicit (p. 440). Prior work said no record should ever sort a person. Can both be true?
- Dominic asks how many people are in a window right now. What do you show him?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Any field, list, or room: none; the window's state on the person page for the person and lead only; a designation at the formal review (5.4)?
- Aggregate view: counts by seat class and period on the leads' review, never names?
- Reads with 5.7.2's word for holding a seat.

**What the book says:** the designation is explicit (pp. 439 to 440); the review template proposes one (workbook p. 468).

**White-paper default:** competencies anyone can see (WP p. 11); the path forward rather than a verdict (WP p. 19). No sorting.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most companies with formal reviews have a rating; most small restaurants have none and a manager who knows. The middle ground, a state visible only to the two people in it, is unusual and buildable.

**Options:** (a) none. (b) the window's state, person and lead only. (c) a review designation per 5.4. Depth: cons. 9.

**Watch for:** an aggregate count small enough to identify a person.

**A finished answer:** the rule, the aggregate view, and the sentence reconciling it with 5.7.2.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.8.11 Decide the plan's form

**The question:** the book's signed improvement plan, a bounded window opened by the record and closed by an assessor, or a second plan in the ninety-day form?

**Why it matters now:** this is the chunk's largest decision and the one counsel cares most about. The build (5.8.22) and the kit (5.8.21) follow from it.

**Openers:**
- Read the book's PIP template (pp. 477 to 481) as the person receiving it. Which sentence do you stop at?
- Sideways: the ninety-day plan already has milestones, assessments, and check-ins (WP p. 18). What does a person in year three need that a new hire's plan does not give them?
- The book says most PIPs where the person never accepted the feedback end in departure (p. 448). Is a document that usually ends one way a plan or a notice?
- Prior work built a window measured in shifts, opened by the record, closed by someone who is not the lead. What in that do you want, and what is machinery?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Form: the PIP with counsel's clauses; the window; a second plan in 3.3.10's form with a stated close.
- Must name: the rows against the seat; the fixes the house owes; the check-in cadence inside; the close and how it is read.
- Does the person sign?
- Never: the first hearing of the gap; a document in the lead's voice alone; a prediction; "probation." The termination sentence: counsel's question.

**What the book says:** the PIP names skills or behaviors, an end date, and how progress is measured; weekly check-ins; be direct that continued underperformance could lead to termination (pp. 444 to 447); the template's sections and clauses (pp. 477 to 481); never the first exposure to the feedback (p. 478).

**White-paper default:** the ninety-day plan's form (WP p. 18); no surprises (WP p. 19). No improvement plan.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. The signed PIP with an at-will clause is standard in US employment practice and is what counsel usually recommends; its reputation among employees as a pre-termination formality is also standard. Some companies have replaced it with a shorter "coaching plan" that carries no termination language and rely on the documented conversations before it.

**Options:** (a) the PIP. (b) the window. (c) a second plan in 3.3.10's form. Depth: cons. 10.

**Watch for:** choosing (b) without an assessor pool for every seat; choosing (a) and letting it be the first hearing.

**A finished answer:** the form, the required contents, signature yes or no, the never list, and the termination sentence's status pending counsel.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; counsel reviews.

### 5.8.12 Decide the time

**The question:** how long does the plan run, by issue type, in shifts or weeks, and who may extend or end it early?

**Why it matters now:** the parameter has to exist before the first plan opens. Prior work's argument for shifts (a part-time person's month is not a full-time person's month) is a real difference in a restaurant.

**Openers:**
- Someone works two shifts a week. Someone else works five. Same gap, same "one month." Is that fair?
- Sideways: the book's three clocks: operational issues may need a full cycle, skills fit one to two months, behavior two to three weeks (pp. 445 to 446). Which of Sŏn's likely gaps is which?
- Who should be able to say "we are done early," and in which direction?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Length per issue type, in scheduled shifts or weeks, as a reset parameter.
- Extension: the lead, the person, or only a made-and-cited fix? How many times?
- Early end: by whom, which direction?

**What the book says:** resolve within three months, sometimes one (p. 437); the three types and their clocks (pp. 445 to 446); the PIP may be ended early (p. 480).

**White-paper default:** silent.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Thirty, sixty, and ninety days are the customary PIP lengths; shift-counted windows are used in some scheduled-shift industries for exactly the part-time reason.

**Options:** weeks per type; shifts per type; a single length with an extension rule. Depth: cons. 10.

**Watch for:** a length nobody can compute for a variable schedule; an early end that only ever shortens toward departure.

**A finished answer:** the lengths as parameters, the extension rule, the early-end rule.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 5.8.13 Decide who judges the close

**The question:** at the end of the plan, who says whether the gap closed, reading what?

**Why it matters now:** the book's own warning is a PIP that ends in a dispute over how to judge it (p. 446). Sŏn already has a pattern (an assessor who neither taught nor decides) for hiring and readiness; whether it applies here decides whether the lead is coach and judge.

**Openers:**
- The lead who has coached someone for six weeks now decides whether they passed. What could go wrong in each direction?
- Sideways: 3.2.35 already says the person who assesses the hiring practical neither teaches it nor decides the hire. Is a plan's close different?
- A behavioral gap, the book's third type: what would a hands-on skills check even show?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Reader: the lead; a separate assessor from the pool; the book's peer input (5.1.3 may have refused it).
- Reads: the readiness rows; the rows the plan named; the attendance count.
- Result written in whose words.
- What the person hears and when.
- The behavioral case: what instrument, if not the rows?

**What the book says:** define the measure; the what and the how; peer feedback or another manager to avoid a dispute (pp. 445 to 446).

**White-paper default:** competency validated by a practical assessment with the domain lead; no one touches a table until ready (WP p. 19).

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Trades with practical certification separate examiner from teacher; most PIPs are judged by the manager who wrote them, and the disputes the book describes follow.

**Options:** (a) a separate assessor, hands-on, rows. (b) the lead. (c) the lead with a second manager's read. Depth: cons. 10.

**Watch for:** an assessor pool that does not exist for the seat; a behavioral gap judged on a skills check.

**A finished answer:** the reader, what they read, whose words, what the person hears, and the behavioral instrument.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for kitchen assessors.

### 5.8.14 Decide the outcomes and who decides each

**The question:** held, a move, a step back, or the hand-off: what does each produce, who chooses, and what exactly goes to 5.10?

**Why it matters now:** 5.10 receives only what this decision hands it. 4.3.7 and 3.2.42 already say how a person moves; whether a move here is chosen or placed is the divergence from the book. The lead-never-alone rule is a policy a candidate will read.

**Openers:**
- The person passes, narrowly. Six months later the same gap. Is that a new window or the same one?
- Sideways: the book's second outcome is the manager moving the person to another team (p. 448). At Sŏn, can a lead place anyone anywhere?
- Is there such a thing as going down a skill level at Sŏn? Who would choose it?
- What is in the envelope that goes to 5.10, and what is not allowed in it?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Held: what is said, what remains, and a second window in a stated period.
- Move: seat inside the domain; period or specialization; domain. Chosen by the person from what is open through 3.2.42 and 3.3.29, or placed by the lead?
- Step back: exists? Person's choice? Pay is 5.5's.
- Not held, no move: the package (citations, plan, close in the assessor's words, person's words, nothing else) to 5.10; decided by whom 5.10 names; never the lead alone.

**What the book says:** three outcomes (p. 444); most unaccepted PIPs end in departure (p. 448); the narrow pass (p. 448); hypothesis-based coaching can find a better-fit role (p. 448).

**White-paper default:** cross-training as career development (WP p. 19); competency conversations scheduled by the person (WP p. 19). No demotion anywhere.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Reassignment at the manager's discretion is the norm; a chosen move from open seats is how internal transfer usually works in larger companies and rarely in restaurants.

**Options:** for moves: chosen, or placed. For the step back: exists or not. For the hand-off: closed package or the book's open file. Depth: cons. 11.

**Watch for:** a "move" that is a placement dressed as a choice; a step back that is a demotion by another name; a package that grows.

**A finished answer:** four outcomes, each with what it produces and who decides, and the package's contents.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; 5.5 for the step back's pay; 5.10 for what follows.

### 5.8.15 Decide how personal hardship behind a gap is handled

**The question:** when something in a person's life is behind the gap, what may a lead ask, what may the house adjust, and who initiates?

**Why it matters now:** leave and accommodation law applies (counsel, 5.8.2). The attendance design (5.8.6) may have removed the place a hardship would have been written. And the first hardship will arrive before anyone expects it.

**Openers:**
- A cook's work falls off. You suspect something at home. What is the first sentence out of your mouth, and what is the one you must not say?
- Sideways: the book's offer is "a part-time or less demanding role if the hardship is temporary" (p. 433). Prior work called that a judgment about someone's life and made it a request the person raises. Who is right, for the person who does not know they may ask?
- Does the window pause during an approved leave?

**Narrowing questions:**
- What a lead may ask now (4.4.6 covers background).
- What the person may raise, to whom.
- What the house may adjust and who decides: a schedule change through the ordinary path with no performance read; a temporary period change; the book's lead-offered lesser role.
- Pause during leave?
- The bar does not move: confirm.

**What the book says:** hardship can sit behind a gap; involve HR; do not back away from feedback; adjust responsibilities, not the bar (p. 433).

**White-paper default:** burnout is a system failure (WP p. 17). Silent on hardship.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Most employers route hardship through a leave or accommodation process the employee initiates, and train managers not to ask about the cause. The standing-offer approach (a published page of what anyone may ask for) is how larger employers avoid the manager's guess.

**Options:** (a) the person initiates through a published standing offer; the lead may not offer a lesser role. (b) the book's: the lead may offer. (c) (a) plus a lead who may say "there are things you can ask for" without asking why. Depth: cons. 12.

**Watch for:** a rule that leaves the person who does not know to ask; a lead who asks about the cause.

**A finished answer:** what a lead may ask, the standing offer's contents, who decides an adjustment, the pause rule, and the bar sentence.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; counsel.

### 5.8.16 Decide what the team hears

**The question:** while a colleague's work is not holding, what reaches the room, and what does a lead say to the people carrying the load?

**Why it matters now:** the people covering know first and are the source of the absorbed-duties trigger (5.8.5). What they hear back decides whether they keep telling the house or stop.

**Openers:**
- A runner tells you, quietly, that they have been doing half of someone else's job for a month. What do they hear back, tonight and in a month?
- Sideways: the white paper says the compensation is "visible to everyone except the person looking at an organizational chart" (WP p. 8). The team already knows. What does privacy protect, then?
- The outcome is a move. What does the team hear, and from whom?

**Narrowing questions:**
- The process is private: nothing reaches a room or a peer. Confirm.
- To the person carrying the load: the fix the domain is making; that it was heard; nothing about the colleague.
- Where the report goes: the channel; the check-in; the counted statement.
- At a move or departure: 4.3.9's rule.

**What the book says:** morale drag is disproportionate (pp. 432 to 433); share with HR only if escalated (p. 436).

**White-paper default:** adjacent nodes compensate at cost (WP p. 8); post-mortems are private (WP p. 17).

**How others have handled it:** Nothing useful beyond the two sources.

**Options:** as narrowed above; the only real choice is what the load-carrier hears back. Depth: cons. 13.

**Watch for:** a "private" process that the team can read from the schedule.

**A finished answer:** the privacy sentence, the words to the load-carrier, the destination, and the pointer to 4.3.9.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
