# 5.8 Managing low performers: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 5, pp. 432 to 448, with related templates at pp. 474 to 482. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn. Full text: `sources/extraction/build/s14/book.md`, exercises at `sources/extraction/build/s14/exercises.md`.

## What makes someone a low performer

When one-off constructive feedback to a report becomes a pattern, and there's a persistent gap between their performance and the demands of their role, you're managing a low performer. A low performer isn't always a low performer: it's about context. There may be a skill issue or a will issue, or the person may simply be in the wrong job. The manager's job is to diagnose which, then either improve the situation or move the person out of the team or company (p. 432).

A pattern of low performance is a problem for two reasons: it drags down the team's overall output, and low performers are often an outsized drag on morale, undermining the group in ways disproportionate to their individual work. She frames handling low performers as some of the most critical work a manager does outside of maintaining the operating system, given the recruiting, hiring, and onboarding cost already sunk into that person (pp. 432 to 433).

The goal is either to help the employee succeed at the company, or to make a decision and help them move on quickly (p. 433).

## Edge cases and the role of HR

Performance issues aren't always about skill or will. Personal circumstances, such as a death in the family, financial hardship, or health issues, can affect performance. Two things help: involve HR if you have it, since there are protections for employees dealing with personal hardship and you should know the legal and regulatory requirements; and don't back away from feedback. You can't lower the bar the role requires, but you can adjust responsibilities, such as moving someone to a part-time or less demanding role if the hardship is temporary (p. 433).

If there's no HR team, a retained lawyer or law firm can often provide materials or training on local legal considerations. An experienced HR person has seen many similar situations and can flag risk that varies by country. Once you've identified a low performer, tell HR, but don't hand them the performance management itself: as the manager, you remain responsible for your reports and know their work best (pp. 433 to 434).

## Key principles

**No surprises.** By the time a bigger decision like a formal improvement plan happens, the employee should already have had two or three feedback conversations naming the gap explicitly. Skipping this means starting from behind: rebuilding trust takes longer and remediation is less likely. The exception is a single act so egregious it calls for immediate action; otherwise, don't wait for the formal review cycle to say something (pp. 434 to 435).

**Use documentation to foster clarity and trust.** Put critical feedback in writing. It reduces the chance something gets lost, helps both sides spot patterns faster, and builds trust through a shared record of what was discussed and agreed. Deliver feedback in person, ideally in a regular 1:1, then follow up in writing: a line in the 1:1 doc for a one-off issue, or an email or document for a more serious, patterned conversation, shared with HR if the situation has escalated that far (pp. 435 to 436).

**Show compassion.** Compassionate management is not the same as being nice and withholding feedback, what Kim Scott calls "ruinous empathy" in *Radical Candor*. It means giving honest observations, being clear about the employee's options to course-correct, naming what would be required to meet expectations, and sharing the likeliest outcome, while still handling your other responsibilities. She cites a coach's observation that managers' biggest mistake is spending all their time on problems and not enough fueling top performers (pp. 436 to 437).

**Move deliberately.** Timing matters: aim to resolve a performance issue within three months, sometimes as little as one month depending on seniority and context. Waiting too long invites new variables, such as an employee's own stress compounding the original issue, which then complicates and slows the process further (p. 437).

## Phases of managing low performers

The process is rarely a formal firing; more often it ends with coaching or "managing out," where the employee decides on their own to move on and both sides manage the transition together (pp. 437 to 438). The phases, with rough timelines:

- **Phase 0:** one-off feedback becomes a pattern; form a hypothesis on the likely outcome (under three weeks).
- **Phase 1:** get aligned about the performance challenge (one to three conversations, about two weeks): share the observed pattern, state plainly that expectations aren't being met, and document the discussion.
- **Phase 2:** agree on next steps (one to two conversations, about one week).
- **Phase 3:** create an action plan (one to three months), leading to one of three outcomes: a performance improvement plan that succeeds, a move to another team, or the employee leaving.

### Phase 0: form a hypothesis

Junior, straightforward roles can move through this quickly; a senior manager-of-managers role in a complex job will likely take a few months. Bake in a timeline and hold yourself to it. Forming a hypothesis before starting, even knowing it might be wrong, produces a better process. The three likely outcomes: the employee improves and stays, changes roles or teams, or leaves the company. If asked "do you think I can succeed," don't say a flat no, but be honest if you think it will be hard (pp. 438 to 439).

### Phase 1: get aligned

Deliver feedback the way you would one-off feedback (she points back to the giving-hard-feedback guidance, p. 355), with two additions: state that this is now a pattern, and say explicitly that the person is not meeting the expectations of the role, ideally against an objective source like a role charter. This is the shift from informal coaching to a formal performance designation, and it should never be left implicit (pp. 439 to 440).

Employees typically respond with denial or with relief and readiness to problem-solve.

**Denial** should be a small share of responses, maybe 10 percent, if hiring has gone well. If the person seems to misunderstand, give more concrete examples. If they dispute the facts, steer the conversation toward your perception and observation rather than arguing the facts. The conversation may need multiple sessions; if you learn new information that shifts your assessment, say so, commit to gathering more data within about a week, and either restart the process or debrief honestly about the earlier misread (pp. 440 to 442).

**Relief and readiness to problem-solve** is the more common response. Useful questions: what do you think is going on, do you like this work, is this the right role for you. These surface whether the person already senses the mismatch, needs a specific skill, or should consider a different role altogether (pp. 442 to 443).

**Document the discussion**, ideally by email, stating plainly that the person isn't meeting expectations and outlining agreed next steps, even simple ones like "you'll reflect and we'll meet again next week" (pp. 443 to 444).

### Phase 2: agree on next steps

Ideally both sides align on the path forward by the second conversation; even without full agreement, move to discussing next steps by the third. The three possible next steps are a performance improvement plan, a move to another team, or departure (p. 444).

### Phase 3: create an action plan

**Performance improvement plan (PIP).** If the conversation hasn't produced a quick mutual decision to part ways, the manager drafts a formal PIP for the employee to agree to. It should name the skills or behaviors that need to change, set an end date for evaluation, and define how progress will be measured. Issues tend to fall into three types, each with a different natural timeline: operational or tactical (easiest to measure, but may need a full cycle, such as a sales quarter, to show through); overall skills fit for the role (assessable in one to two months even if full skill-building takes longer); and behavioral or attitudinal (assessable within two to three weeks). The hardest part is defining how you'll measure progress on anything that isn't a clean metric: rely on the what (output and quality) and the how (process, collaboration, communication), and consider peer feedback or another manager's assessment to avoid ending the PIP in a dispute over how to judge it (pp. 445 to 446).

She gives sample talking points for introducing a PIP: name the gap plainly, describe the plan's goals, commit to weekly check-ins during 1:1s, and be direct that continued underperformance could lead to termination (pp. 446 to 447).

Most PIPs where the employee never accepted the feedback end in departure, by her account about 99 percent of the time; the PIP in that case still serves as documentation of the gap. Where the employee accepts the gap and is motivated, tactical skill gaps are more fixable than behavioral ones, though she has seen even those succeed. Watch degree of difficulty, motivation, and your own hypothesis throughout, since some employees narrowly pass a PIP only to land back in the same situation months later (p. 448).

**Move roles or teams.** Sometimes someone is a genuinely good worker who is wrong for their current role. Hypothesis-based coaching can help identify a better-fit role and how to pursue it inside the company (p. 448).

Departure itself, including managing out, firing, and layoffs, is covered in the next section of the chapter.

## Templates

**Performance improvement documentation (pp. 474 to 477).** Four email templates for the stages of a PIP process: initial documentation of feedback after a 1:1 or a "partially meets expectations" review; a progress update; a message confirming the employee is back on track; and a message for insufficient progress after a reasonable period, which sets up a realistic conversation about whether the role is still viable.

**Performance improvement plan template (pp. 477 to 481).** A signed, standalone document, typically running four to six weeks, that should never be the employee's first exposure to the feedback; several documented conversations should precede it. Sections: role expectations (three to five key duties tied to the areas needing improvement); areas for improvement (each with the expected standard, the gap and its impact, and dated examples); improvement goals and targets (goal, time frame, and deliverable per area); milestones with deadlines; resources available, including HR contact; and progress checks and evaluation, stating the check-in cadence, the end date, that the PIP can be ended early, and that it doesn't change at-will employment. It closes with employee and manager signature lines.

**Managing out checklist (pp. 481 to 482).** Provide feedback on the performance issue; document it; run a formal PIP process if relevant; check local employment regulations; inform your manager and HR partner; hold the coaching-out conversation; agree on the timeline for leaving and the communication plan; and announce the departure to the team, with transition plan details if the role needs immediate coverage.
