# 5.3 Creating a culture of informal feedback: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 5, pp. 395 to 398. Full text: `sources/extraction/build/s12/book.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## The gap companies leave

Companies often invest in formal review processes but neglect their culture of informal feedback: the one-off advice and observations colleagues and reports offer each other in day-to-day work (p. 395).

Her Stripe example cuts both ways. When she joined, the culture of commenting on each other's work was strong, almost too strong: direct feedback lived in written comments and Slack messages, which could feel abrupt and intimidating to new people. As the company scaled, both the volume of work a person can absorb and the time others have to comment on it declined. One practice persisted and expresses the "users first" principle: testing content with a small group before sending it to a wide audience, the whole company, or externally. The cofounders (CEO and president) do this the most, which sets the example from the top and signals humility, collaboration, and respect for others' time (pp. 395 to 396).

What Stripe did less well: bidirectional, informal feedback flowing upward and sideways without being asked for. She attributes this to fewer examples set by leaders, rapid growth, new hires lacking confidence to speak up, and possibly an emphasis on generosity and kindness that some don't associate with feedback. Her view: feedback is a kindness, even if not everyone experiences it that way (pp. 396).

Her broader point: companies are well served by examining their feedback culture, and if it's found wanting, leaders are the ones who have to steer the change. That investment matters because a company without a strong informal feedback muscle makes the formal review process feel destabilizing: people go into it not knowing where they stand (p. 396).

She quotes Don Hall, Hallmark's executive chairman and former CEO: brutal honesty requires an environment that isn't itself brutal (p. 396).

## Building the culture: modeling

Model the behavior in team meetings and 1:1s. "Praise publicly and criticize privately" remains a good general rule, especially for individuals, though she draws a distinction for team-level feedback: feedback about the team's performance as a whole can be data-driven and shared honestly and publicly, an honest assessment of how a quarter or project went, focused on what was learned and what to change, builds a culture of learning and open feedback (pp. 396 to 397).

Personally soliciting and welcoming feedback matters most when setting the tone of a working relationship: a newly forming team, or a newly managed employee. Example: after trying a new meeting format, set the expectation up front that feedback on it is wanted. If the team already feels safe sharing, ask publicly, going around the room; if not yet, follow up privately with individuals (p. 397).

## Asking for feedback

Since the best way to build a feedback culture is to ask for feedback yourself, her pointers (pp. 397 to 398):

- **Ask on different occasions and through different forums.** In 1:1s, during or after meetings, over email, in work sessions. Phrase the ask as a request for something specific to improve, not a generic check-in: "what could I have done differently in that meeting?" rather than "how do you think that meeting went?"; "what do you think I can do to make this project more successful?" rather than "is there anything else on your mind?"
- **Thank the person, don't explain yourself.** Explaining the choices you made in response to feedback risks appearing defensive or signaling the feedback wasn't welcome.
- **Normalize the practice.** If you received feedback privately, consider mentioning it in the next team meeting, to set the norm that giving and receiving feedback is welcome.
- **Let the feedback sit.** Resist the urge to problem-solve or explain in the moment, even when well-intentioned, it can read as defensive. Repeat the feedback back to confirm you heard it accurately, thank the person, and return later if you want to work the problem together.
- **Follow up.** At a later point, tell the person whether and how you acted on their feedback. Sometimes the answer is that it was valid but not prioritized; either way, the person needs to know they were heard.

She quotes an unnamed executive practice: soliciting feedback from the team in front of others so the behavior is modeled, paired with mandatory reading of *The Speed of Trust* by Stephen Covey, on the premise that nothing is personal and everything should be open and transparent (p. 398).
