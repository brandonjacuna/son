### 1.1 "Why Tipping Gives You the Ick" (2026-08-07)

**Account.**
- **Opening:** a long quotation from Danny Meyer's *Setting the Table* defining "hospitalitarians": people for whom caring for others is a selfish act, whose energy is rarely depleted.
- **Her case against the book:**
  - It sold a service that "doesn't consider itself labor", built on "unsustainable people-pleasing".
  - Meyer acknowledges emotional labor but "doesn't give it a line on the P&L".
  - When a hospitalitarian runs out, the implied fix is to replace them.
- **Care as unpaid work:** she links the expectation of free care to the unpaid emotional labor assigned to mothers and women. Core line: "we want to be cared for and caring has a cost."
- **What causes the "ick":** not the service but "the monetization of care". Tipping makes the payer admit they want to be cared for, and that the care is labor.
- **Tipping as a wage:**
  - Tipping is an "imperfect and clumsy" payment for emotional labor, tied to the price of the food, and it "performs itself as a gift, not a wage".
  - Other professionals are paid whatever the outcome. The server's pay is set afterward, at the receiver's discretion.
- **Direct rebuttal to Meyer:** "No, empathy is finite."
- **Close:** hopeful ("it sucks but we can make it better").
- **Series and sources:** the post is part one of a three-part series on tipping. Part two, "The Service was Bad", covers the history of tipping and its power dynamics. She links Viviana Zelizer, *The Purchase of Intimacy* (Princeton UP).

**What it adds to the Meyer and Guidara lens.** Meyer and Guidara treat care as a disposition you hire for and a gift you give. Catubig treats it as finite, costly labor: either someone pays for it or someone absorbs the cost. (My description of Guidara's lens comes from general knowledge; I did not re-read him this pass, so it is unverified.)

**Where it shapes Sŏn training.**
- **Tone of modules:** never write care as a personality trait a learner has or lacks. Teach it as a craft that has a cost.
  - This fits Brandon's group 7 answer: hire capable people, and development is on us.
  - It also fits BG 03, where Nunchi is framed as built through mastery, not temperament.
- **Emotional labor:** name it as labor in ORI and CUL content. Teach recovery as part of the job, not a private matter. This maps onto WP Part II "The cooling system" (cite it, do not restate it) and BG 01 Company non-negotiables.
- **Compensation:** outside training scope, and Brandon's call. WP Part II "How the team is paid" and BG 15 (Arrival) already take positions that touch tipping. A module can cite them but never explain how pay works. Any pay figure is a `fact.*` binding.
- **Read next:** part two of the series, before any module cites part one.

### 1.2 "The Grass is Greener... Where You Ethically Farm It" (2026-07-29)

**Account.** She profiles three restaurants that are "making our tiny worlds more beautiful, more just."

1. **Ambriza Social Mexican Kitchen, Houston (Julio and Amber Garcia).** From a Welcome Conference talk she heard.
   - Julio came to Houston young from Mexico. He rose from dishwasher to director of operations under an owner who abused him for years.
   - After years of failing to open his own place, the same owner called to offer him a restaurant. Julio bought it on terms far beyond his savings and paid it off far ahead of schedule.
   - She asks the moral question directly: could you let go of resentment, or "take unethically-made money", to reach your dream?
   - The restaurant now provides "education, financial backing and path for their most talented individuals to own their own restaurant."
   - Her reading: ownership takes hard work *and* the luck of financial privilege. She quotes Julio: "Why not serve the people around us?"
   - Thesis: "Progress doesn't have to be the invention of something new. It can be dismantling the barrier of entry."
2. **Pidgin Collective, Seattle (Seth and Zach Pacleb, Erica Reich, Spencer Rossman).** A Pan-Asian restaurant and wine shop owned by its workers.
   - Workers join by meeting a work threshold and a buy-in that can be paid in time, followed by a vote. Year-end surplus is divided among the worker-owners.
   - She admits she was afraid to write it. She has worked for investor-backed groups whose "We're a family" promises she distrusts. She says her fear "will never create the future", but their optimism might.
3. **Joe Beef, Montreal (David McMillan).**
   - She sets it in a mid-2000s era of excess (she names The Fat Duck, *Kitchen Confidential* and the 50 Best list) that normalized "Hurt People, abuse and mental breakdowns."
   - McMillan went to rehab and therapy. His staff stopped drinking at work and started going to bed early: "He changed, his leadership changed, and his restaurant changed."
   - He later relapsed during the pandemic and left.
   - Her lesson: "restaurants are always the reflection of the people inside it." Who is in leadership "is more important than the choices of any Board of Directors."

She closes with an admission of her own. She was "both a victim and a perpetrator" of a toxic culture. She wishes she had stood up to every bully and "took up more space in the name of diversity." She quotes Nate Ready of Hiyu: "Pick a problem and then try to solve it."

**What it adds.** Meyer and Guidara place excellence in the moment with the customer. Catubig places it in the path a worker can walk (all the way to ownership) and in the example a leader sets, relapse included. It is the only source on the list that treats a leader's own recovery as curriculum.

**Where it shapes Sŏn training.**
- **Advancement:** the skill tree and the on deck reserve should visibly lower barriers to entry, with clear, published paths and no gatekeeping by favor.
  - This lines up with WP Part I "Individual progression: the inverted web" and Part II "Training infrastructure".
  - Ownership or equity structures are not a training decision: founder-gated.
- **Leadership modules (LEA):** teach leaders that their habits become the house's habits. Frame it as modeling, not policing. Her victim-and-perpetrator admission is a model for how a leader can own past harm while teaching.
- **Tone:** avoid the "we're a family" register, which Catubig reads as a warning sign. This matches BG 06 and 11 (declarative, no performed conviction).

### 1.3 "Hey... Are You Okay? The Ever-Expansion of the Modern Wine Woman" (2026-08-15)

**Account.**
- **The baseline:** she forgot her eyeliner one morning, and colleagues asked all day whether she was okay. She had broken her "baseline": "the narrow, legible version of someone built from repeated exposure."
  - At work the question has a subtext: will you get your state in check, or is this going to be a problem?
  - Social media trades complexity for hooks, and the baseline "becomes a cage."
- **Suppression as protection:** using Freud's Madonna-Whore complex, she argues that women in wine and business take a third option, adopting masculine traits for protection. "An emotional person becomes a liability; a contained person is a productive tool."
- **Reading faces:** she argues, without citing a study, that short-form video has weakened our ability to read complex facial expressions.
- **Her own path:**
  - She has had a long career in hospitality. She is a Filipina woman raised in Connecticut who has navigated white patriarchy since early childhood.
  - She has succeeded by being "exceedingly formal" and through "a convincing performance of harmlessness": good but not too good, funny but not too funny, standing up for herself but not too much. "There is no safety in excellence."
  - She holds that fine dining is a relative haven for non-white workers because it demands decorum, while casual settings encourage bad behavior.
- **Two incidents:**
  - At a fast-casual job, a customer picked her up and carried her out the door because she was "so small."
  - A customer told her he "would like a white man" to talk to about wine.
- "Joy itself is an act of resistance."
- **The turn:** the persona that got her this far will not get her further. Her liberation lies in complexity and creativity.
- **Her ask:** give others the curiosity you would want. Assume your biases shape what you read in a face. "Ask a better question than '... Are you okay?'"

**What it adds.** Meyer and Guidara teach how to read the customer. Catubig asks who reads the worker, and warns that reading people through a flattened baseline is a failure of skill. She also names mistreatment that is racial and gendered, which neither book addresses.

**Where it shapes Sŏn training.**
- **Customer mistreatment:**
  - Her two cases (being physically handled, and a customer asking for a different expert because of race and gender) are exactly the scenarios a mistreatment module has to cover.
  - The house, not the individual, owns the response. BG 15 (floor authority: escalation triggers defined before service) supports treating this as a call the system makes in advance.
  - Whether Sŏn honors a request, on those grounds, to swap the server or sommelier is **founder-gated** and HR-gated. Bind it as `founder.mistreatment_policy` and `workflow.escalation.mistreatment`.
  - A mirror case worth noting: BG 05 persona Mia fails when the floor explains her own food to her. Both are about assumptions drawn from appearance.
- **Feedback and critique:**
  - Brandon wants anyone to be able to critique anyone (group 4). Catubig shows that speaking up does not cost everyone the same: some team members hold back, standing up for themselves "not too much."
  - Critique has to be structural: a protocol and a shared language, taught on both sides (giving and receiving), with the TBRI seat reviewing. Confidence should not be the price of entry.
- **Emotional labor and the "are you okay" check-in:** train leads to ask better questions than a compliance check. This pairs with WP Part II "The cooling system".
- **Perception (Nunchi) turned inward:** the reading skill BG 03 asks the floor to use on customers also applies to teammates. A see-it drill on reading a teammate without flattening them is a candidate.
- **Identity:** modules should not demand one legible persona. BG 06's "present without performing" can be taught as relief from performing, not as a new performance.

## 2. Gastrophysics and flavor science

"Psychotaste" (Brandon's term) is not an established term: a literature search on 2026-09-28 found no use of it. Keep it as the house's umbrella label, and never present it as a field. The actual fields are:
- gastrophysics: Charles Spence, Oxford Crossmodal Research Laboratory; *Gastrophysics: The New Science of Eating*, 2017
- neurogastronomy: Gordon Shepherd, *Neurogastronomy*, Columbia UP, 2012
- crossmodal correspondence research
- sensory science

**Who gets what.**
- **Servers:** flavor is built from smell, sight, sound and expectation; how you describe something shapes the experience of it; frame things honestly; drop the myths. This fits "Know it" and "See it" in the modality library (tasting with recall; contrast pairs).
- **Hosts:** expectation starts at arrival. BG 13 (nine-beat sequence) and BG 15 (arrival) are the house's own design; cite them. Teach, as a principle and never as manipulation, that first impressions set the frame the palate tastes through.
- **Beverage team:** the color and price studies, judge inconsistency, retronasal tasting technique, and humility about vessel shape. Anything that touches the sound system defers to BG 14.
