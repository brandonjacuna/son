# Session 7: Onboarding, hiring mistakes, and the Chapter 3 exercises

This page is the Sŏn answer to the Onboarding and Hiring mistakes sections of Chapter 3 (Book p.216 to 227) and to the Chapter 3 exercises and templates (Book p.228 to 259), with the Chapter 3 workbook's remaining material (Workbook p.78 to 88) read for anything the book version lacks. It is written to be run from. A person who has not read the book can read this page and know what happens to someone from the day they accept a seat to the day their ninety-day plan closes: what arrives in their hands and through which tool, who stands beside them, what they are taught and in what order, what is read and what is written down, when they first hold a table or a station alone, and what the house refuses to conclude about them along the way.

Every position on this page is numbered from P1 and carries exactly one mark. (Recommended) is the team's position, ready for founder ratification. (Founder-gated) requires input the team cannot supply. (Chef-gated) is left open for the chef partner. (Team-filled) is populated once the team exists. Nothing on this page is final. A recommended position is a recommendation until Brandon ratifies it, and a founder-gated position is argued to the ceiling one founder can reach and then marked for what Dominic, the chef, or the team must close.

Vocabulary follows the ledger and Sessions 4 to 6. "Operating system," unqualified, is the human system; the software is "the stack" or "the OS surface." The customer is the customer, in every module, every module's narration, and every sentence a new person is taught. Service periods are named by time of day. A "seat" is a node in the web with a domain, a strand, and documented competency requirements; the "seat sheet" is the sixteen-field artifact that opens it (S5 P12), and its field 6 carries the entry bar and the first unlock, field 7 what the house develops, field 10 the decision-rights range, field 11 the shift shape, and field 14 the languages. "The four dimensions" are the scorecard's dimensions Session 6 named and Session 13 inherits (S6 P33). "The domain lead" is the lead of the domain a seat sits in, named by seat, holding the functions Session 5 stated and not the functions it stated beside them (S5 P33). "Service-ready" is defined on this page (section 6.6) and nowhere before it.

Financials are not a context source for this program. Wage rates, training pay, mentor pay, and the pay for authoring a module are the same rule: none is stated, estimated, or implied, and where a mechanism needs one the page says the figure is not a context source and routes the gap to Session 13. Her chapter's one figure in this range, a rejected-candidate survey result on page 226, does not enter this page.

The stack is bought and this page builds into it (carryover `86ajgmm86`): Greenhouse is the applicant system; Rippling is the single sign-on, human-resources, and payroll layer; Trainual, Synthesia, and Loom are the training production stack; Google Workspace is the floor team's backbone. The service-period count is a parameter, said once. Onboarding is written for dinner, which is the first and for now the only configuration under a ratified charter. ## 1. Her ask, and what Sŏn already holds

### 1.1 Her ask

Her onboarding section opens on the moment of arrival: a new person is "on high alert for context and opportunities to learn," and a company that only has them "show up, fill out some paperwork, get set up on your laptop, and start working" has missed "a huge opportunity to start inculcating new hires into the company and the culture" (Book p.216). The curriculum she asks for carries three things in order: "The 'why' of the company, including the mission and the story behind it"; "The 'what' of the business, including current priorities and goals"; "The 'how' of company behaviors, including how people work together and the operating principles" (Book p.216). She asks that "company leadership should take part in onboarding new hires," that "Stories, not bullet points, are what really get a message across," and that the curriculum be "the fastest possible ramp to success" (Book p.216). Her own house runs "a weeklong core onboarding curriculum for every new hire, no matter their level," with team programs alongside, and the manager translates "the company operating system to the team level" in a first 1:1 or a one- to two-hour session (Book p.216).

The one voice in her section from a kitchen says what this page has to answer for a cook: "people just get thrown onto the job. I think this is the wrong way to do it. Training is very important, and understanding all the layers of the company is very important. If you're getting hired as a cook, it's not just about the kitchen," and "when you train someone, it's not like, 'Now you're trained.' No. You always have to be on top of it. It's a continuation of things" (Book p.216 to 217, Dominique Crenn).

Her prep rule: "all prep work should be done asynchronously before the onboarding program starts," paperwork and equipment included (Book p.217). Her nine outcomes for a person emerging from onboarding: a community of other new hires; leadership's vision and the principles; the business and its products; the users' perspective; the operating structures and current goals; a picture of the organization; key processes such as compliance; where the team fits; and "An ability to find internal resources to self-educate and seek assistance" (Book p.217).

Internal mobility: a survey at every team change, five questions, read over time for "bad mobility" (Book p.217 to 218); and manager-to-manager transitions, with a guide in the appendix (Book p.218; p.252 to 253). New hire onboarding: her "Working with Claire" document, the first meeting devoted to mutual expectations across six headings (onboarding, operating approach, management style, communication preferences, initial priorities, the upcoming career conversation), and the invitation to the new person to write their own (Book p.218 to 219). New leader onboarding: standardize, then add; the New Leader Experience with welcome emails, prescheduled meetings and pre-reads, a leadership assessment, a coach, first-month actions, and a ninety-day 360 (Book p.219); Table 5's six objectives and tactics (Book p.220); the daily or every-other-day standup for critical roles, and the "narrow" path between listening and acting (Book p.221); and the direct report's sidebar in nine parts, from "Commit to their success" to "Share stories and histories" (Book p.221 to 224).

Hiring mistakes: most surface "a few months into the person's tenure" as performance, but some right away, even before the start date; four example signals; "anything that violates your employee handbook"; get minor feedback to the person "quickly and strongly"; "The main thing is to do something," rescind or terminate swiftly, a short note to the team (Book p.224 to 225). Closing the loop: a retrospective by the hiring manager; ATS data on time in process, funnel performance, and interviewer stats; engagement and pulse survey prompts; marrying hiring data with performance data at milestones; surveying the rejected (Book p.225 to 226). Her closing line: "Hiring is expensive, but talent is everything" (Book p.227).

The exercises appendix reproduces the workbook's Chapter 3 templates (Book p.228 to 259). Four of the eight are Session 6's and are answered there: the interview framework and rubric, the sample questions, the written exercise, and the candidate review framework (Workbook p.43 to 77; S6 P17 to P55). This page confirmed against the book's versions (Book p.229 to 251) that nothing there is absent from Session 6, and it answers them by reference (section 15). The four that are this page's: the Manager Transitions Guide (Book p.252 to 253; Workbook p.78), Working with Claire (Book p.253 to 257; Workbook p.80 to 83), the Working with Me template (Book p.257; Workbook p.84), and the New Leader Experience with its schedule (Book p.257 to 259; Workbook p.86 to 88). ## 2. What onboarding is for at Sŏn, what it may conclude, and what it refuses to conclude

### 2.1 The intended behavior, stated so it can be tested

The People Systems Designer's first move is to state, in behavioral terms, what the system is supposed to make people do, because a system with no statable intended behavior cannot be evaluated. Her section states an intent, "the fastest possible ramp to success" (Book p.216), and the record states a stronger one, "early success is planned rather than hoped for" (WP p.18). Neither is behavioral. This page states the behavior, and every mechanism after this section exists to produce it or is cut.

At the close of the ninety-day plan, a person in an hourly edge seat: holds their seat solo on the schedule at service tempo, reproducing the seat's entry procedures without a mentor beside them; states their decision-rights range and its escalation triggers unprompted, in their own words, and has used the range at least once inside it; speaks of the customer as the customer in every sentence, spoken and written, and of a dish with felt knowledge of the dishes that exist; has opened the learning platform and moved on it without being asked, so the invisible advocate has a record to read; has told the house, at six check-ins, what was in the way, and can say what the building did about it; and knows the seat after theirs and what it requires, from their own account on the team home, without asking anyone.

That is the whole of it. Onboarding is not for producing a person who feels welcomed, which is a mood, or one who has "been onboarded," which is a document. It is for producing the six behaviors above, and the Realist's addition is that the first of them has to hold on a Friday with a full book, not on the calm Tuesday the sequence was designed on.

### 2.2 What onboarding may conclude

Four things, and each has a record.

Whether the person is service-ready in the seat, per section 6.6: the entry procedures reproduced in a paired service and read on the practical's rows by an assessor who is not the mentor (S6 I4), the range and triggers stated, the language constant held, and the felt-knowledge block passed for the dishes that exist. The record is the readiness form (I7) and the first solo's date on the schedule.

Whether each milestone of the ninety-day plan reached its state, and on what date. The record is the plan block on the person page, written by the stack for the milestones it can see (modules, the paired services on the schedule, the form's date) and by the lead at the check-in for the rest (section 7).

What the building supplied and did not supply of what the person said they needed, which is dimension 4 read on the building at six check-ins (S6 P33). The record is the check-in lines.

What in the house's training infrastructure did not hold for this person: a module missing in their language, a mentor absent, a paired service that ran short, a felt-knowledge block that could not be passed because the dishes had not been tasted. The record is the training-infrastructure line at the leads' review (S5 12.6's trigger), and it is read as a load on the infrastructure, never on the person.

### 2.3 What onboarding refuses to conclude

A verdict on the person. The review is not a verdict (WP p.19) and the check-in refuses one (S4 P15); the plan cannot be a verdict on the way to a review that is not.

A level, a placement, or a pay outcome. Everyone enters at the seat's entry point (S6 P62) and the architecture is Session 13's; the plan's close changes nothing about pay and says nothing about it.

Nunchi or Jeong. Neither is a ninety-day expectation (ledger; S5 P6). Nunchi is "Developed through mastery, exercised through judgment" (Canon v3.0, p.15), and ninety days is the beginning of mastery; Jeong "requires time, repetition, and memory" (Canon v1.0, section 01) and the plan is too short to have accumulated any. A plan that named either as a milestone would be scoring at ninety days what recruiting refused to score at entry.

Month-six performance, cross-strand fluency, or a person's ceiling. The plan reads the entry horizon of field 6 and nothing past it.

Whether a hire was a mistake. The plan reads states; where a hire that does not hold is read, and what is concluded from it, is Session 14's (S6 18.6; section 13). The plan may say a milestone did not reach its state and by which link. It may not say what that means about the person.

Whether the person "fits." There is no such dimension at hiring (S6 6.2) and none at onboarding.

Anything about the person that the person has not heard. The person page is written in the room and read back (S4 P18), and the plan block is part of the page.

**P4 (Recommended).** Onboarding's intended behavior is the six behaviors of 2.1 at the close of the ninety-day plan. It may conclude service-readiness per seat with a readiness form, the state and date of each plan milestone, what the building supplied against what the person said they needed, and what in the training infrastructure did not hold. It refuses to conclude a verdict, a level or pay outcome, nunchi or Jeong, month-six performance or a ceiling, whether a hire was a mistake, fit, or anything the person has not heard.

### 2.4 Who runs it, and the departure from her manager

Her onboarding has one owner, the manager, who understands the curriculum, adds team content, holds the first 1:1, sets expectations, and later runs the career conversation (Book p.216 to 219). At Sŏn nothing is assigned, reviewed, or escalated by a person standing above another person (ledger), so her manager's functions are distributed across five runners, each with a stated range, and no runner holds all of them.

The domain lead, by seat. Holds the person's check-in and reads and writes the person page there, and only there (S4 P20). Names the mentor from the pool (section 11). Places the paired services and the first solo on the schedule, the first solo only on a passed readiness form (section 6.5). Reads the training-infrastructure line to the leads' review. Does not teach the modules, does not run the readiness read, does not write a verdict, does not assign tasks, does not hold advancement (S5 P33). For floor seats on the outward-facing strand the domain lead is the Maitre d, who owns "Team development, training, and the invisible advocate function" (WP p.10); for the inward-facing floor strand, and for runner and host seats, the lead is held (S5 P64; `86akh7r10`) and every entry on this page that names the domain lead for those seats is incomplete until it closes; for beverage the Head of Beverage; for the kitchen, chef-gated.

The mentor. A proven cultural steward from the person's strand, named by the lead from the pool, paid as shift, beside the person at the live session and the paired services, answering questions, carrying the house's ways and the standard, and writing nothing on the person page (section 11).

The assessor. A trained assessor from the seat's strand who is not the mentor and does not decide, scoring the readiness read on the practical's rows (S6 I4; S5 P41). The same separation hiring uses: the person who taught is not the person who reads.

The founder, for the why. Pre-opening and through dinner's first quarter, a founder delivers the why block of orientation in person (section 5.4). After that, the holder of the concept's voice internally, which is an open question the ledger carries (`86akh2qq0`; spec `86akh3u2y`).

The stack. ### 5.5 The blocking dependency, argued

## 6. The first shift, the first week, the first ninety days

### 6.1 The sequence, and why it is the interviewer's sequence

Session 6 fixed the training form for interviewers: modules read, a live session, an observed stage, a paired stage read side by side, then solo, all on scheduled shift, with the record written by the stack (S6 P38). The brief asks whether operational training uses the same sequence and why.

It does, and the reason is not economy of design. The sequence is the one canon's training requirement produces when it is taken seriously: "training architecture that progresses from procedure mastery to autonomous judgment" (Canon v1.0, section 01). Modules carry the procedure as knowledge; the live session carries it as demonstration; the observed service carries it as the procedure seen at tempo; the paired service carries it as the procedure done at tempo with a person beside you; the solo is the procedure done alone, and judgment begins there. Her section's own kitchen voice describes the industry's alternative, "thrown onto the job" (Book p.216), and the record describes it as "Shadow someone for two shifts, catch fragments because they are busy, get thrown in before ready" (WP p.19). Two shifts of shadowing is the observed step with the other four missing. The sequence is what the record's paragraph is asking for, and the interviewer training is the first place the house wrote it down.

Two things differ from the interviewer's sequence, and they are stated. The paired step, for a floor or kitchen seat, is not one paired service but as many as the seat's entry procedures need, and the count is on the seat sheet's training plan (item 5) and not a house constant, because a runner's entry procedures and a server's are not the same length. And the solo step is gated by the readiness read (section 6.5), scored by an assessor, where the interviewer's solo is gated by the paired read of two scorecards; the difference is that an interviewer's solo produces a scorecard the Maitre d reads afterward, and a server's solo produces a customer's evening, which no one reads afterward.

The steps, defined once:

Modules read. The seat's first module set (section 8), on Trainual, taken on scheduled shift in the check-in's unit of time per module, self-paced within the first week, in the person's language. Modules are read before the live session and never in place of it.

The live session. The mentor walks the section or station with the person, outside service, in the building: the setup, the sequence, the positions, the surfaces the seat uses on the stack, the close. For a floor seat this is the section's setup and the step-back positions walked on the floor with no customer in the room; for a kitchen station, chef-gated in every specific; for the bar, the well. One block, on shift, in the pre-service window of a service the person then observes, or in a training service.

The observed service. The person is on the schedule beside the mentor for a service, carrying nothing: not a table, not a station, not a runner's tray. They attend the brief and do not speak at it, they stand in the step-back coverage position beside the mentor, they watch the pass, and they attend the close and watch the capture. The customer is never told a person is observing. One observed service for an edge seat; more where the seat sheet says so.

The paired services. The person works the section or station with the mentor or the seat holder beside them, carrying what the mentor hands them, on services the leads' review has not flagged short, placed by the stack with the mentor as a teaching body (S6 P31's rule for practicals, applied). The count is the seat sheet's. The person never carries a recovery, a generosity decision, or a David flag alone during a paired service; those are the seat holder's range until the solo (S5 P41's rule for candidates, applied through the paired step).

The readiness read. In the last paired service, or a separate one, an assessor who is not the mentor scores the seat's entry procedures on the practical's rows (S6 I4), the two people rows, the range stated, the language constant, and the felt-knowledge block, and submits the readiness form (I7). Section 6.5.

The solo. The first service the person holds a section or station without the mentor beside them, placed on the schedule by the domain lead only on a passed form, never a new period's first service, never dinner's first service (P10), never a service the leads' review flagged short. The mentor is in the building, on their own section, and reachable. The four-week load read starts here.

**P18 (Recommended).** Operational onboarding uses the interviewer training's sequence, modules read, a live session, an observed service, paired services, then solo, because it is the sequence canon's procedure-to-judgment requirement produces and the record's sink-or-swim paragraph describes by its absence. Two differences: the paired count is the seat sheet's, and the solo is gated by an assessor's readiness form.

### 8.3 What a module is, under canon

A module is the unit the platform teaches, and canon governs every word in it because a new team member is taught in the house's voice and will speak in it. The standard (I11) is a specification and not a template.

A module must contain: the procedure it teaches, as a sequence of what is done, in what order, with what, and what it looks like when done; the seat and the field 6 row it serves; the one thing the procedure is for, said as a decision and not an outcome, in the way canon's menu copy "names what is present, how it is present, and the role of its presence" (Canon v1.0, section 09); a Loom recording of a real person doing it, where the procedure is physical; the range that touches it, if any, with a trigger named; the language it is in, the author, the approver, and the version; and how mastery of it is demonstrated, which is the readiness row it feeds.

A module must do: exist in every language the seat's field 14 names before the seat's first start date; take no longer than the check-in's unit of time to complete on shift; be readable on a phone; be revised by versioned decision when the floor contradicts it (the week-four line, section 7.4); carry the language constant in every sentence; pass the six binary tests sentence by sentence (Canon v1.0, section 09; Canon v3.0, p.54), because a module that could appear unchanged in another house's training is not teaching this house.

A module must refuse: a script for a customer interaction, because canon prohibits "service delivered by script rather than felt knowledge" (Canon v1.0, section 01) and "service protocols built entirely on scripted delivery" (Canon v1.0, section 01), so a module teaches the arrival's one line, "We have you" (Canon v1.0, section 08), as the house's line and teaches nothing else as lines; a procedure with no decision inside it where the seat holds one, which is "training systems that measure procedure execution without building toward judgment" (Canon v1.0, section 01); a description of nunchi or Jeong as a skill to execute, since neither is executed; a translation, gloss, footnote, or italic definition of a Korean term (Canon v1.0, section 04), which is the finding in 19.6 and is held; canon's forbidden word for the customer; a daypart code name; a figure or a rate; a forbidden-lexicon word (Canon v1.0, section 09); "welcome" or "enjoy" in the written module, which are "acceptable as in-person service language only" (Canon v1.0, section 09), so a module may say that the Maitre d at the steps says "We have you" and may not write "welcome the customer"; a synthesized presenter (P32); a recording with a customer in it; a quiz that scores a person, since the readiness read is the read; and a module that teaches a competency the seat sheet's field 7 says the house develops as if it were required at entry.

Authorship and approval. "Any qualified team member can create training modules" (WP p.19), and "qualified" is defined here as holding the competency the module teaches, validated by the platform's own record or the readiness form, in the seat the module serves. The domain lead approves a module for their domain against the standard; the chef partner for the kitchen; canon's owner for any module that carries brand facts, the arrival line, the register, or the uniform, until the concept's voice holder is named (`86akh2qq0`); and the Operations Lead for any module carrying a compliance or food-safety procedure, with counsel where the law draws a line. Approval is against the standard and not against taste, and a module refused names the standard's item. Payment for authorship is the record's commitment and Session 13's mechanism; this page states no figure and files the coupling (section 19, finding 19.1, and the carryover).

**P33 (Recommended).** A module is specified as in I11: what it must contain, do, and refuse, with canon on every word, no script beyond the house's one arrival line, no synthesized presenter, no scoring quiz, and no competency taught as required that field 7 says is developed. "Qualified" to author means holding the validated competency; approval is the domain lead's against the standard, the chef's for the kitchen, canon's owner's for brand-carrying content, and the Operations Lead's with counsel for compliance. Authorship pay is Session 13's.

## 9. Felt knowledge and the menu: the training sequence against the chef chain

Canon requires that "service speaks to a dish with felt knowledge, not recited description" and that "the Executive Chef can articulate what each dish is reaching for, not just what it tastes like" (Canon v1.0, section 01). The strand definition does not carry felt knowledge (S6 18.5), the hiring read scored only the capacity for it (S6 P18), and the menu is chef-gated and does not exist (S5 P44). Carryover `86akh9tap` gives this page the training plan's sequence and asks it to state the interval the sequence needs as a gate condition without inventing a figure the record does not hold.

What felt knowledge is, operationally. Not a description memorized; canon forbids exactly that. It is a person who has eaten the dish, knows what decision produced it in the chef's own account, and can say one true thing about it in their own words when a customer asks and nothing when a customer does not. Mia "cannot receive unsolicited instruction" and "To explain what she already knows is an insult" (Canon v3.0, p.22), so felt knowledge includes knowing when to say nothing, which is a judgment the readiness read cannot score and the plan does not try to. The readiness read's felt-knowledge row scores three things only: the person has tasted every dish on the current menu; the person can state, for a dish named at random, what it is and the decision that produced it, in the chef's terms and their own words, with no forbidden word and no gloss of a Korean term; and the person does not describe a dish in a category word ("savory," "rich," "complex" are named placeholders, Canon v1.0, section 01).

The sequence, against the chain. The chain runs ruling, chef seated, canon's Session 1B, menu, kitchen seat sheets, kitchen practicals, kitchen hires, their training, dinner's gate (S6 P15). Felt knowledge for the floor hangs off the fourth link and needs, after it and before the gate: the chef's per-dish account written, one entry per dish, what it is reaching for and the decision that produced it, in the chef's words, which is the felt-knowledge block's source and the menu module's content (chef-gated); the dishes cooked and tasted by the whole floor cohort in training services, each dish at least once by every person who will serve it, with the chef or a station lead present to say the account aloud; the felt-knowledge block built from the account on Trainual, in the floor's languages; and the readiness reads' felt-knowledge rows passed, which cannot happen before the tastings. The kitchen's own felt knowledge is the kitchen's practicals and training and is chef-gated whole.

The gate condition, stated without a figure. No floor readiness form may read ready on its felt-knowledge row before the cohort has tasted every dish on the fixed menu in a training service after the menu was fixed, with the chef's account given. The interval the sequence needs is therefore measured in training services, not weeks: at least as many training services after the menu is fixed as it takes to cook every dish for the cohort at least once and to run every floor cohort member's readiness read after that, with the paired-service count the sheets require. How many training services that is depends on the menu's length, the kitchen's readiness to cook it at cohort volume, and the cohort's size, none of which this page holds, and it is chef-gated with the founders holding the calendar. A menu that changes between the tastings and dinner's gate re-opens the row for the changed dishes and is a chef-gated calendar event. A menu fixed so late that the training services cannot carry the tastings is the record's own sequence failing, and dinner's gate does not open around it, because a floor without felt knowledge is a floor "delivered by script," which canon forbids at Tier 1.

The steady state. Every menu change, by versioned decision, adds the changed dishes to the tasting record: each server tastes each new dish, with the account, before serving it, in the pre-service window on shift, and the stack records it against the person's page; a server who has not tasted a dish does not carry a table that orders it alone, which is the seat holder's or the mentor's until they have. That is the record's "It is a continuation of things" (Book p.216, Crenn) as a mechanism, and its bandwidth cost is a tasting in a window already funded.

**P37 (Recommended).** The felt-knowledge row on the readiness form scores that every current dish has been tasted, that a dish named at random can be stated in the chef's terms and the person's words with no placeholder and no gloss, and nothing else. The sequence hangs off the menu link of the chain: the chef's per-dish account, the cohort's tastings in training services after the menu is fixed, the block built from the account, then the rows. In service, every menu change adds tastings before the dish is served alone.

**P38 (Chef-gated).** The per-dish account in the chef's own words, the number of training services needed to cook every dish for the cohort at least once after the menu is fixed, the kitchen's own felt knowledge and training, and any menu change between the tastings and dinner's gate. The gate condition stands without a figure: no floor form reads ready on its felt-knowledge row before every dish has been tasted with the account, and dinner's gate does not open around a floor that has not.

### 11.1 What the record says, and what it leaves

"Every new person is paired with a mentor, a proven cultural steward rather than simply the strongest performer, someone who answers questions and makes the early experience reflect the standard" (WP p.18). Her equivalents are the "spin-up buddy" and the "guide," one for day-to-day questions and one for "a safe space for any 'Why do we do things this way?'" (Book p.219; p.258). The record's one sentence is more specific than her two roles: it names who the mentor is (a cultural steward, proven), who they are not (the strongest performer as such), and what they do (answer questions; make the early experience reflect the standard). It leaves who chooses, how, from whom, how the mentor is paid, what the mentor carries beyond questions, and what the mentor refuses.

### 11.2 Who, chosen how

The pool. A person is eligible to mentor when: they have closed their own ninety-day plan with its goal held, or, pre-opening, hold a readiness form reading ready from a training service (P8); their person page carries no non-negotiable line; they have completed the mentor module (I6) on the platform; they hold the seat, or a seat on the same strand in the same domain, as the person they will mentor; and they have said yes. The pool is a list on the stack per strand per domain, readable by the domain leads and the Maitre d, like the ready-now pool (S6 P72), and a person's presence on it is on their own person page. "Proven cultural steward" is read as those five conditions and nothing softer: a steward is proven by the record, not by a lead's sense of them. "Rather than simply the strongest performer" is read as the record's own refusal of the industry's default, and the pool does not rank.

Who names. The domain lead, at the offer, from the pool, so that recruiting can write the name on the person page (S5 P16) and connect the mentor before arrival (P13). The lead may not name a person not on the pool, may not name the person who will run the readiness read, may not name themselves, and may not name a person already mentoring someone whose plan is open, because a mentor with two open plans is node overload in the strand (WP p.14). Where the pool holds no eligible person for a seat, the start date cannot be set, and that is the training-infrastructure load S5 12.6 already routes to the leads' review, and pre-opening the two-wave rule (P8) is the answer.

Matching. The lead names for strand and domain and, where the pool allows, for the person's language from the fourth read (S6 P23) and for the shift shape that lets the two overlap. The lead does not match for temperament, because the house has no reading of the new person's temperament and refuses one (S5 P8), and does not match for "who would get along," which is the room's read the hiring system was built to keep out (S5 P9).

**P41 (Recommended).** The mentor pool is per strand per domain: a closed plan with its goal held or, pre-opening, a ready form from a training service; no non-negotiable line; the mentor module complete; the seat or the strand in the domain; and a yes. The domain lead names from the pool at the offer, never themselves, the assessor, a person off the pool, or a person with an open plan already, matching for language and overlap and never for temperament. No eligible mentor means no start date, as an infrastructure load.

### 11.3 Paid how, carrying what, refusing what

Paid. As shift. Every hour a mentor spends beside the person is a scheduled hour, placed by the stack as a teaching body on the paired services and the live session (S6 P31's rule), and the record's rule that "If it matters, Sŏn pays for it. Employees should not subsidize it" (WP p.19) closes any version in which mentoring is an unpaid favor. Whether mentoring is recognized beyond shift, as teaching in the record's paid-teaching sense (WP p.19), is Session 13's (P35), and this page states no figure and files the coupling. What the mentor is not paid for: pre-arrival contact beyond the one message on shift (P13), and nothing else is asked.

Carrying. The mentor carries the house's ways: what the module could not say about how this section, this pass, this well actually runs; the live session; the person's questions, in the first week before the first check-in and after; the paired services as the teaching body; the standard, by being the person the new person watches most, which is the record's "makes the early experience reflect the standard" (WP p.18); the arrival line and the language constant by example; and the feedback channel's use, by showing the person where "what is in the way" goes between check-ins (WP p.22). The mentor is the second of the ledger's three communication channels, "carried by the domain lead or mentor" (S5 P34, item 4), and is the person a new hire asks before they ask the lead.

Refusing. The mentor is not the assessor and never scores the readiness read (P23). The mentor does not hold the check-in and writes nothing on the person page, ever; anything the mentor thinks the lead should know is said to the person first and then, if the person agrees, goes to the check-in in the person's own words, because a person page that accumulates a mentor's notes is her shared 1:1 document with the sharing removed (S4 P18). The mentor gives no verdict on the person to anyone, and a lead who asks a mentor "how are they doing" is asking for the impression the system was built to refuse; the lead reads the person page. The mentor does not assign, schedule, or discipline. The mentor does not deliver the why block, the range card, or the legal onboarding. The mentor does not stand between the person and their lead: the new person's check-in is theirs and the mentor is not in the room. The mentor never sees the packet (P13). The mentor is not a supervisor, a trainer with a rubric, or a buddy with a checklist, and the mentor module teaches the refusals before it teaches anything else.

Duration. Through the plan, and after it as the person chooses. At the fifth check-in the person says what shape they want the relationship to have (section 7.4), and the stack stops placing the overlap at the plan's close. A mentor may step back before the plan closes by telling the lead, and the lead names another from the pool without a line on either person's page.

**P42 (Recommended).** The mentor is paid as shift, placed by the stack as a teaching body, with any recognition beyond shift Session 13's. The mentor carries the house's ways, the live session, the first week's questions, the paired services, the standard by example, and the feedback channel's use. The mentor never scores, never writes on the person page, gives no verdict to anyone, does not assign or discipline, does not deliver the why block or the range card, is not in the check-in, and never sees the packet. The relationship runs through the plan and after it as the person chooses.

### 11.4 Where this reads against the record and Session 5

Second, Session 5 has recruiting write "the mentor, connected before arrival" at the offer (S5 P16), and this page has the domain lead name the mentor from a pool at the offer (P41). These are the same act read from two sides: the lead names, recruiting writes the name, and the stack connects. No Session 5 position changes; the pool is added beneath S5 P16 as the source of the name, and Brandon ratifies the addition with it.

## 13. Her hiring mistakes, answered and routed

### 13.1 Her section, and the seam it sits on

"For most hiring mistakes, you won't realize the error until a few months into the person's tenure. In that case, it boils down to a performance issue" (Book p.224). Some surface "right away, sometimes even before the person's start date," and her four signals are confidential information posted, a misrepresented background, poor treatment of people before the start date or during onboarding, and "arrogance and bad behavior toward their new team members in the first week" (Book p.224). Her rule: "The main thing is to do something" (Book p.224). Her loop: a retrospective, and data (Book p.225 to 226).

The section sits on three seams. Session 6 found that the record's "blame the process" has no hiring object and routed where a hire that does not hold is read to Session 14 (S6 18.6). Managing out is Session 15's. Low performers are Session 14's. This page writes only what onboarding owns and routes the rest by carryover; it does not design managing out, a probation, or a termination.

### 13.2 What the ninety-day plan reads as a hire not holding

A state, never a verdict. The plan reads: a milestone not reached by its expected date and by which link (modules incomplete, live session not held, paired services short, a not-yet form with rows named, a solo not placed, load lines not holding, the range unused); a supplied line that reads "not supplied" against something the person said they needed; and an infrastructure line at the leads' review. That is the whole of what the plan reads, and it reads it as the record's own sequence: "when something goes wrong, the first question is not who failed. It is what allowed this to happen" (WP p.17).

### 13.3 What the house fixes before it reads a person

In the building, in order, before any question about the person: the module (was it in the person's language, did the floor contradict it, is it version one); the mentor's load (two open plans, an overlapping schedule that did not overlap); the paired count (was it the sheet's, did the services run short); the readiness read (did it run in a flagged service, was the assessor also the mentor by error); the schedule (was the solo placed as sole coverage, on a flagged night); the felt-knowledge sequence (had the dishes been tasted); and the building's supply against the fourth-read baseline (the locker, the uniform, the language, the shift shape the person said they needed). Each of these is a training-infrastructure line and is fixed by versioned decision where it is a document and by the schedule where it is a placement. This is her hiring manager's retrospective (Book p.225) with its object moved from the manager's process to the house's, and with one addition her section does not have: the fourth-read baseline gives the house a written statement, in the person's own words, of what they said they would need, so the house's first question has a document to be asked against.

Only after that, the hiring system: the ninety-day calibration read opens the packet beside the page and reads whether the marks corresponded (S6 P41), and a hire who did not hold under load in the first four dinner weeks "is first a question about the practical's rows and the training plan, and only after that a question about the interviewer" (S6 P41). Onboarding has no view into the packet and asks for none.

### 13.4 What the plan may not conclude, and what is routed

The plan may not conclude that the person should leave, that the hire was a mistake, that the plan should be shortened, or that a probation has failed, because none of those is a state the plan holds. The plan may extend once for a building fix (P29). Past that, a plan whose goal is not held, with its record, is handed to Session 14, which owns where a hire that does not hold is read and what is fixed, and Session 15, which owns managing out. The carryovers say what onboarding hands over: the plan block whole, the infrastructure lines, the supplied lines, and the record that the building's fixes were made first.

Her "consistently late to meetings" (Book p.224), the minor and feedback-worthy case: at Sŏn a person late to a paired service is a schedule fact the stack holds and a line at the second check-in in the person's own account; it is not "feedback quickly and strongly" from a manager, it is a question about what is in the way, and only a pattern the check-ins have recorded and the person has heard becomes anything else.
