# Session 5: Recruiting

This page is the Sŏn answer to the Recruiting section of Chapter 3 (Book p.167 to 192), with the Chapter 3 workbook read only for what bears on recruiting (Workbook p.42 to 66). It is written to be run from. A person who has not read the book can read this page and know what happens when a seat opens, who runs each stage, what each stage reads, what it writes to the record, and what it refuses to do.

Every position on this page is numbered and carries exactly one mark. (Recommended) is the team's position, ready for founder ratification. (Founder-gated) requires input the team cannot supply. (Chef-gated) is left open for the chef partner. (Team-filled) is populated once the team exists. Nothing on this page is final. A recommended position is a recommendation until Brandon ratifies it, and a founder-gated position is argued to the ceiling one founder can reach and then marked for what Dominic, the chef, or the team must close.

Vocabulary on this page follows the ledger. "Operating system," unqualified, is the human system of strategy, accountability, and cadence. The software is "the stack" or "the OS surface," never "the OS" alone. The customer is the customer, in every stage of this process and in every document a candidate reads. Service periods are named by time of day. Financials are not a context source for this program: no wage, no percentage, no recruiting spend, and no headcount cost appears on this page, and where one is needed the gap is stated.

Two words are this page's own. A "seat" is a position in the web: a node with a domain, a strand, and documented competency requirements. The record uses "position" and "role"; role naming is an open item in the record (WP p.15), so "seat" is a working word, not a title. The "seat sheet" is the artifact that opens a seat, specified in section 4. Its name is provisional for the same reason.

Two tools are named on this page because Brandon named them on the session task: Greenhouse is the applicant tracking system, retained over Rippling's native hiring module; Rippling is the single sign-on and human-resources layer. The white paper's five-layer stack names a data hub the program's source boundary says is out of use; that is an open question (`86akh5u6e`) and no operating-system document names a hub tool. Nothing on this page assumes a hub.

## 1. Her ask, and what Sŏn already holds

### 1.1 Her ask

Her opening premise is that "A company's talent is its destiny" (Book p.168), and her operating demand follows from it: "practically everyone at the company must be involved in hiring, and they must consistently prioritize the work and adhere to the operational and cultural standards you've set" (Book p.168). She wants rigor at every level, not only at the top: "If leaders seemingly waltz in, there will be suspicion of their credibility, even if they have impressive résumés" (Book p.168). And she wants both quality and speed, which she concedes is "the scariest thing you can say to an operations leader" (Book p.168).

Her frame for the work is commercial. "What the industry calls 'talent acquisition' is really just marketing and sales" (Book p.169): invest in the talent brand, identify leads, direct traffic into a funnel, assess lead quality, optimize conversion. The process "doesn't end with a candidate accepting the job but rather with a successful onboarding experience" (Book p.169). She adds a pyramid: volume hiring at the base with proven people interviewing and "a final manager interview," a set committee in the middle, and a bespoke executive team at the top (Book p.170).

Her recruiting section then carries six things this session must answer. The founder's role: interview every finalist while "100 employees or less per founder," model the no on the candidate who is "just fine," and be transparent about why (Book p.171). The talent brand, the careers page, and the job description: "as clear as you can about the work environment and the expectations" without being "overly verbose or prescriptive," with attention to "language about the role or expected qualifications that transmits bias" (Book p.174). The recruiting seat and its timing: a recruiter and possibly a coordinator once "past your initial team, say, 10 to 20 people" and "consistently hiring"; a recruiting team once "hiring multiple people a week"; and "a recruiter should never be a substitute for a hiring manager" (Book p.175). Hiring as everyone's job, with mutual commitments between recruiting and everyone else (Book p.175 to 178). Role definition before opening: "Build insights on talent needs and candidate success" (Book p.178), the three-circle employee who is good at the work, has impact, and loves it (Book p.178), self-selection through honest descriptions (Book p.180), referrals and their limit (Book p.180 to 181), and screening (Book p.181). And leadership recruiting: determine what kind of leader, build the rubric by interviewing the field, the three questions ("What is the work to be done? What does 'great' look like? How will we assess people against that benchmark?" Book p.184), the rule that you "do not use your interview process to calibrate your understanding and expectations of a role" (Book p.184), the experience trap and the playbook thinker (Book p.189), promoting from within against hiring outside with her one-third guidance (Book p.190), search firms (Book p.190 to 191), and "Never forget that you are ultimately the recruiter" (Book p.191).

The workbook is read for two things only. Its interview framework structure, competencies assessed, stages, who may run each, length, and a rubric per stage (Workbook p.43 to 45), is the object recruiting must produce before a seat opens; Session 6 writes what goes inside it. And its "Recruiting for Recruiters" loop is her definition of the seat: full life cycle, sourcing and closing, pitching the company against competitors, and pushing back on candidate asks (Workbook p.43, p.47). That definition matters in section 9, because the core behavior her recruiter is scored on is one the record designs out.

### 1.2 What the record holds

The record decided a great deal of this before the book was opened, and it decided some of it against her.

Talent acquisition, as a system. "The industry runs the worst hiring process of any major sector, and it treats the result as a labor-market problem rather than a design problem. The correction is to build hiring as a system that respects the candidate's time and reads competency rather than instinct" (WP p.17). The system is specified. Candidates "self-schedule, choose the interview language, and choose the format, phone, video, or in person," and the system "tells them exactly what to expect at each step. No manual resume re-entry, no unanswered applications" (WP p.17). High-volume roles "such as line cook and porter" get "a short phone screen" (WP p.17). "Format choice does double duty as a qualification filter" and "Language choice protects dignity" (WP p.17). "Interviewers are trained, because asking a good question and reading the answer is a learned skill, not an instinct that arrives with a title. Interviews are semi-structured with documented questions, and they assess specific competencies, cultural alignment, and growth potential rather than gut feeling. The goal is not only whether this is the right person, but whether the building is equipped to create the environment this person needs to succeed" (WP p.18). "For roles with a physical skill component, the second interview is a paid practical in a real environment. This replaces the stage" (WP p.18). "Transparency is built into the first interview. The candidate receives a single sheet with everything needed to decide: the org chart, their direct lead, compensation and benefits, communication expectations, the training plan with pay dates, the compensation mechanics, and the advancement path" (WP p.18). "Speed is respect" (WP p.18). "For leadership and salaried roles, the final round is a dinner in the room, which is evaluation and recruitment at once" (WP p.18). "No one is ghosted. Every candidate receives a clear answer, including the no. A candidate treated well at the no returns to the pool next year. A candidate ghosted is one the business will never see again" (WP p.18).

The structure that system stands on. New people "always enter at the outside edge. The web never acquires altitude, only reach" (WP p.9). The outside edge is "the most sensitive position," and front-line team members are "the primary receivers of the operation" (WP p.9). "Thread removal is tension loss, not slot vacancy" (WP p.9), so a departure is "a tension re-establishment problem," not "a backfill problem" (WP p.9). Below the management layer "the web is composed of strands, not levels," the outward-facing strand's intelligence "relational and expressive and emotionally attuned under load," the inward-facing strand's "architectural and coordinated and timing-precise," and "These are not a promotion sequence" (WP p.10 to 11). "Cross-strand fluency, the ability to hold both at once, is the real development marker" and is "recognized in compensation as meaningful" (WP p.11). "Every position has documented competency requirements visible to anyone" (WP p.11), and the invisible advocate auto-schedules the practical assessment with the domain lead and places a person with no open position into "a ready-now pool" (WP p.11). "When advancement depends on documented competencies anyone can see, it favors the people who want it most" (WP p.11). When a key person leaves, "the ready-now pool surfaces who is furthest along on the required track" (WP p.14).

The management layer and the leadership count. The General Manager is split into an Operations Lead and a Maitre d, "parallel. Neither reports to the other" (WP p.10). The Maitre d owns "Team development, training, and the invisible advocate function" (WP p.10). Functional designations "mark accountability in the moment, in the domain. They are not permanent elevations above anyone" (WP p.10). The house runs "roughly eight leadership lines running the full span of the day against fourteen running one dinner service" (WP p.22), with the back-office administrator removed by software (WP p.22).

Succession as the goal. "The goal the system builds toward is internal succession: over time, leadership roles filled from within by people who have proven the culture and demonstrated the competency, to the point where the building rarely hires a manager from outside at all" (WP p.19). "A host who wants to learn everything can become the Maitre d the city has never seen, and never has to wait on a manager's attention to do it" (WP p.19).

Labor as asset, and the market. "The best people have options. To attract and keep them, Sŏn has to be worth choosing. Austin's restaurant labor market is tight" (WP p.16). The return on culture includes "the reputation that makes the best people want to work here before a role is even posted" (WP p.23). Retention rate and internal promotion rate are committed measures (WP p.23). "Employees are the users" (WP p.17), and "Universal problems are not people problems. They are design problems" (WP p.17).

The founding seats. "Two of its founders own everything outside the kitchen," and "the chef partner owns the culinary vision as an equal founding partner" (WP p.3). Whether lunch is ever built "is an equal decision among all three partners, the chef partner included" (WP p.36). "The culinary vision is real and it is led by the chef partner, but the business does not rest on the survival of any one person in it" (WP p.37). Canon, in both versions, holds the chef as a hire: "[HELD], Pending Executive Chef Hire" (Canon v1.0, What Remains; Canon v3.0, p.87), with two named hiring criteria, the Mahk standard and the chef's relationship to Bangjja Yugi, Baekja, Buncheong, and Cheongja (Canon v1.0, What Remains; Canon v3.0, p.89). That conflict is already open as the chef seat ruling (`86akh2qrp`) and is argued in section 10.

The documented build. "Documenting the actual build, the real problems, the real decisions, produces content that persists" (WP p.29), and the record names the material: "a live-fire installation, a lease negotiation, a chef search, regulatory timelines" (WP p.29). The content approval framework routes roughly eighty percent green-light, fifteen strategic review, five leadership review, the last covering "crisis response, strategic announcements, sensitive topics" (WP p.31). Creators are offered access, including "a seat at a training day" (WP p.31).

Canon's constraints on every word. The company non-negotiables: no exploitation ever, no toxicity ever, no disposable people, with the employee-first order "bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results" (Canon v1.0, section 01; Canon v3.0, p.4). The language constant: "'Guest' is not used, at any daypart, in any context, in any copy, in any internal communication. No exceptions" (Canon v1.0, section 01; Canon v3.0, p.5). The playground philosophy prohibits "training systems that measure procedure execution without building toward judgment" and requires "training architecture that progresses from procedure mastery to autonomous judgment" and "service authority distributed to the closest-to-customer position" (Canon v1.0, section 01). Nunchi is "Developed through mastery, exercised through judgment" (Canon v1.0, section 01; Canon v3.0, p.15). Jeong "cannot be scripted" (Canon v1.0, section 01). The five voice rules, the six binary tests, the register matrix, and the forbidden lexicon govern every copy surface (Canon v1.0, section 09; Canon v3.0, p.53 to 57). And canon's photography section carries the record's one worked selection instrument: photographer selection criteria with "must demonstrate," "immediate disqualifiers," and a cultural-fluency evaluation with a stated failure condition (Canon v1.0, section 10). That form is used in section 4.

### 1.3 The gap

The record holds the candidate-facing system nearly whole. What it does not hold is the machinery behind it. It does not say who may open a seat, or what document opens it, or where that document lives relative to the competency requirements the invisible advocate reads. It does not name the recruiting seat, or say there is none. It does not say who decides a hire, what range that decision sits inside, or what happens when a domain lead and a founder disagree. It does not say where the pipeline lives or who reads it on what rhythm. It does not say what the paid practical costs a running service, who scores it, or how it is paid. It does not say what the documented build may show of a search. It does not say when internal succession starts binding in a house that has no inside yet. It says the sheet at the first interview carries "the org chart" and the "compensation mechanics," and neither exists. It states two in-room stages, the practical and the dinner, for a cohort that must be hired before there is a room. It names cross-strand fluency and not cross-period fluency. It holds no position on the diversity of the team. And its three artifacts carry three different lists of what seats exist.

Every gap on that list is filled or marked below. None of the positions re-derives a decision the record already made.

**P1 (Recommended).** The record's Talent acquisition section (WP p.17 to 18) is held as decided. Every element of it is carried onto this page unchanged, and this page extends it with the machinery it does not state: who opens, who reads, who decides, in what range, on what record, and at what cost to a running service.

## 2. The reframe: what recruiting is at Sŏn

### 2.1 Her funnel, tested against the web

She calls talent acquisition "marketing and sales" and draws a funnel: traffic in, leads assessed, conversion optimized at each stage (Book p.169). The test is whether that language survives in a code-bearing organization.

Three properties of the record bear on it. First, the record has already rejected the word she reaches for, in its own domain: "This part rejects that word. What grows a brand now is not marketing spend, it is cultural equity" (WP p.28). The record's acquisition model for customers is not a campaign but a documented build that produces "a community that arrives already invested" (WP p.29), and it names the talent analogue directly: "the reputation that makes the best people want to work here before a role is even posted" (WP p.23). If the brand engine is not marketing, the talent brand is not marketing either, and the same mechanism carries both.

Second, a funnel narrows toward a decision point held by one person at the bottom, the hiring manager. The web has no bottom. A seat is a node; filling it is re-establishing the tension the node held, and the adjacent nodes are the ones who feel whether it is re-established (WP p.9). The funnel's end state, "conversion," is an offer accepted. The record's end state is later and different: "no one touches a table until they are actually ready" (WP p.19). Her own line agrees with the record on this and disagrees with her figure: the process "doesn't end with a candidate accepting the job but rather with a successful onboarding experience" (Book p.169).

Third, a funnel discards. What falls out of a funnel is gone. The record's pipeline has a state a funnel cannot represent: "A candidate treated well at the no returns to the pool next year" (WP p.18). A no is a routing, not a loss.

What survives, then, is the funnel's one genuinely useful property: it is a way to count. Applications per seat, decisions per stage, time in each stage, source of each candidate. Greenhouse counts this way natively, and the record wants leading indicators on everything it can see early (WP p.12, p.22). The stage counts are how the pipeline is read (section 6). What does not survive is the posture: the talent brand as advertisement, "closing" as a competency, and the idea that a candidate who declines or is declined has left.

**P2 (Recommended).** Her funnel survives at Sŏn as a measurement structure only: stages, counts, time in stage, and source. It does not survive as a posture. Sŏn's recruiting is the talent face of the brand engine, not of a marketing function, and its end state is tension re-established at the node, not an offer accepted.

### 2.2 Her pyramid, tested against strands

Her pyramid sorts hiring by altitude: volume at the base, a committee in the middle, executives at the top, with the process becoming "more focused and customized to the role" as it narrows (Book p.170). The record has no altitude to sort by. It does, though, distinguish seats, and it distinguishes them by what the work is, not by where the seat sits: "high-volume roles such as line cook and porter" get a phone screen (WP p.17); "roles with a physical skill component" get a paid practical (WP p.18); "leadership and salaried roles" get a dinner in the room (WP p.18). Those are three classes drawn by the work's shape. A fourth is the founding seats, which the record treats as partners (WP p.3) and canon treats as a hire (Canon v1.0, What Remains), and which section 10 holds.

The classes are not levels. A line cook's phone screen is not a lighter process than a lead's dinner; it is a different process for a different shape of work, and both run inside the same commitments: trained interviewers, documented questions, the transparency sheet, speed, and the answer. That is also her own rule, "the fundamentals and cultural import of leader and employee hiring should be similar" (Book p.168), and it is the record's, since every node "matters because the sequence is the mechanism" (WP p.8).

**P3 (Recommended).** Seats are classed by the shape of the work, in the record's own three classes plus the founding seats: high-volume seats (phone screen added), physical-skill seats (paid practical added), leadership and salaried seats (dinner in the room added), and founding seats (held). A seat may sit in more than one class. The classes set which stages a seat's process includes; they set no difference in rigor.

### 2.3 What the pipeline is a pipeline of

A pipeline of leads is a pipeline of strangers being narrowed. Sŏn's pipeline is a pipeline of people the house has answered. Every person in it is in one of four states: in a stage with a dated next action; in the pool with a dated note and the seat they were read for; declined with a sent answer; or hired, at which point they leave the pipeline and enter the person page. There is no fifth state, because "no unanswered applications" (WP p.17) and "No one is ghosted" (WP p.18) forbid it. A candidate in Austin is often also a customer and often a follower of the documented build (section 5), so the state a candidate leaves in is also the state a customer leaves in.

**P4 (Recommended).** The pipeline is a pipeline of people the house has answered. Every candidate is in exactly one of four states at all times: in a stage with a dated next action, in the pool with a dated note, declined with a sent answer, or hired. The stack surfaces any candidate who has fallen out of those four states, and that surfacing is the first line of section 6's pipeline read.

## 3. What the system is built to select for

A people system is the behavior it reliably produces, not what it declares. The question for a hiring system is therefore not what it says it values but what kind of person it reliably produces on the floor in month six. This section works out what Sŏn's recruiting must select for, given the record, and then handles carryover C1: her work style grid, whether it enters recruiting at all, and what does the job it was reaching for.

### 3.1 The four reads

The record names what interviews assess: "specific competencies, cultural alignment, and growth potential rather than gut feeling," plus a fourth thing that is not about the candidate at all, "whether the building is equipped to create the environment this person needs to succeed" (WP p.18). Those are the four reads, and each is given its Sŏn content here.

Competency for the strand. Every seat sits in a strand and the strand names its intelligence (WP p.10 to 11). The seat's documented competency requirements (WP p.11) are what the process reads, and only at the level the seat needs on the first day: what must be present at entry, and what the house develops. A candidate is not read for the competency the next unlock requires; that is the training platform's work, not recruiting's. Her three-circle employee, good at the work, high impact, loves the work (Book p.178), maps onto this read and the next.

Growth potential, read as evidence of self-directed mastery. The record's advancement system "favors the people who want it most" (WP p.11), and "Advancement requires preparation, not permission" (WP p.14). The behavior that system rewards is a person who, given a visible path and self-paced material, moves along it without being asked. Recruiting therefore reads for prior evidence of that behavior: something the candidate learned to mastery on their own account, in any domain. This is her "raw curiosity and signs of pure learning aptitude" (Book p.189) with the record's mechanism behind it.

Cultural alignment, read as the non-negotiables and the language constant, not as likeness. "Fit" at Sŏn is not resemblance to the people already here; that is the diversity failure she names (Book p.172) and section 6 addresses. Alignment is read as three things: no evidence of the behavior the non-negotiables forbid, exploitation and toxicity, in the candidate's account of prior work and in how they treat every person they meet in the process, including the steward or runner on a practical; comfort with distributed authority, since the house will hand them a range and expect them to act inside it without asking, which is her own screening question rewritten ("Are you comfortable with owning your own career outcomes rather than having a clear progression of goals and milestones described to you by a single decision-maker?" Book p.180 to 181); and, on the outward-facing strand, comfort with the customer as a customer, since the house "keeps the transaction visible" and does not perform (WP p.25).

What the building must supply. The record's fourth read is the one no other hiring system makes. The interview asks what this person needs to succeed and whether the building can give it: the language they work best in, the shift shape their life permits, the training format that reaches them, the accommodation the work requires. This read produces information the seat sheet and the ninety-day plan need, and it is where "Employees are the users" (WP p.17) enters the hiring system: the process is designed for the candidate, and it asks the candidate what the design must hold.

**P5 (Recommended).** The four reads are competency for the strand at entry level, growth potential as evidence of self-directed mastery, cultural alignment as the non-negotiables and comfort with distributed authority and the customer as customer, and what the building must supply for this person. Every stage's scorecard assesses one or more of the four and nothing else. "Fit" is not a scorecard attribute.

### 3.2 What cannot be selected for, and what stands in

Nunchi cannot be selected for, and a hiring system that tries to will select for something else. Canon is exact: nunchi is "Developed through mastery, exercised through judgment" (Canon v1.0, section 01; Canon v3.0, p.15), and the playground philosophy requires a training architecture "that progresses from procedure mastery to autonomous judgment" (Canon v1.0, section 01). The house's register of reading a table is built in the house, on top of the house's procedures. A candidate cannot arrive with it. What a candidate can arrive with is the raw capacity the register is built on, attention to what is happening around them before it is announced, and the willingness to master procedure first. A charming candidate who reads a room by performing at it is exactly the person the step-back architecture (Canon v1.0, section 08) is designed to stop, and a hiring system that scores charm as nunchi will staff the floor with performers. The paid practical is where this is protected: it scores procedure and reproduction, not presence (section 8.3).

Jeong cannot be selected for either. It "cannot be scripted" and "requires time, repetition, and memory" (Canon v1.0, section 01). It is what the house produces with the customer across visits. Recruiting owes it only one thing: a person who will stay long enough to accumulate it, which is the retention read, and retention is a property of the system (bandwidth, then joy, then retention, WP p.12), not of the candidate.

**P6 (Recommended).** Recruiting does not select for nunchi or Jeong and no scorecard names either. It selects for the capacities they are built from, attention and procedure mastery, and it scores the practical on procedure and reproduction so that presence is not mistaken for the register the house has not yet taught.

### 3.3 The strands as the selection frame

The record's two strands are not two kinds of person. They are two kinds of work, each requiring "a distinct intelligence" (WP p.10). That distinction is the selection frame, and it does two things a personality frame cannot.

It anchors the read in the seat, not the candidate. A candidate is read for the intelligence the seat's strand requires. The same candidate can be read for an inward-facing seat and an outward-facing seat and score differently, which is correct: "The best outward-facing operators may be mediocre in the inward-facing role, and the reverse" (WP p.11).

It removes the promotion gradient. Because "An outward-facing server is not a more senior inward-facing server" (WP p.11), a runner seat is not read as a step toward a server seat, and a candidate is not offered a runner seat as a consolation. Each seat is opened on its own requirements. Cross-strand fluency is a development marker the house pays for once someone holds both (WP p.11); it is not a hiring criterion, and a candidate is not scored for it.

**P7 (Recommended).** The strand is the selection frame. Each seat sheet names its strand and the intelligence the strand requires in the record's words, the candidate is read against that intelligence at entry level, no seat is read as a step toward another, and cross-strand fluency is not a hiring criterion.

### 3.4 Her work style grid, and carryover C1

Her grid crosses introvert and extrovert with task and people orientation to produce Analyzer, Director, Collaborator, and Promoter. In her book it is a self-awareness tool, and her one recruiting use of a comparable instrument is for complementarity on a leadership team, where she had a CFO candidate take an assessment and used the result to build "a common language" (Book p.189). Carryover C1 states the hazard of using it any further than that in a restaurant: service pressure selects hard for Director and Promoter, a floor staffed by pattern-matching to the room ends up with almost no Analyzers, and then nothing gets measured and no one notices.

The distortion is real and it is structural. It is not that leads are careless. It is that the room rewards assertion and expression in the moment, the people doing the interviewing are the people the room rewarded, and a hiring system that lets the room's read stand as the hiring read reproduces the room. A cultural answer, telling a Director-heavy floor to value Analyzers, does not work against a structural pressure. The answer has to be structural, and a code-bearing organization has one.

First, the grid does not enter recruiting. Not as a scorecard attribute, not as an interview question, not as a "balance" target for a team. Structure beats intuition in selection; the strongest predictors are the work sample and the structured interview, and a temperament typology is neither. A typology also reads the candidate, where the record reads the seat. The grid may return as a self-awareness tool inside the house, in onboarding or team development, and that is Session 7's and Session 10's to place; Session 6 does not put it in the interview.

Second, the work the grid was reaching for, protecting analytical capacity somewhere in the house, is done by the strands and the stack, not by a temperament quota. The inward-facing strand's intelligence is "architectural and coordinated and timing-precise" (WP p.11). That is where the disposition the grid calls Analyzer lives in the record's own structure, as a kind of work with its own seats, its own requirements, and equal standing. A house that opens and fills inward-facing seats on their own competency requirements, with the same rigor as outward-facing seats and never as a stepping stone, has protected the capacity structurally. And the measuring itself does not depend on hiring a measurer at the edge: the stack "is the nervous system" (WP p.12), the leading indicators are "the nervous system's signal" (WP p.12), and the Operations Lead "oversees those systems and is accountable to their outputs, not their manual production" (WP p.10). Nothing gets measured is a stack failure and an Operations Lead accountability, not a hiring shortfall in Analyzers.

Third, the paid practical scored against a rubric by an assessor who does not decide (section 8.3) is the specific mechanism that stops the room's read from becoming the hiring read. A rubric scores what the candidate did on the station. It does not have a column for how the candidate made the room feel.

Fourth, the service periods sharpen the profiles (C1), and one labor pool staffs several of them. The record's structure handles this without separate teams. Late night "is dinner's own later register" (WP p.35), the same room "loosened" (Canon v3.0, p.30), with the server's jacket off and the register table-state dependent (Canon v1.0, section 08). It is the same seats, later, and not a separate hire; the late-night profile C1 names, energy, presence, the room, is a scheduling preference on the seat sheet and a development read, not a sourcing target. The early morning is a different shape of work: "grab-and-go for the neighborhood," "a modular build-it menu," labor front-loaded in the back so "the person at the bar pours, finishes, and serves" (WP p.5). Its intelligence is repetition and precision at a window, closer to the inward-facing description than the outward, even though the customer is in front of it. That period has no ratified charter yet, so its seat sheets are drafted, not opened, and its sourcing is planned, not run. Lunch is not attempted and has no seats. Dinner wants the full range, and dinner is the first and for now the only configuration.

The one-pool consequence is that a person hired into a dinner seat may, once the early morning opens, be the person who holds a morning seat too. That movement is cross-period fluency, and the record names cross-strand fluency but not this (section 15). Section 11 recommends naming it.

**P8 (Recommended).** Her work style grid does not enter recruiting. No scorecard, interview question, or team-composition target uses it. Session 6 does not place it in the interview loop; whether it returns as a self-awareness tool in onboarding or team development is Session 7's and Session 10's.

**P9 (Recommended).** The structural answer to analyzer scarcity is: inward-facing seats opened and filled on their own competency requirements with equal rigor and never as a step toward outward-facing seats; measurement held by the stack and accounted for by the Operations Lead rather than by a disposition hired at the edge; and the paid practical scored by rubric by a non-deciding assessor, so the room's read never becomes the hiring read.

**P10 (Recommended).** One labor pool staffs the service periods. Each seat sheet carries a service-period field populated only from ratified charters, dinner alone for now. Late night is the dinner seats' later register and not a separate hire; its profile is a scheduling and development read. Early-morning seat sheets are drafted against the record's description of that work and open only when its charter is ratified. No lunch seats exist.

## 4. Role definition, and the seat sheet

### 4.1 Where role definition sits

Her three questions, "What is the work to be done? What does 'great' look like? How will we assess people against that benchmark?" (Book p.184), sit in her recruiting section, before sourcing, and her rule is absolute: "do not use your interview process to calibrate your understanding and expectations of a role. You will churn through potentially good candidates, and you may even damage your company's reputation in the process" (Book p.184). Her hiring section then consumes the rubric this produces. The session task asked whether role definition lives in recruiting or in hiring; it lives here. The scorecard precedes sourcing, and Session 6 inherits an instrument this session specifies.

The record makes this easier than she does, because the answer to her second question already exists as a standing artifact. "Every position has documented competency requirements visible to anyone. Employees do not need to ask what it takes to advance. They open the system and see it" (WP p.11). Those requirements are what the invisible advocate reads when it unlocks the next skill set and auto-schedules the practical assessment (WP p.11). If recruiting wrote a second definition of the seat for hiring, the house would hold two accounts of what a server is, and they would drift: the one advancement reads and the one candidates are read against. Skill invisibility is a named failure mode, "employees do not know what advancement requires because the competencies are not documented or accessible, and politics fills the vacuum" (WP p.14), and two versions is a subtler form of it.

**P11 (Recommended).** Role definition sits in recruiting, upstream of sourcing and upstream of Session 6's interview loop. The seat sheet that opens a seat is the same document as the seat's documented competency requirements that the invisible advocate reads. There is one account of each seat, and the hiring read and the advancement read are made against it.

### 4.2 What the seat sheet contains

The seat sheet is built on canon's one worked selection instrument, the photographer selection criteria: what a candidate "Must demonstrate," "Immediate disqualifiers," and a "Cultural fluency evaluation" with a stated failure condition (Canon v1.0, section 10). That form already passed canon's own review. The seat sheet generalizes it to every seat and adds what the record's structure requires.

The seat sheet must contain:

1. The seat's working name, and the note that role naming is an open item (WP p.15). No title carries altitude: no "junior," "senior," "assistant," or "head" except where the record itself uses the word (Head of Beverage, Lead Host, WP p.10), and "Lead Host" is a functional designation, "not permanent elevations above anyone" (WP p.10), which the sheet says.
2. The strand, and the intelligence the strand requires, in the record's words (WP p.10 to 11). For kitchen seats, the domain-owner and domain-execution distinction (Station Lead; Chef on the line; Chef on prep; Steward, WP p.11).
3. The domain lead: the lead of the domain the seat sits in, named by seat not by person, and stated as "the domain lead," never as "manager" or "reports to." This is the "direct lead" of the transparency sheet (WP p.18), and carryover C4 fixes its meaning.
4. The service period or periods the seat is open for, drawn only from ratified charters.
5. The work to be done, her first question, in the record's form: the seat's primary output and what the customer feels or does not feel of it (WP p.10 to 11). For a runner seat, for instance, the record's own sentence does the work: the customer "never feels this strand, they feel that everything arrived right" (WP p.11).
6. What great looks like, her second question, as the documented competency requirements at two horizons: what must be demonstrable at entry, and what the first unlock requires, so the candidate reads the path before accepting (WP p.11). The entry requirements are competencies, not credentials.
7. What the house develops. Every competency the house teaches rather than requires at entry, named, so that a candidate who lacks it is not declined for it and an interviewer does not score it.
8. How the seat is assessed, her third question: the stages the seat's class includes (section 8), which of the four reads each stage assesses, who may run each stage and who may not, and a reference to the practical's scoring rubric where the seat has a practical. This is the structure the workbook's framework carries (Workbook p.43), and it is what Session 6 fills with questions and rubric rows.
9. Immediate disqualifiers, in canon's sense: the small set of things that end a candidacy at any stage regardless of the rest, limited to the non-negotiables and to legal requirements the seat carries.
10. The decision-rights range the seat carries, in owner-range-triggers form: what the holder decides without asking, what they may not decide, and where each trigger goes. For a server seat much of this exists already: the server "owns the table" and "resolves it using their authorized judgment and generosity range" (WP p.13; Canon v1.0, section 08), with escalation triggers named (Canon v1.0, section 08). Carryover C4 says this range travels with the offer; it travels because it is on the sheet.
11. The shift shape: the periods, the pre-service and close windows the seat is scheduled into, and the check-in placement (fifteen minutes, first fifteen of a scheduled pre-service shift, per the ledger). No wage figure on this page; the sheet carries what the compensation architecture supplies once Session 13 ratifies it.
12. The compensation commitments the record states (section 7.4), without architecture.
13. The physical demands, stated plainly.
14. The languages the seat works in, and the languages the process is available in.
15. The reason the seat is open, in one line: new seat under a charter, tension loss (a departure, WP p.9), or period expansion. A seat opened by tension loss says what the departing node held, because the adjacent nodes are already compensating (WP p.9) and the sheet is how the house knows what it is re-establishing.
16. Who reads the seat's pipeline (section 6), and the response window.

Length is not a target. A sheet is as long as the seat's requirements, and no longer; canon's rule that the sentence ends at its last load-bearing word (Canon v1.0, section 09) governs.

**P12 (Recommended).** The seat sheet's sixteen fields as listed, built on canon's photographer-selection form. Fields 6 and 8 are what Session 6 consumes; field 10 is what carryover C4 says travels with the offer; field 12 is what carryover C3 says recruiting states.

### 4.3 What the seat sheet must do and must refuse

Must do. Exist, complete, before the seat is opened in Greenhouse; the stack refuses to open a job without a linked sheet. Live on the team home, "visible to anyone" (WP p.11), in the languages the team speaks, so a person in the house reads the same sheet a candidate reads. Be the source from which the posting is derived (section 5), never the reverse. Be readable by a candidate before the first interview, since the record's transparency sheet is handed at the first interview and the seat sheet is most of it. Be the document the invisible advocate reads for the next unlock. Change only by versioned decision (Canon v3.0, p.94), and any change re-reads every open candidate for the seat against the new version.

Must refuse. A seat opened without it. A competency that no stage can assess. "Fit," "attitude," "culture fit," or any attribute that is not one of the four reads. A years-of-experience requirement, since experience is among the weakest predictors and the record's own path runs on preparation, not tenure (WP p.14). A credential where a competency is meant. "Guest," anywhere. A wage or a percentage this program generated. A service period without a ratified charter. A title that implies altitude. A field left blank and marked "to be determined": an incomplete sheet does not open a seat.

**P13 (Recommended).** The seat sheet's obligations and refusals as stated. The stack enforces the first: no seat opens in Greenhouse without a complete linked sheet.

### 4.4 Who may open a seat

Written in the ledger's form: owner, range stated as may and may not, triggers with named destinations.

Owner. The domain lead of the domain the seat sits in. Authority sits with the closest competent owner, and the domain holds the information about what tension is missing. Pre-opening, before the leads exist, the owner is the partner who holds the hiring workstream, which Session 4 marked founder-gated with a recommendation of Brandon's seat (S4 P4). Once a lead is in the building, that lead opens their own domain's seats, and the first seats a lead opens are the seats of their own domain: the leads are hired first and hire their domains. Kitchen seats are the chef partner's to open, and the chef seat's own ruling (section 10) gates them.

Range. The domain lead may open any seat that is on the seat inventory for a service period with a ratified charter, when the seat's sheet is complete. The lead may open a seat by tension loss the day the departure is known, provided the ready-now pool has been read first (WP p.14) and the sheet records the reading. The lead may set the seat's response window inside the house standard (section 6.3). The lead may not open a seat for a period without a ratified charter. The lead may not open a seat that is not on the inventory; creating a seat is structure, not recruiting. The lead may not open a seat by re-titling an existing one. The lead may not open a seat without a sheet, or with a sheet missing a field. The lead may not open a seat in another domain.

Triggers. A request for a seat that is not on the inventory goes to the partners' review as a structure item, and in service to the leads' review first, which carries it to the partners' review with both leads' read. A seat opened by tension loss goes to that week's leads' review as one line: what the node held and what the adjacent nodes are carrying. A seat open past its response window with no candidate advanced goes to the leads' review as a load reading on the domain, not as a recruiting failure. A seat open past three consecutive leads' reviews goes to the partners' review as a possible node-overload entry (WP p.14), because the adjacent nodes have been compensating for three weeks. A seat that the lead wants to hold open rather than fill below the bar goes to the leads' review as a slack reading: the Realist's condition is that the bar holds only if the house can absorb the hole at tempo, and the leads' review is where the house says whether it can.

**P14 (Recommended).** The decision-rights entry for opening a seat as stated: the domain lead owns it, pre-opening the hiring partner; the range permits opening inventory seats under ratified charters with complete sheets and by tension loss after the pool is read, and forbids opening off-inventory, un-chartered, un-sheeted, re-titled, or other-domain seats; the triggers name the leads' review, the partners' review, and the node-overload path.

**P15 (Founder-gated).** The owner of the seat inventory. The inventory is a structure artifact and the record does not hold one (section 15). The team's recommendation: the partners' review holds it pre-opening, as a versioned list on the partners' page, and Session 8 receives it with the team-structures work. Brandon and Dominic close who holds it and whether the chef partner's seat on it is a vote or a domain.

### 4.5 What recruiting owes the check-in

The ledger fixes the check-in and this page does not redesign it: fifteen minutes on the clock, the first fifteen minutes of a scheduled pre-service shift, placed by the stack, three questions plus an upward question, one written line on the person page, every two weeks inside the ninety-day plan. The first check-in falls two weeks after the start date. For it to have anything to read, the person page must exist before the first shift, and recruiting is the stage that creates it.

Recruiting owes the check-in the person page's first entries, written at the offer and not at the first shift: the seat and its sheet version; the strand; the domain lead; the decision-rights range as accepted; the service periods; the start date; the mentor, connected before arrival (WP p.18); the ninety-day plan's start; and the first unlock the sheet names, so the page shows the path from day one. It also owes the check-in what the fourth read produced: what this person said they need from the building, so the first check-in's "what is in the way" question is asked against a stated baseline. Recruiting writes nothing else on the page. Assessment scorecards do not travel to the person page; the packet closes at the hire and its record stays in Greenhouse.

**P16 (Recommended).** Recruiting creates the person page at the offer with the entries listed, including what the candidate said they need from the building, and writes nothing else to it. Scorecards stay in Greenhouse and do not become the person's record.

## 5. The talent brand, and the documented build

### 5.1 The talent brand is the transparency

Her talent brand is built by "educating candidates about its existence," by founders posting where the audience lives, by events like Capture the Flag that "presented the company with participants who might be great job candidates" (Book p.172), and by a careers page that "explains what it's like to work at the company" (Book p.174). Her warning is that "what you say early on about your company and about these first roles is the beginning of both your talent and company brand" (Book p.174).

The record already runs the mechanism she describes, at higher volume than she imagines and with a different theory of why it works. The documented build produces "content that persists, relationships that compound, and a community that arrives already invested" (WP p.29). It already names "a chef search" as content inside it (WP p.29). And it already reaches the audience a talent brand reaches: the TikTok process content, the LinkedIn "operational and strategic thinking for the industry" (WP p.30), and the Twitch stream where "fifty people watching a construction problem get solved, a menu decision get made, or a vendor call happen live are fifty people who have spent hours with the founders directly" (WP p.30). An industry person who has watched a hundred hours of the build knows more about working at Sŏn than any careers page could tell them.

What the record adds, and what her chapter does not have, is the theory that the talent brand is the transparency itself. "Transparency attracts the people who want what the building offers and filters out those who do not" (WP p.18). The seat sheet is visible to anyone (WP p.11). The transparency sheet hands the candidate everything at the first interview (WP p.18). The self-selection she wants from an honest job description (Book p.180) is produced at Sŏn by publishing the actual terms rather than a description of them. Canon says why this is the only version the brand can carry: "Warmth is in the attention, not the adjectives" and "Specificity is the mechanism of memory" (Canon v1.0, section 01). A posting that says the house is a good place to work has named its own effect and failed rule one (Canon v1.0, section 09). A posting that states the schedule, the training plan with pay dates, the check-in cadence, and the range the seat carries has not.

**P17 (Recommended).** The talent brand is the documented build plus the published terms. Sŏn runs no separate talent-brand effort, no recruiting event, and no "what it's like to work here" copy. The seat sheet, visible to anyone, and the transparency sheet, handed at the first interview, are the talent brand's whole content, and the build is its distribution.

### 5.2 What the build may show of a search, and may not

The record names the chef search as content, and it names the discipline: "document what actually happened and why, and let the rawness be part of the credibility" (WP p.30). It also runs an approval framework whose leadership-review class covers "sensitive topics" (WP p.31). A search involves people who are not yet team and may never be, and the framework has no class for them (section 15). This page supplies the seam.

What the build may show, green-light. That a seat is open, what it asks for, and why: the seat sheet is public by the record's own rule, so publishing it is not a disclosure. The founders' reasoning about what kind of person the seat needs. The process itself: what the stages are, why the practical is paid, why no one is ghosted, what the transparency sheet contains. The number of applications, stage counts, time to answer, and the house's own performance against its response window, because that is the house being honest about its own conduct. A team member who has been hired, on their own terms, after they are in the house; canon's photography rules then govern (Canon v1.0, section 10), with people present and no role photography.

What the build may not show, ever, without written consent given after the decision. Any candidate's identity, image, voice, application, or scorecard. Any content from a phone screen, an interview, a practical, or a dinner in the room. Any reference to a specific person as a candidate, including a public figure the community might guess. The consent is given after the decision, never before, and never as a condition of candidacy or advancement, because a candidate asked to consent while the decision is pending cannot refuse freely, and No Exploitation Ever (Canon v1.0, section 01) covers the person who has not been hired as fully as the one who has.

What the build may not do at all. Invite the audience to weigh in on a candidate. The record's community "weigh in on decisions" and their input is used "when it was good" (WP p.29), and that is right for a menu decision and wrong for a person. A hiring decision is a domain lead's, inside a range, on a packet; it is not the community's, and a candidate who learns they are being discussed by an audience has been treated as content. Stream or film a practical or a dinner in the room. Canon already forbids spectacle during dinner service (Canon v1.0, section 06), and a camera on a practical changes what the assessor is reading. Use a search's setbacks as content while a candidate is in process: a search that stalls is honest material after the seat is filled, and a story about a candidate who fell through is never material.

The chef search is the hard case, because the record wants it as content and the person being searched for may be a founding partner (section 10). Under either branch of the chef ruling, what the build shows is the founders' search, their reasoning, their travels, the kitchens they ate in, and what they are looking for. It does not show who they met until that person is in the seat and has said yes to being shown.

**P18 (Recommended).** The build shows the seat, the process, the reasoning, and the house's own conduct, all green-light. It shows no candidate, in any form, without written consent given after the decision and never as a condition of candidacy. It never invites the audience into a hiring decision, never streams or films an assessment stage, and shows the chef search as the founders' search, not as the candidates.

**P19 (Recommended).** The content approval framework (WP p.31) gains one rule rather than a new class: any content that would identify a person as a candidate is leadership review and requires the consent record in section 14 before it moves. Everything else about a search is green-light.

### 5.3 The candidate who is also a follower

A person who applies because they followed the build has done the thing the record most wants a customer to do: invested before the first transaction (WP p.29 to 30). They arrive with an account of the house that is months deep and mostly accurate. Two things follow.

The first is that they are also a customer, or will be, and the record's economics of the no are doubled for them. "A candidate ghosted is one the business will never see again" (WP p.18) is, for a follower, a customer lost and a community member turned. The no to a follower is a customer correspondence as much as a candidate one, and it is written in canon's correspondence register: "names what it knows specifically. Confirms without reselling. No hospitality-speak" (Canon v1.0, section 09). It names what was read and what the pool means. It does not perform regret.

The second is that a follower has watched the founders and may want to work for the founders, which is a load-bearing structure in the making. The record is explicit that the company is "built to run without them" (WP p.6). A follower-candidate is read like every other candidate, against the seat, and the first interview says plainly that the domain lead is who they work with, that the founders' floor presence runs on the service metronome and not on the candidate's expectation of proximity, and that the house they saw being built is the house, not the people who built it.

**P20 (Recommended).** A follower-candidate is read against the seat like any other. Their no is written in the correspondence register and treated as a customer correspondence as well as a candidate one. The first interview states that the domain lead, not the founders, is who the seat works with.

### 5.4 The posting, under canon

The posting is derived from the seat sheet and says nothing the sheet does not. Canon governs every word of it, and applying canon concretely removes most of the vocabulary the industry uses to describe a job.

The forbidden lexicon applies "in all contexts, no exceptions" (Canon v1.0, section 09; Canon v3.0, p.57). From it, the following cannot appear in a posting or in any candidate correspondence: passion, journey, curated, hand-crafted, crafted, artisanal, thoughtful, intentional, elevated, experiential, innovative, authentic, chef-driven, community-driven, seasonal, memorable, unforgettable, amazing, incredible, beautiful, stunning, must-try. That removes "passionate about hospitality," "career journey," "a curated team," "hand-crafted cocktails," "thoughtful service," "intentional culture," "an elevated dining experience," "innovative kitchen," "authentic Korean," "chef-driven menu," and "seasonal ingredients" in one pass. Also forbidden in written copy: "welcome" and "enjoy" (Canon v1.0, section 09), so no written correspondence to a candidate says "welcome to the team," including the offer. No exclamation points and no emoji (Canon v1.0, section 01), so no "we're hiring!" and no "excite-a-mail" (Book p.177) in her form. No "we believe," no "journey," and no "founder hero narrative" in longer-form digital (Canon v1.0, section 09), so the posting does not open with the founders' story. No "we are committed to," by this program's standing rule. No em dashes.

The five voice rules, applied to a posting sentence. Rule 1, copy never names its own effect: "a role where you'll grow" and "a team you'll want to be part of" are out. Rule 2, every noun carries its own weight: "we're looking for passionate, dedicated people" fails the swap test twice, since "passionate" is forbidden and "dedicated people" survives replacement by any adjective and any noun. Rule 3, the sentence ends at its last load-bearing word: "servers who bring their whole selves to every shift, every time" ends at "shift." Rule 4, implication is the customer's work: a posting does not explain why the practical is paid; it says the practical is paid and the sentence ends. Rule 5, specificity is precision: "competitive pay" applies to every posting in Austin and is out; "the training plan carries pay dates and the first fifteen minutes of a scheduled shift every two weeks is your check-in, on the clock" applies only here.

The six binary tests run on every sentence in sequence (Canon v1.0, section 09; Canon v3.0, p.54), and test five, "Could this sentence appear unchanged in a competitor's copy?", is the one that does most of the work on a posting. Almost every sentence in an industry job posting could.

The register. Canon's register matrix names six surfaces and none is a recruiting surface (section 15). The team's reading: a posting is longer-form digital ("statements accumulate without explaining each other. Brand position named directly," Canon v1.0, section 09), candidate correspondence is correspondence, and the seat sheet on the team home is in-space copy's cousin, "present when found, not announcing itself" (Canon v1.0, section 09). Canon's "We" in brand voice holds. Canon's "You" is specified for customer-facing contexts, and a candidate is not a customer in the moment of reading a posting; the team reads "you" as correct for a candidate as well, since the alternative is a third-person posting, and marks the reading held for canon's owner.

Korean terminology. Canon says Korean terms "stand without explanation in all brand contexts from day one" and that "Translation never appears" (Canon v1.0, section 04). A posting that says "Jeong" and "nunchi" without gloss is canon-compliant and will read as a closed door to a candidate who has never heard the words. The team's reading: a posting names the work in plain terms and does not use the Korean terms; the transparency sheet and the training platform introduce them inside the house, where they are taught rather than glossed. Whether a document handed to a candidate is a "brand context" is a question canon does not answer, and it is marked held.

**P21 (Recommended).** The posting is derived from the seat sheet, states only what the sheet states, and passes every sentence through the five voice rules and the six binary tests. The forbidden lexicon removes the industry's job-posting vocabulary outright, and "welcome," exclamation points, "we believe," and the founders' story do not appear. Test five, the competitor test, is run on every sentence.

**P22 (Founder-gated).** The candidate-facing register. Canon names no recruiting surface. The team reads a posting as longer-form digital, candidate correspondence as correspondence, "you" as correct to a candidate, and the Korean terms as absent from postings and present inside the house. Canon's owner closes it; this is a brand fact and the team does not assert it.

### 5.5 The careers surface, pending the website

Canon defers website IA and templates: "IA must precede template-level component work" (Canon v1.0, What Remains; Canon v3.0, p.91). A careers page is a website surface, so the designed careers page is gated on the IA closing. Greenhouse hosts a job board that can stand without the website, and until the IA closes that board is the careers surface. What canon can govern on it, it governs: the posting copy in full; sentence case; no social proof widgets, so no testimonial carousel; no urgency signals, so no "apply now, roles fill fast"; no forced account creation, which canon forbids for the reservation flow and the same principle holds for an application (Canon v1.0, section 12); no loading state or empty state that performs (Canon v1.0, section 12). What canon cannot govern on a hosted board, the type and the tokens, is accepted as a hosted surface's limit and noted for the IA session.

Her bias-language point (Book p.174) is met structurally rather than by a tool. The sheet's entry requirements are competencies, not credentials or years; the posting inherits that; and the forbidden lexicon and the competitor test already strip the reassurance adjectives that carry most of the signal she is worried about. A language requirement appears only where the seat's work requires the language, and the posting states which languages the process itself is available in.

**P23 (Recommended).** Until the website IA closes, the Greenhouse-hosted board is the careers surface, governed by canon's copy rules and its digital prohibitions where a hosted board permits. The designed careers page is gated on the IA. Bias in postings is addressed by competencies-not-credentials and by the lexicon, not by a third-party tool.

## 6. Sourcing, the pipeline, and referrals

### 6.1 Referrals

Her position has two halves. Referrals are effective and referrers should be treated well, and "Referrals are also a great way to gauge employee happiness and engagement" (Book p.181). And referrals "can undermine attempts to diversify your team," because "our friends tend to be just like us in terms of experience, background, and often race and gender identity" (Book p.172, p.181). Her own hire came by referral, and the referring manager "grilled me for 45 minutes" rather than waving her through (Book p.181).

The record is silent on referrals. It is not silent on where information lives. The people at the edge "feel the customer, the room, and the cultural temperature of the building before anyone else" (WP p.9), and a referral is that sensitivity pointed at the labor market: a server who has worked three rooms in Austin knows who in those rooms is worth having. A code-bearing organization captures that information into the system and does not privilege it. So a referral enters Greenhouse as a source tag with the referrer named, and nothing else about the process changes: the same stages, the same reads, the same sheet. She allows referrals to skip the résumé review (Book p.181); at Sŏn there is no résumé review to skip, because the format choice and the phone screen are the filter (WP p.17), and a referral takes both. The referrer never runs a stage for the person they referred, which is the assessor-decider separation applied to the one case where it is most easily lost.

The referrer is thanked, in writing, whatever the outcome, and told the outcome at the same time the candidate is, which is how "Value referrals and referrers" (Book p.177) reads at Sŏn. Whether a referral is paid is a founder decision. The team recommends against a cash referral bonus: the record's stance is labor as asset and the peer recognition platform already carries acknowledgment "from the people who understand the work" (WP p.19); a bounty turns the edge's information into a sale, and the pipeline read will show soon enough whether referrals are flowing without one. No figure is stated and none is needed to decide the principle.

The diversity half is the harder one, because the record holds no position on the diversity of the team (section 15). It holds inclusion mechanics: interview language choice "protects dignity" (WP p.17), and the fourth read asks what the building must supply for each person (WP p.18). It states no goal for who the team is. Her chapter treats this as first-order, and her workbook scores a recruiter on it (Workbook p.49). The team does not assert a position for the house. It recommends the read that any position would need: Greenhouse's source and stage data is read at the leads' review for whether any single source dominates a seat's pipeline and whether any stage declines a group of candidates at a rate the others do not, with no target set and no figure on this page. Whether the house states a position on the composition of its team is founder-gated and is routed to Session 11, whose subject is inclusion.

**P24 (Recommended).** Referrals enter Greenhouse as a tagged source with the referrer named and take every stage the seat's class includes. The referrer runs no stage for their referral and is told the outcome in writing when the candidate is.

**P25 (Founder-gated).** Whether a referral is paid. The team recommends no cash referral bonus, for the reasons stated. No figure is involved in the decision.

**P26 (Founder-gated).** Whether the house states a position on the diversity of its team. The record is silent. The team recommends the source-dominance and stage-rate read at the leads' review regardless, with no target, and routes the position itself to Session 11.

### 6.2 Sources, by seat class and strand

Sources are stated per class and strand rather than per period, because dinner is the only period with a charter and because one pool staffs the periods (P10). Four sources exist for every seat; the mix differs.

The build's audience. Self-selecting, invested, and already reading the seat sheet. Strongest for outward-facing dinner seats and for leadership seats, because the build's LinkedIn and long-form surfaces reach the industry (WP p.30). Weakest for high-volume kitchen seats, which the build reaches least.

The board. Direct applications through the Greenhouse-hosted board, in the languages the process runs in. The default source for high-volume seats, where the phone screen exists precisely because "a short phone screen identifies fit in a few minutes" for a volume of applicants (WP p.17).

The house. Referrals, per section 6.1, and the pool: every candidate declined well "returns to the pool next year" (WP p.18), so from the second season onward the house's own prior candidates are a source with a dated note attached. A seat opened by tension loss reads the ready-now pool before any external source (WP p.14), which is not a source in the recruiting sense but sits ahead of all of them.

### 6.3 The pipeline: what it is, where it lives, who reads it, on what rhythm

What it is. A pipeline of people the house has answered (P4), organized by seat. Each seat is a Greenhouse job linked to its sheet; each candidate is in one of the four states; each stage carries a scorecard against the sheet's reads.

Where it lives. In Greenhouse, wholly. No spreadsheet beside it, no message thread carrying a candidate's state, no lead's memory. The ledger's rule is that a pipeline that runs because someone remembers to look at it is the failure, and the record's bus failure is "verbal handoffs, tribal knowledge, and informal relationships" (WP p.13). Anything about a candidate that is not in Greenhouse did not happen.

Who reads it. The domain lead for each of their seats, because the lead decides. The Maitre d for every seat, because the Maitre d owns "Team development, training, and the invisible advocate function" (WP p.10) and the house's commitment that no one is ghosted and no interviewer is untrained is a team-development commitment. The Operations Lead for the process's compliance and record, because the Operations Lead "is accountable to their outputs" of the systems (WP p.10). Pre-opening, the hiring partner in the leads' place.

On what rhythm. Two rhythms, matching the ledger's two cadences. The stack's rhythm is continuous: Greenhouse acknowledges every application on receipt; the stack surfaces any candidate past the response window with no dated action, any candidate outside the four states, and any seat past three leads' reviews, to the domain lead and the Maitre d, without anyone asking. The house's rhythm is weekly: one standing line on the leads' review agenda, the pipeline read, carrying per open seat the stage counts, the candidates past window, the source mix, and the seat's age. Session 4 fixed the leads' review's eight agenda items and an alternating chair; the pipeline read enters as the source of one of those items' reads, or as a ninth line if Session 4's eight do not carry it, and the team recommends it be read under the load reading, since an open seat is a load on adjacent nodes.

The response window. "Speed is respect" (WP p.18), and "an arbitrary multi-week wait signals that the business does not understand their reality" (WP p.18). The team sets the house standard at five days from any stage's completion to a sent decision, and a lead may set a shorter window on a sheet but not a longer one. Five days is a parameter for the mechanism reset, not a law, and it is chosen because a week is the leads' review interval and a candidate should never wait for a meeting.

The four-part entry test on the pipeline read. Bandwidth cost: a few minutes of the leads' review each week, and the stack's surfacing costs no one's attention until it fires. What it buys: no unanswered applications and no ghosting as system properties rather than intentions, and the earliest read on a domain carrying an open seat. Who runs it: the stack surfaces, the domain lead acts, the Maitre d holds the standard, the leads' review reads. Where the record lives: Greenhouse for the candidates, the leads' review record for the weekly line.

**P28 (Recommended).** The pipeline lives wholly in Greenhouse, one job per seat linked to its sheet, every candidate in one of four states. It is read by the domain lead per seat, the Maitre d across seats, and the Operations Lead for the record; pre-opening, by the hiring partner. The stack surfaces exceptions continuously; the leads' review reads one standing line weekly under the load reading.

**P29 (Recommended).** The house response window is five days from any stage's completion to a sent decision. A lead may shorten it on a sheet and may not lengthen it. It is a parameter for the mechanism reset.

### 6.4 The candidate pulse

"Employees are the users," and the record wants "continuous feedback loops so the tools evolve based on real usage" (WP p.17). The applicant system is the first system a future employee uses, and the person best placed to say whether it worked is the candidate, at the end, whatever the outcome. The team adds one mechanism: a single open question sent with the decision, hire or decline, asking what in the process should change. No rating scale, because canon prohibits rating scales on its customer surfaces (Canon v1.0, section 12) and a candidate is often a customer. Answers are read at the monthly partners' domain read, not weekly, because the point is the process and not the person.

Four-part test. Cost: one line in the decision correspondence; a few minutes a month to read. Buys: the record's own feedback loop applied to its first system. Runs: the stack sends, the Maitre d reads. Record: Greenhouse, attached to the candidate, anonymized in the monthly read.

**P30 (Recommended).** One open question accompanies every decision, hire or decline, with no rating scale. Read monthly by the Maitre d for the process, never for the person.

## 8. The screen sequence

Her screening is résumé review, an optional assessment for sought-after companies, then a phone screen by "a recruiter or a hiring manager," then onsite interviews (Book p.181), with her caution to "beware of creating too many hurdles while you're still building a talent brand and your pipeline" (Book p.181). The record's sequence is different and shorter: application with format and language choice, a phone screen for high-volume seats, a first interview with the transparency sheet, a paid practical for physical-skill seats, a dinner in the room for leadership and salaried seats, a decision, and an answer (WP p.17 to 18). This section specifies each stage: what it reads, who runs it, what it may decide, what it may not, and what it writes.

### 8.1 The stages by class

Every seat: application, first interview, decision, answer. High-volume seats add the phone screen before the first interview. Physical-skill seats add the paid practical after it. Leadership and salaried seats add the dinner in the room after it. Domain-owner seats (Station Leads, Head of Beverage, Lead Host as a designation) add an adjacent-domain interviewer to the first interview, because the adjacent nodes are the ones who will compensate if the node does not hold (WP p.9). Pre-opening, every seat adds the founder stage (section 10). No seat has a written exercise or a take-home; her caution against hurdles applies, and the practical is the work sample.

**P38 (Recommended).** The stage sequence by class as stated. No seat carries a written exercise or take-home; the paid practical is the only work sample.

### 8.2 The application and the phone screen

The application. Reads: the seat chosen, the format and language chosen, availability against the seat's shift shape, and the seat's legal requirements. Runs: the stack. May decide: whether the application is complete. May not: anything about the person. Writes: the source, the date, the choices, the state.

The phone screen, high-volume seats. "A short phone screen identifies fit in a few minutes rather than spending an hour of the candidate's time, and theirs getting ready and traveling, on a conversation that was never going to work" (WP p.17). Reads: availability against the shift shape; the seat's entry must-haves from the sheet; the candidate's questions; the language and format they want the next stage in. Runs: a trained interviewer from the seat's domain, never the domain lead, scheduled by the stack from the trained pool. May decide: advance, or decline for a stated must-have reason from the sheet. May not: score any of the four reads beyond the must-haves; assess presence; decline for a reason not on the sheet. Writes: a scorecard with the must-haves marked and one line of what the candidate asked, and a decline reason from the fixed list. The candidate is told, on the call, what the next stage is and when they will hear.

**P39 (Recommended).** The application and the phone screen as specified: the phone screen reads must-haves and availability only, is run by a trained interviewer who is not the domain lead, and declines only for a reason on the sheet.

### 8.3 The first interview

Reads: the four reads (P5), at the depth the sheet's field 8 assigns to this stage; and it hands the transparency sheet. Runs: one trained interviewer from the seat's strand; for domain-owner seats, a second from the adjacent domain; for lead seats, a partner and the co-lead once one exists. The domain lead may run a first interview for their own seats and often will, and when they do, the second interviewer is required, because a lead who both assesses and decides under a short floor will let urgency override the bar. Semi-structured, documented questions per the record; Session 6 writes them. May decide: advance to the practical or the dinner, or to decision for seats with neither; decline on any of the four reads with the read named. May not: make or imply an offer; state a figure beyond the sheet; ask what the candidate earns elsewhere; read the candidate's format choice as a score. Writes: a scorecard per interviewer, submitted before the interviewers speak to each other, per read; the candidate's answer to the fourth read, what they need from the building; and the sheet version handed.

**P40 (Recommended).** The first interview as specified: two interviewers whenever the domain lead is one of them, scorecards submitted independently before discussion, the transparency sheet handed, and no offer implied.

### 8.4 The paid practical

The record replaces the stage: "the unpaid tryout shift that is common in the industry, frequently degrading, legally exposed, and a poor predictor of actual performance" (WP p.18). The practical is "in a real environment" and it is paid. What the record does not say is what it reads, who scores it, what it costs a service, or how it is paid. This is a floor-execution object and the Realist owns whether it survives a real service.

What it reads. Procedure mastery and reproduction under load, and nothing else. Canon requires a training architecture "that progresses from procedure mastery to autonomous judgment" (Canon v1.0, section 01), and mastery comes first, so the practical reads the first thing. On a kitchen station: the station's language, mise discipline, whether the candidate reproduces the plate the same way each time, whether they clean as they go, whether they ask before they guess. On the floor: the sequence, the walk ("We walk, we never point," Canon v1.0, section 06), the step-back positions, what they do with silence, whether they hand a runner a clean problem. On every practical: how the candidate treats the steward, the runner, and the person who is training them, which is the non-negotiables read in the one place it cannot be performed. It does not read presence, charm, or how the room felt about the candidate; P9 protects that. It does not read nunchi, which the house has not taught them.

Who scores it. A trained assessor from the seat's strand, against the rubric on the sheet, and the assessor is not the person who decides. The domain lead decides on the packet (section 8.6). Structure beats intuition: the strongest predictor of performance is the work sample, and it predicts only when it is scored rather than watched. A practical with no rubric is a stage with a paycheck.

Who is beside the candidate. The candidate is never alone on a station and never the only person a table has. The seat's holder or the mentor works the station with them. The customer is never told a candidate served them, and the candidate never carries a table's recovery or a generosity decision; those are the seat holder's range, not the candidate's.

What it costs a running shift, plainly. A station's slack for the service, since the seat holder is teaching and watching and not only working. The assessor's attention for the scoring window and a written scorecard after close. A table or a section that is one degree slower. On a full Friday with a green runner and a station short, none of that exists, and a practical scheduled on that night reads nothing true and costs the room. The Realist's condition is therefore a range condition: a practical runs only on a service the leads' review has not flagged short, never on the first service of a new period, never on a service the build is streaming, and never as the only coverage for a station.

Where it sits in the two cadences. Pre-opening, there is no real environment, and the practical for the cohort runs in training services: the Realist's test that "opening night is not month six" cuts the other way here, since a training service is the only floor there is, and a practical in one reads procedure honestly and reads load not at all. The cohort's practicals are marked as run without load, and the ninety-day plan reads load for them. In service, the practical runs on service tempo, on the schedule, on a service the leads' review has not flagged, and its date is never chosen to hit a start date. That last rule is the seam protection: the hiring timeline is a pre-opening object and a practical scheduled to make a date puts build tempo into the room.

How it is paid. Through Rippling, at the seat's rate, for the hours worked, and the record's word "paid" leaves the employment form unspecified: a person on a paid practical is either on payroll for the day or engaged some other way, and each has consequences the record does not name. The Operations Lead owns compliance (WP p.10) and closes the form with counsel. This page does not decide it and marks it.

Kitchen practicals. The station, the plate, the mise standard, and the rubric are the chef partner's, and the chef seat's ruling gates them (section 10). Until the chef is seated and the menu exists, no kitchen practical can run, because there is nothing to reproduce. That sequences the whole kitchen pipeline behind the chef ruling.

**P41 (Recommended).** The paid practical reads procedure mastery, reproduction under load, and the candidate's treatment of the people around them, against a rubric on the sheet, scored by a trained assessor who does not decide. The candidate is never alone on a station or a table, never serves a customer who is told, and never carries recovery or generosity.

**P42 (Recommended).** The practical's range condition: only on a service the leads' review has not flagged short, never on a new period's first service, never on a streamed service, never as sole coverage, and never scheduled to make a start date. Pre-opening, the cohort's practicals run in training services and are marked as run without load.

**P43 (Founder-gated).** The employment form of a paid practical. The record says paid and does not say how. The Operations Lead closes it with counsel; until then the founders decide the interim form for the cohort. No figure is involved.

**P44 (Chef-gated).** The kitchen practical's station, plate, mise standard, and rubric. No kitchen practical runs before the chef is seated and the menu exists.

### 8.5 The dinner in the room

Per section 7.5. Reads: the candidate as customer; their treatment of the server; their read of the room in their own register; whether they can hold a table conversation with the partners without performing for them. Runs: the partners, and the co-lead for a lead seat. May decide: advance to decision or decline, with the read named on a scorecard. May not: make an offer at the table; discuss the mechanics before Session 13 has ratified them; run on a flagged service. Writes: one scorecard per partner, submitted independently.

### 8.6 The decision and the packet

Her hiring meeting is a committee that discusses scorecards, "tied to particular capabilities," with a recruiter moderating (Book p.176 to 177). Sŏn has no committee and no recruiter. It has a packet and an owner.

The packet is complete when every stage the seat's class includes has a scorecard, each submitted independently, plus the sheet version handed, the candidate's fourth-read answer, and pre-opening the founder-stage scorecard. Greenhouse refuses a decision on an incomplete packet.

The decision is the domain lead's, inside a range (section 12). The lead may hire a candidate whose packet meets the sheet's bar. The lead may decline any candidate, with a read named. The lead may not hire below the bar to fill a hole; the hole is held and the leads' review is told (P14's trigger). The lead may not override a non-negotiable flag on any scorecard. The lead may not decide on a packet missing a scorecard. The lead may not decide their own seat's replacement without the co-lead's scorecard.

What replaces the committee is the independent scorecard and the named read. Her point that disagreement should be "specific and clear" and "tie directly into one of the capabilities" (Book p.176) is a system property here: a scorecard has no free-text verdict, only the four reads and the must-haves, and a decline names which.

Speed. The decision is made the day the packet is complete, and the answer is sent the same day. "When the skills and the alignment are there, the decision is made quickly and development starts immediately" (WP p.18).

**P46 (Recommended).** The decision is the domain lead's, on a complete packet of independent scorecards, made the day the packet is complete, inside the range in section 12. There is no hiring committee. A hole is held rather than filled below the bar, and the leads' review reads it as a slack question.

### 8.7 The answer, and the pool

Every candidate receives an answer, and the no is written. The answer names the stage reached and, for a decline, the read that decided it, in one line, in the correspondence register, in the candidate's language. It does not soften, explain twice, or perform regret; canon's rule 4 says implication is the reader's work and rule 6 says sentence two does not explain sentence one (Canon v1.0, section 09). A candidate the house would read again is told so and asked whether they want the pool; a yes tags them, with the seat and a dated note, and the pool is a source from the next season (P27). A candidate the house would not read again is not offered the pool and is not told why beyond the read.

The candidate pulse (P30) travels with every answer.

**P47 (Recommended).** Every answer is sent the day of the decision, in the correspondence register, naming the stage and for a decline the read. The pool is offered by name to candidates the house would read again, tagged in Greenhouse with the seat and a dated note.

## 9. The recruiting seat, and hiring as everyone's job

### 9.1 Whether Sŏn staffs a recruiting seat

Her thresholds are stated: a recruiter and possibly a coordinator once "past your initial team, say, 10 to 20 people," and a recruiting team with an experienced leader once "hiring multiple people a week" (Book p.175). Her recruiter "should never be a substitute for a hiring manager" and needs "a tight partnership" with "the person who will ultimately be accountable for the new hire's work" (Book p.175). Her workbook defines the seat: full life cycle, sourcing and closing, able to "pitch your current company against competition when closing candidates," "Able to push back on unreasonable asks from candidates" (Workbook p.43, p.47).

Three things in the record decide the question. The first is volume. A single house that "compounds without headcount growth" hires in one burst, the pre-opening cohort, and thereafter on tension loss and period openings. The record's succession goal is that "the building rarely hires a manager from outside at all" (WP p.19), and its retention stance is that the industry's turnover "is not" inevitable (WP p.16). Her "multiple people a week" is not the shape of this house after opening. Her lower threshold, ten to twenty people and consistently hiring, describes the pre-opening burst and nothing after it.

The second is what the seat's core competency is. Her recruiter closes: pitches against competitors, handles the financial component of the offer, steers candidates "away from apples-to-apples comparisons" (Workbook p.47). The record replaces closing with the sheet: "Transparency attracts the people who want what the building offers and filters out those who do not" (WP p.18). A house whose offer is a published sheet has no closer's work to do, and a person hired to do it will do it anyway, which is the behavior the record designed out.

The third is where the coordination she names actually goes. Her coordinator handles "scheduling and logistics" (Book p.175). The record's whole argument about the leadership count is that "Most of those positions are not leadership. They are manual integration work that software now does" (WP p.22), and it removes the back-office administrator on exactly that reasoning. Self-scheduling, acknowledgment, window surfacing, and the record are Greenhouse's (section 7). The coordinator is the stack.

What remains is the candidate relationship and the process standard, and the record already gives those a home: the Maitre d owns "Team development, training, and the invisible advocate function" (WP p.10), which is the seat that holds the standard that no one is ghosted and no one interviews untrained. The Operations Lead owns compliance and the record (WP p.10). The domain lead owns the seat. Hiring is everyone's job in the record's sense: the domain hires the domain, every trained person may interview, and every person may refer.

**P48 (Recommended).** Sŏn staffs no recruiting seat. The coordination is the stack's, the candidate-experience standard is the Maitre d's, the compliance and record are the Operations Lead's, and each seat's pipeline and decision are its domain lead's. Her recruiter's core competency, closing, is the behavior the transparency sheet designs out.

**P49 (Founder-gated).** How the pre-opening burst is run. The team recommends: the hiring partner (S4 P4) runs the process with the stack until the two leads are seated, the leads are hired first and then hire their domains, and no contracted recruiting help is engaged. Whether to contract help for the burst is the partners' to decide, and it is a cost question this program does not price.

### 9.2 Her mutual commitments, as Sŏn's specification

Her recruiting-commitments document (Book p.176 to 178) has two lists: what everyone owes recruiting, and what recruiting owes everyone. Its assumptions are a recruiting team, a hiring manager who owns the requisition, a hiring meeting, and a tacit rule that interviewing is done on top of the job. Three of those do not exist at Sŏn and the fourth is forbidden by the four-part entry test: a mechanism that costs bandwidth says so. Sŏn's version has the same two directions with the record's vocabulary, and it is written as a specification for the document the house will hold, not as the document.

The house's commitments to hiring. What every person who takes part in a hire can rely on:

- The seat sheet exists, complete and visible, before the seat opens; no one interviews for a seat they cannot read.
- Interviewer training is completed before a person runs a stage, on the clock, as scheduled shift; the stack will not schedule an untrained interviewer.
- Interview time, phone screens, and practical scoring are scheduled shift placed by the stack, never an expectation on top of a service. This is her "Keep your calendar accurate" (Book p.176) turned around: the house keeps the calendar, and the person's obligation is to be where the schedule placed them.
- Prep material for a stage reaches the interviewer before the stage, on the OS surface, in their language; her "Read any prep material" (Book p.176) becomes the house's duty to deliver it.
- The scorecard is submitted before interviewers speak to each other, and the stack enforces the order.
- What an interviewer learns about a candidate stays in Greenhouse; her "Handle sensitive information carefully and privately" (Book p.178) is a system property, since the record holds it and no one carries it.
- The bar holds when the floor is short; no interviewer is asked to lower a read to fill a station, and the leads' review carries the hole as a slack question.
- The no is sent by the house through the stack, never left to the interviewer to deliver or dodge.

Hiring's commitments to the house. What the process owes every node, and, since there is no recruiting seat, what the stack and the leads owe on its behalf:

- No candidate is ghosted; every candidate is in one of four states; the window is five days.
- Every stage writes to the record; nothing about a candidate lives outside Greenhouse.
- No assessor decides and no decider assesses alone.
- Interview language and format are the candidate's choice and are honored.
- The transparency sheet is handed at the first interview, identical for every candidate for the seat.
- The practical is paid, scored, and never scheduled onto a flagged service.
- The pool is kept, and a departure reads the ready-now pool before any external source.
- The pipeline is read weekly at the leads' review, and an open seat is read as a load on the domain, not a failure of it.
- A seat opens only in range, and a new seat is a structure decision the partners' review makes.
- The referrer is told the outcome when the candidate is.

Where her version assumes the hiring manager who owns the requisition, Sŏn substitutes the seat inventory and the range: the seat exists on the inventory before anyone wants it filled, the domain lead opens it in range, and no person requisitions a body from a function. Her "recruiter should never be a substitute for a hiring manager" (Book p.175) has no work to do here, because there is no recruiter and the domain lead is not a manager standing above the seat; the lead is the node with the most connections to it.

Her "publicly celebrate those who contribute the most" (Book p.175) and her "ABR (always be recruiting)" (Book p.177) are met by mechanisms the record already holds. Recognition runs through the peer platform "in the moment" (WP p.19) and needs no recruiting-specific ritual; the house's version of always recruiting is the record's own sentence, "the reputation that makes the best people want to work here before a role is even posted" (WP p.23), which is produced by how the house runs, not by anyone recruiting.

**P50 (Recommended).** Sŏn's mutual-commitments document is specified as the two lists above, in the record's vocabulary, with the house's commitments enforced by the stack where they can be and hiring's commitments owed by the stack and the leads in the absence of a recruiting seat. The requisition is replaced by the seat inventory and the opening range.

**P51 (Recommended).** No recruiting-specific recognition ritual and no "always be recruiting" campaign. The peer platform carries the first and the house's conduct produces the second.

### 9.3 Interviewer training as an obligation of the house

The record's rule is that "asking a good question and reading the answer is a learned skill, not an instinct that arrives with a title" (WP p.18). This page does not write the training; Session 6 does, with the interview loop. It fixes what recruiting owes: a training record on the person page, written by the stack when the training is completed; Greenhouse's interviewer pool limited to people with the record; training on scheduled shift; and a re-training trigger when a scorecard is returned without a named read, since a scorecard with a verdict and no read is the tell of an untrained interviewer.

**P52 (Recommended).** No person runs a stage without a training record on the person page, the stack enforces it, and a scorecard returned without a named read triggers re-training before the interviewer's next stage.

## 10. The founder's role, and the third seat

### 10.1 The founder stage, pre-opening

Her rule is that with "100 employees or less per founder or trusted senior leader," the founder interviews every finalist, because "founders are the people best equipped to model the hiring behavior they want to see" (Book p.171). She is specific about what the founder adds: the no on the candidate who is "just fine," who passes the rubric but leaves "some hard-to-name concern," a no that "needs to be modeled" because "Many interviewers will hesitate to call out amorphous concerns" (Book p.171).

The whole house is under her threshold and will stay under it. But the ledger forbids a person standing above another reviewing their decision, and a founder who re-interviews the domain lead's finalist and can overturn the lead is that person. The answer is to make the founder's part a stage, not a review: a scored stage in the packet, with its own reads, submitted independently like every other, and read by the domain lead who decides. Pre-opening, it is the last stage for every seat, because there is no inside yet and the founders are the only people who have held the record long enough to read the non-negotiables against a person. It runs in the check-in's form of length and place, on the clock for the candidate where the candidate is already in the house's pay, and it reads two things only: the non-negotiables, and the fourth read (what this person needs from the building, and whether the founders know the building can give it).

The amorphous no does not survive. She herself says to "eventually work to build that filter into your process" (Book p.171); Sŏn builds it in from the first hire and never models it. A no needs a named read. The record supplies the read that the "just fine" case is usually reaching for: "whether the building is equipped to create the environment this person needs to succeed" (WP p.18). A founder who cannot name a competency and cannot name what the building cannot supply has no no to give. That is a stricter rule than hers, and it is the record's.

Her question of when founders step back is answered by the cadence. Once dinner is open and its first quarter has run, the mechanism reset (Session 4) reads the founder stage against its record and cost, and the team's recommendation for that reset is that the founder stage ends for edge seats and continues for lead seats permanently, since the leads are the center's peers and their dinner in the room already has the partners at the table.

**P53 (Recommended).** Pre-opening, the founder stage is the last scored stage for every seat, reading the non-negotiables and the fourth read only, submitted independently, and decided on by the domain lead like every other scorecard. A founder's no names a read or is not a no. The amorphous "just fine" no is not modeled and does not exist.

**P54 (Recommended).** At the mechanism reset after dinner's first quarter, the founder stage ends for edge seats and continues for lead seats. The reset ratifies it against the stage's record.

### 10.2 Leadership recruiting, for the two leads

Her leadership section is built for a company that discovers it needs a leader it cannot describe: "if you're hiring for a new position, you probably don't know exactly what you're looking for" (Book p.184). Her method is to interview the field, build the rubric from what successful holders of the role say, assemble a list of profiles, and never calibrate the role through candidates (Book p.184 to 189).

The record has already answered her first question. "Determine what kind of leader you need" (Book p.183) is answered by the split: an Operations Lead accountable to "labor efficiency, cost targets, compliance, vendor performance, and facility integrity," reading the systems "and accountable to their outputs, not their manual production," and a Maitre d who owns "the how of the experience," "Team development, training, and the invisible advocate function," and "Customer relationship systems and recovery standards" (WP p.10). Neither reports to the other. Those are the seat sheets' fields 2, 5, and 6 for the two seats, in the record's words, and her three questions are answered for both before anyone is interviewed.

What her method still supplies is field 8, how to assess, and her rubric-building conversation is the right way to build it, with one adaptation: the field to interview is small, because the seats do not exist elsewhere in the form the record describes. A General Manager is what the record says not to hire. The Realist's calibration rule applies: Brandon's own lineage is the reference set, and any assessment criterion that rests on a practice at Coqodaq, Alinea, or Gracious is Brandon's to confirm, not the team's to assert. The team recommends the rubric be built by interviewing people who have held one half of the split in a room the founders respect, service directors for the Maitre d's half and operations leads for the other, and by asking her questions (Book p.187): what they were accountable for, what made them qualified, what the first year's hardest problem was, and how they worked with the person beside them rather than above them.

Her experience trap and her playbook thinker (Book p.189) are sharper here than in her book. The record's structure is unusual on purpose, and the most experienced candidate is the one most likely to arrive with the General Manager's playbook: to resolve a cross-domain problem by taking it, to assign, to review from above, and to ask on the first day who they report to. Those are not faults of character; they are the load-bearing model working correctly in the wrong building. The rubric tests for the opposite: a candidate who can hold a domain with no apex above and none below, who reads a problem as a system question first, and who can say what they would refuse to do. Her signal for it, "raw curiosity and signs of pure learning aptitude" (Book p.189), and her caution, "the more experienced someone is, the better they probably are at being interviewed" (Book p.189), both hold. The dinner in the room, or its pre-opening substitute (P37), is where a playbook shows: a candidate who manages the server is telling the partners how they will manage the floor.

Search firms. She reports mixed results and a rule: they "need to intimately understand your company stage" and the role, and "it's better to reach out to your own network, your board, and other advisers" first (Book p.190 to 191). The team recommends no search firm for the two leads: a firm cannot position a seat that does not exist in the market's vocabulary, the documented build already reaches the audience a firm would call, and the partners are "ultimately the recruiter" (Book p.191) for the seats that will sit beside them. It is founder-gated because it is the partners' call and because it is a spend, which this program does not price.

**P55 (Recommended).** The two leads' seat sheets carry the record's own definitions (WP p.10) as fields 2, 5, and 6. Field 8 is built by her method, interviewing holders of each half of the split in rooms the founders respect, with any lineage-specific criterion confirmed by Brandon. The rubric tests for holding a domain without an apex and for refusing the General Manager's playbook.

**P56 (Founder-gated).** Whether a search firm is used for the two leads. The team recommends none, for the reasons stated. The partners close it.

### 10.3 The third seat, argued both ways

Carryover C2 binds this section: the design of third-seat recruitment cannot proceed until the founders rule whether the seat is a founding partner or an executive hire (`86akh2qrp`, ruling filed at `86akh3tw9`). The record disagrees with itself. The white paper: "the chef partner owns the culinary vision as an equal founding partner" (WP p.3); whether lunch is built "is an equal decision among all three partners, the chef partner included" (WP p.36). ## 11. Internal succession, and what she gets wrong for a single house

### 11.1 Her one-third rule, and the house it was written for

"For early-stage companies growing quickly, my experience has been that at least one-third of promotions should come from within," one-third from outside, and "The final third is a toss-up" (Book p.190). She is honest that the rule serves a company that "is unlikely to have the talent pool it needs to hire internally only" because it is adding people faster than it can grow them (Book p.190). Her warning, "Don't assume the folks who got you here will get you there" (Book p.191), is a warning about scale: the job changes under the person as the company grows past them.

Sŏn is not that house. It "compounds without headcount growth" (ledger), and its growth is not people but periods: dinner, then the early morning, then late night, then, if ever, lunch (WP p.36). The job does not grow past a person; the day grows around them. The record's goal is stated as the opposite of her ratio: "over time, leadership roles filled from within by people who have proven the culture and demonstrated the competency, to the point where the building rarely hires a manager from outside at all" (WP p.19). A ratio would also be a lagging indicator, a count of what already happened. The record measures succession as a leading one: "internal promotion rate" is committed under cultural impact measurement (WP p.23), and the mechanism that produces it, the invisible advocate and the ready-now pool, is a structure, not a target.

**P59 (Recommended).** Her one-third rule does not apply. Sŏn sets no ratio for internal against external hiring. The succession commitment is a mechanism, the invisible advocate and the ready-now pool, and its measure is the record's internal promotion rate, read as a leading indicator.

### 11.2 The tension at opening

There is no inside at opening. Every person in the pre-opening cohort is an outside hire by definition, including the two leads, and the record's succession commitment has nothing to run on until the cohort has been in the house long enough for the first unlock to fire. The pool is empty on day one. That is not a failure; it is the condition the commitment starts from, and the question is what the cohort owes the first internal promotion and when the commitment starts binding.

What the cohort is. It is the first set of nodes, hired to hold the tension the web needs on the day dinner opens, and it is also the population from which every later lead line is meant to come. Both things are true of the same people, so the cohort is hired against the seat sheet for the seat and onboarded against the sheet's first unlock, from the first day. The dinner cohort is also the pool from which the early morning's and late night's seats will be held when those periods open, because one pool staffs the periods (P10).

What the cohort owes the first internal promotion. Three things, and recruiting owes the first two before the cohort arrives. The documented competency requirements exist for every seat on the inventory, including the seats that do not open until a later period, so that a person in the house on the first day can "open the system and see it" (WP p.11) for the seat they want next, not only the seat they hold. The invisible advocate runs from the first day: the set period after which the next skill set unlocks (WP p.11) is fixed on the sheet before anyone is hired, so the clock is running on the cohort from the first shift. And the ready-now pool exists as a list on the stack from the first day, empty, with its reading rule written: on any departure, the pool is read first (WP p.14).

When the commitment starts binding. The team's position is that it binds from the first tension loss after dinner opens. On the first departure, the domain lead reads the pool before opening the seat externally (P14's range). If the pool holds someone on the track, that person's practical assessment is the next stage, not an external search. If the pool is empty, the seat opens externally and the sheet says why. For lead seats, the rule is stronger: an outside hire for any lead seat after dinner's first quarter requires a written line on the partners' page stating that no one in the pool is on the track and how far the nearest person is, so that the house's own record shows whether the succession commitment is real or stated. That is the record's own test applied to itself: "retention and internal promotion are direct evidence the development commitments are real rather than stated" (WP p.23).

The Realist's condition on the pool. Ready-now must mean able to reproduce the station at tempo, not able to pass a calm assessment. A person surfaced from the pool for a critical-path seat takes the seat's paid practical in a real service before they hold it, exactly as an external candidate would, because the floor will not honor a paper readiness. That is the one place an internal candidate runs a recruiting stage, and it is a stage, scored, not a formality.

**P60 (Recommended).** The cohort is hired against the seat and onboarded against the first unlock. Before the cohort arrives, every inventory seat's competency requirements exist, the unlock period is fixed on each sheet, and the ready-now pool exists with its reading rule. The succession commitment binds from the first tension loss after dinner opens: the pool is read first, and an outside hire for a lead seat after dinner's first quarter carries a written reason on the partners' page.

**P61 (Recommended).** A person surfaced from the ready-now pool for a critical-path seat takes that seat's paid practical in a real service before holding it, scored like any candidate's.

### 11.3 Cross-period fluency

The record names one development marker, cross-strand fluency, and pays for it (WP p.11). The one-restaurant thesis, "one restaurant and one brand, one brand texture, met at different times of day" (WP p.33), and canon's "One system, four moods" (Canon v1.0, section 03) both require the same people to hold different registers at different hours: the server whose jacket comes off at late night (Canon v1.0, section 08), the person who is at the window at seven and on the floor at seven. The record does not name that as a marker and does not say whether it is recognized. It is a silence (section 15), and it bears on recruiting because the seat sheet's service-period field (P10) is where a person's second period would be recorded, and on succession because the first period expansions are the first succession events: the early morning's lead line is most likely to come from the dinner cohort.

The team recommends naming cross-period fluency as a development marker parallel to cross-strand fluency, visible on the person page as connections, and routes whether it is recognized in compensation to Session 13 and its structural placement to Session 8.

**P62 (Recommended).** Cross-period fluency is named as a development marker parallel to cross-strand fluency and recorded on the person page. Whether it is recognized in compensation is Session 13's; its structural placement is Session 8's.

## 12. Decision rights for recruiting

Every entry below is in the ledger's form: owner; range stated as may and may not; triggers, each naming its destination. ### 12.1 Opening a seat

### 12.2 Running each stage

Owner. The application: the stack. The phone screen: a trained interviewer from the seat's domain, drawn by the stack from the trained pool, not the domain lead. The first interview: a trained interviewer from the seat's strand; a second from the adjacent domain for domain-owner seats; the domain lead may be one of the two and then the second is required. A runner may not score a read the stage does not assess; may not decide a hire; may not discuss a candidate with another runner before both scorecards are submitted; may not discuss a candidate outside Greenhouse; may not run a stage for a person they referred; may not run a stage without a training record; may not state a figure beyond the transparency sheet.

### 12.3 Declining a candidate

Owner. At any stage, the stage's runner, for a read the stage assesses. At the packet, the domain lead, for any read. A founder, at the founder stage or at the dinner in the room, for the non-negotiables or the fourth read only.

Triggers. ## 14. Instrument specifications

Specifications, never instruments. Nothing here is built, and no session builds any of it until the document methodology exists (ClickUp task `86ajgn2z5`). Each specification is written so a downstream cluster can build the instrument without asking a question: what it is, whether Sŏn uses it, what it must contain field by field, what it must do, what it must refuse to do, where it lives and whether that is configuration or a build, who owns it and on what revision rhythm, and what it is gated on. "Configuration" means the tool does it as bought; "build" means something is made in the mesh or on the team home; "to confirm" means the team has not verified the tool's surface and the setup will. Nothing here asserts a tool feature as fact.

The instruments come in the order a seat moves through them: the inventory and the sheet that open it, the posting and the application that fill it, the sheet the candidate is handed, the scorecards and the packet that decide it, the answers and the records that close it, and the entries that hand the person to the check-in. The instruments the record refuses come last, each with what does its work instead.

**I1. The seat inventory.** What it is: the versioned list of every seat the house holds or will hold, by service period, from which alone a seat may be opened. Whether Sŏn uses it: yes, and the record does not hold one (section 15); the page adds it because P14's range ("may not open a seat that is not on the inventory") has nothing to refer to without it. Must contain, per seat: the working name and the note that role naming is open (WP p.15); the strand; the domain; the domain lead named by seat, or "held" with the open question named for runner and host seats; the service period or periods, each tagged with its charter state (drafted, ruled, built, tested, opened); the seat's class or classes from P3; the count of that seat the period's charter calls for; the sheet's version number once a sheet exists; and the seat's state: drafted, open, filled, held.
