# Session 6: Hiring

This page is the Sŏn answer to the Hiring section of Chapter 3 (Book p.193 to 215), with the Chapter 3 workbook read for its interview framework and rubric, its sample questions, its written exercise, and its candidate review framework (Workbook p.43 to 77). It is written to be run from. A person who has not read the book can read this page and know what happens once a candidate is in a seat's pipeline: what each stage asks, who asks it, what counts as evidence, what is written down, how the decision is made, what the candidate is told, and what the process refuses to conclude about a person.

Every position on this page is numbered from P1 and carries exactly one mark. (Recommended) is the team's position, ready for founder ratification. (Founder-gated) requires input the team cannot supply. (Chef-gated) is left open for the chef partner. (Team-filled) is populated once the team exists. Nothing on this page is final. A recommended position is a recommendation until Brandon ratifies it, and a founder-gated position is argued to the ceiling one founder can reach and then marked for what Dominic, the chef, or the team must close.

Vocabulary follows the ledger and Session 5. "Operating system," unqualified, is the human system of strategy, accountability, and cadence; the software is "the stack" or "the OS surface." The customer is the customer, in every stage and every document. Service periods are named by time of day. A "seat" is a node in the web with a domain, a strand, and documented competency requirements; the "seat sheet" is the sixteen-field artifact that opens it (S5 P12); its field 8 is the assessment rubric this page fills (S5 P67). "The four reads" are competency for the strand at entry level, growth potential as self-directed mastery, cultural alignment as the non-negotiables plus comfort with distributed authority and the customer as customer, and what the building must supply for this person to succeed (S5 P5). "The packet" is the complete set of independently submitted scorecards on which the domain lead decides (S5 P46). Section 6 gives the four reads a second name, as the scorecard's four dimensions, because that is the form Session 13 inherits.

Financials are not a context source for this program. Her chapter carries salary bands, a compa-ratio worked example, a pay parity review, and the claim that compensation is a company's largest expense (Book p.199 to 202). None of it enters this page, as quotation, illustration, or range. Where a decision needs a figure, the page says the figure is not a context source and states the gap. Her line on page 214 that ends in profanity is referred to and not quoted.

Two tools are named because Brandon named them: Greenhouse is the applicant tracking system, retained over Rippling's native hiring module; Rippling is the single sign-on and human-resources layer. This page says, for every mechanism it places in a tool, whether it is configuration, a build, or to confirm. Nothing on this page asserts a tool feature as fact. ## 1. Her ask, and what Sŏn already holds

### 1.1 Her ask

Her hiring section opens on a claim and a discipline. The claim: "interviewing is a skill that improves with practice" and is "best employed within a set process, with clear frameworks for assessment" (Book p.193). The discipline is small and exact: "a relatively simple framework for what all interviews should focus on, a clear sense of the tactical steps a candidate will experience, and a plan for how a decision is reached on making an offer" (Book p.193). She adds the candidate's side, that it is "critical to prep your candidates well," not by telling them the questions but by "setting them at ease" with the process, its length, the interview types, the dress code, and who they will meet (Book p.193).

Her process guidelines run as a list (Book p.193 to 194): a clear job description; decide who interviews; a ceiling of eight interviewers, "reduced to four or five" for entry-level and well-understood roles; trained interviewers; the rubric shared with every interviewer and "one or two elements of the rubric" assigned to each "to avoid overlap"; a kickoff for custom roles; a first round of three to narrow before the panel; a hiring committee or candidate review "to finalize your selection"; references checked by recruiter and hiring manager; the offer. Complexity "can flex up or down" with volume, and for high-volume roles "you may even be able to eventually forgo hiring committee meetings" (Book p.194).

Interviewing education: STAR as a framework; consistency as "the most effective interviewing tactic," meaning "the same questions of every candidate, while also leaving space in the interview to probe" (Book p.194); four common mistakes, not using the rubric, not interviewing for the capabilities the role most needs, assessing liking rather than success in the role, and over-prioritizing "the right candidate experience rather than the right trajectory and aptitude to learn" (Book p.195); the five things the best interviews suss out (Book p.195); her self-awareness and learning test, and the "I" versus "we" read (Book p.195); the three question types, situational, behavioral, and strict competency (Book p.195); working company values into behavioral questions without naming them (Book p.196); the written exercise (Book p.196); and the candidate experience as the one thing most candidates will ever know of the company (Book p.196).

Non-leader roles: scaled roles with a wide interviewer pool and a hiring manager or proxy, less frequent roles with "the same people interview all of the candidates" and a cross-section of functions on the panel (Book p.196); the Bar Raiser and Elevate programs, with Elevate's selection built from ATS feedback histories (Book p.197 to 198); the Stripe offer-approval channel with a twenty-four-hour turnaround (Book p.197); decision-making as consultative with the hiring manager as primary decision-maker and "checks and balances" such as executive or committee review (Book p.198); job level selection and everything that follows from it, which this page does not carry (Book p.198 to 202); candidate selection at Stripe with a live committee only on disagreement, newness, or uniqueness, the recruiter measured on funnel efficiency and hire quality, and the norm that "anyone on the hiring committee can veto a hire" (Book p.203); the four goals of whoever stewards the process, guard the candidate experience, really listen to interviewers, bring the organization along, decide for the company (Book p.204); the candidate review sidebar at full length (Book p.204 to 207); reference checking, with the rule that "Not making reference calls is a mistake," the lukewarm-reference rule, the percentile question, and seven behavioral reference questions (Book p.207 to 208); and the offer, verbal before written, nothing a surprise, documents automated early, the offer letter as a legal document, and the sell chat (Book p.208 to 209).

Leadership: transparency inside the company and its limits, layering, confidentiality for the candidate, the decision as the CEO's on a consultative process, the whole thing run as change management, the steps (two important people excited, stakeholders identified, a loop of stakeholders, deliberation as a feedback-collection forum, the announcement), the experience trap in the interview, hiring disagreements in both directions, and references and offers for leaders (Book p.209 to 215). Hiring internally: one candidate or several, no sham process, friction sussed out, external candidates met and said so, a lightweight internal loop with a feedback loop for the unselected, confidentiality, and the announcement (Book p.213 to 214).

The workbook supplies the worked form: one role's full framework, a recruiter screen, a team screen run in tandem with a written project, and four onsite interviews each with named competencies, named runners, a length, documented questions with situation, action, and result follow-ups, and a three- or four-column rubric with anchors (Workbook p.43 to 66); the sample questions with what each seeks (Workbook p.67 to 69); the written exercise with its twelve-point grading (Workbook p.70 to 72); and the candidate review decision-making framework in five steps (Workbook p.73 to 77). Pages 78 onward belong to Sessions 7 and 15 and are not read here.

## 2. What an interview is for at Sŏn

### 2.1 Her five things, tested against the four reads

"The best interviews suss out how someone: Works with other people. Gets quality work done themselves. Motivates and develops themselves. Has or can develop the expertise needed for the role. Demonstrates leadership and resilience" (Book p.195). Five things, written for a company where a hire may be a manager and the interview is the whole assessment. Sŏn's interview is one stage among several, and its reads are fixed at four. The test is whether her five are already inside the four or whether one is missing.

Works with other people. In a load-bearing organization this is a personality trait. In a tensegrity web it is the structure itself: "The strength of the whole lives in the relationships between elements" (WP p.8), and the inward-facing strand's intelligence is "architectural and coordinated" (WP p.11), the outward-facing strand's "relational" (WP p.10). So working with others is not a fifth read; it is inside the first, because the strand's intelligence is a way of working with adjacent nodes, and inside the third, because "No Disposable People" (Canon v1.0, section 01) is a rule about how a person treats the people around them, including the steward and the runner who will never be above them.

Gets quality work done themselves. At entry level this is the practical's read for physical-skill seats, "procedure mastery and reproduction under load" (S5 P41), and the first read's evidence for every seat: a specific thing done, described specifically.

Motivates and develops themselves. This is the second read whole: "growth potential, read as evidence of self-directed mastery" (S5 3.1). The record's advancement system "favors the people who want it most" (WP p.11), and the interview reads for prior evidence of wanting something enough to have mastered it without being asked.

Has or can develop the expertise. "Has" is the first read at entry; "can develop" is the second read plus the seat sheet's field 7, what the house develops, which the interviewer does not score (S5 P12). Her "or" is exactly the record's split between the two horizons of field 6.

Demonstrates leadership and resilience. Leadership is not a read for an edge seat, because there is no altitude to lead from and a functional designation is "not a permanent elevation" (WP p.10). For the two lead seats it is competency for that seat, which S5 P55 defined as holding a domain without an apex, and section 3.5 gives it a question block. Resilience, read as holding up under load, is the practical's read, "reproduction under load," and the interview does not score it; a candidate can describe a hard night, and that description is evidence for the second read (what they learned) and the third (who they blamed), never a score for resilience itself, because the room is the only honest test of it and the practical is the room.

So her five map onto the four with one thing left over, and the leftover is deliberate: an interview at Sŏn does not read leadership at the edge and does not read resilience in a chair.

**P3 (Recommended).** Her five things are inside the four reads: working with others inside competency for the strand and the non-negotiables; getting work done inside competency and the practical; self-motivation is the second read whole; expertise present or developable is field 6's two horizons; leadership is competency for a lead seat only; resilience is the practical's load read and is never scored in an interview. No read is added.

### 2.2 Her self-awareness test, and the "I" and "we" read

"The best way to test for self-awareness is to ask a candidate how their colleagues would describe them. If they only say positive things, probe what constructive feedback they've received. Then ask, 'And what have you done to improve?'" (Book p.195). Eric Yuan's line beside it, that the number one thing is "Do they really want to learn?" (Book p.195), is the record's own bar for the second read. The three-part question is the best single instrument in her chapter for self-directed mastery, because its third part asks for an action the candidate took on their own account after hearing something they did not want to hear, and that is the behavior the invisible advocate rewards. It becomes the anchor question for the second read at every first interview, for every seat, in the same three parts and the same order (section 4.4).

"Watch for how much they use 'I' or 'we.' Too much 'I' is a flag that they may not be humble or collaborative ... Too much 'we' may obscure what role they played" (Book p.195). She prefers "we." At Sŏn the read is different in a specific way. The web's rigid anchor points are "domain ownership: small, specific, explicitly defined, interlocking" (WP p.9). A person who cannot say what they held is not describing a node; they are describing a team they stood inside. So "we" is the expected register and "what did you hold" is the required probe, every time, and the answer to the probe is evidence for the first read (did they own something specific) and for the third (did they credit the adjacent nodes). Her own example of the good answer, "It was my idea, but the credit goes to the whole team" (Book p.195), holds both. Her example of the less good, "I just did what everyone else decided" (Book p.195), fails distributed authority, because a person who will be handed a range and expected to act inside it has just said they wait to be told.

**P4 (Recommended).** Her three-part self-awareness question is the anchor question for the second read at every first interview, asked in her order. The "I" and "we" read is recorded as evidence, not scored as a trait: "we" is the expected register, "what did you hold" is the mandatory probe, and a candidate who can name what they held and credit the nodes beside them has given evidence for the first and third reads at once.

### 2.3 What an interview may conclude, and what it may not

An interview at Sŏn is one stage, run by a trained person, against a seat sheet, producing a scorecard with evidence against four reads. That sentence sets what it may conclude.

It may conclude that evidence for a read was heard or was not, against the entry bar the sheet states, and it may say what the evidence was. It may conclude that a disqualifier from field 9 was present, with the evidence. It may record, in the candidate's words, what the building must supply. It may recommend advance or decline, with the read named. That is all.

It may not conclude nunchi, because nunchi is "Developed through mastery" (Canon v3.0, p.15) and the house has taught the candidate nothing yet (S5 P6). It may not conclude Jeong, which "requires time, repetition, and memory" (Canon v1.0, section 01). It may not conclude presence, charm, energy, or how the room would feel about the person; those are the outgoing-profile distortion carryover `86ajgnhh3` warns about, and section 4.9 says what in the loop catches them. It may not conclude "fit," which is not a read (S5 P5). It may not conclude a level, a rate, or a place on any scale, because Sŏn has none (section 12). It may not conclude that the candidate can reproduce a procedure at tempo; only the practical can (S5 P41). It may not conclude anything about a competency the house develops (field 7); an interviewer who scores field 7 as absent has scored the house's own obligation as the candidate's failure. It may not conclude that the candidate will succeed in month six; the ninety-day plan and the check-in are where that is learned, and an interview that claims it has confused prediction with assessment. It may not read the candidate's format choice as anything (S5 P31, 15.12). And it may not conclude an offer, imply one, state a figure beyond the transparency sheet, or ask what the candidate earns elsewhere (S5 P40).

Two of those refusals are stricter than hers and are the record's. She allows an interviewer to weigh "the right trajectory and aptitude to learn" against experience (Book p.195); at Sŏn experience is not on the sheet at all, so there is nothing to weigh it against. She allows the founder's amorphous no on the candidate who is "just fine" (Book p.171); Session 5 removed it, and a no with no read is a re-training trigger (S5 P53, P52).

**P5 (Recommended).** An interview at Sŏn may conclude whether evidence for each assigned read was heard against the entry bar, whether a disqualifier was present, what the candidate said they need from the building, and a recommendation with a read named. It may not conclude nunchi, Jeong, presence, fit, a level, tempo reproduction, a developed competency's absence, month-six success, anything from format choice, or an offer.

### 2.4 Her four mistakes as the four failure modes

Her four most common interviewer mistakes (Book p.195) are, at Sŏn, the four failure modes the training names and the calibration read looks for.

Not using the rubric, "asking different questions of different candidates, and therefore never finding a way to benchmark an excellent answer." At Sŏn the question set is on the seat sheet's field 8 and the interview kit is configured from it; the stack shows the questions in order; the calibration read checks whether every question was asked (row 68). A scorecard with a blank question line and no reason is the tell.

Not interviewing for the capabilities most needed. At Sŏn the reads per stage are fixed and the anchors say what evidence looks like for the seat's strand. The tell is evidence recorded under the wrong read, or evidence for a competency the house develops.

Assessing liking instead of success in the role. This is the mistake the room produces: service pressure "selects hard for the outgoing profile" (carryover `86ajgnhh3`), and an interviewer the room rewarded will like the candidate the room would reward. The tell is a presence word on a scorecard, and section 4.9 makes the scan for them a check.

Over-prioritizing candidate experience over trajectory and aptitude. At Sŏn this one is structurally blocked, not trained against: the sheet refuses "A years-of-experience requirement" and "A credential where a competency is meant" (S5 P13), and no anchor names experience. An interviewer who reaches for it has nothing on the sheet to write it against. Her version of the trap in the leadership section, "the more experienced someone is, the better they probably are at being interviewed" (Book p.189), returns in section 13 with its counter-questions.

**P6 (Recommended).** Her four mistakes are the four interviewer failure modes the training names and the calibration read scans for: the unasked question, the misfiled read, the presence word, and the experience reach. The fourth is blocked by the seat sheet rather than trained against.

## 3. The loop, stage by stage

Session 5 fixed the stages, the reads per stage, the runners, and what each stage may decide (S5 section 8). This section fills each stage with what happens inside it: its length, what the candidate is told beforehand, what the interviewer receives beforehand, the documented questions by reference to section 4, the answer anchors by reference, what the scorecard records, and what the stage may not conclude. Her candidate-prep and interviewer-prep guidance (Book p.193 to 194) are placed first because they run before every stage.

### 3.1 What the candidate is told before every stage

She sent candidates a guide "to let them know what to expect from the interview process and their onsite interviews," and "Candidates often shared their appreciation for this guide" (Book p.193). The record makes the guide a system property: the applicant system "tells them exactly what to expect at each step" (WP p.17). Session 5 placed it as the stage-expectation note in the candidate answer set (S5 I15). This page fills the note's content per stage.

Every stage's note states: what the stage is and what it is for; how long it will take, stated as a length the house then keeps to; who will be there, by seat, never by title implying altitude; the format and language the candidate chose, confirmed; what the candidate will be asked to do, in kind (questions about prior work; a scenario; a station with a person beside them; a dinner as a customer); what the stage reads, in the seat sheet's words, so the candidate knows the four reads before they walk in; what the stage does not read, in one line (for the practical, that presence is not scored; for the dinner, that the server is not scored); what to wear, and for the practical what the house supplies and what the candidate brings; how the candidate will hear the outcome and by when, which is the five-day window (S5 P29); and that they may bring questions and will be given time for them. It does not state the questions themselves, which is her rule (Book p.193), and it does not state the anchors.

For the first interview the note also carries the transparency sheet, sent ahead so the candidate arrives having read it, and states that the sheet will be handed again at the interview and walked through (section 3.3). For the practical the note carries the hours, the station or section, the service or training service and its date, whether it runs with load, who will work beside the candidate, and that it is paid, at what rhythm, and through what (S5 I11). For the dinner in the room the note carries the room and date, that the partners will be at the table, that the candidate is a customer for the evening and will be treated as one, and that nothing is decided at the table (S5 P45).

The Realist's condition on the note: what it promises, the floor keeps. A practical note that says three hours and a station with the seat holder beside them is a promise the schedule has to carry on the day; a note that promises a first interview of a stated length has promised the interviewer's time on the clock. The note is therefore generated from the schedule, not the other way around, and a note goes only when the stack has placed the stage.

**P7 (Recommended).** The stage-expectation note carries, for every stage, what it is, its length, who is there by seat, the confirmed format and language, what the candidate will do, what is read and what is not, what to wear, how and when they will hear, and that they may ask questions; never the questions or the anchors. For the first interview it carries the transparency sheet ahead. The note is generated from the placed stage and never promises what the schedule does not hold.

### 3.2 What the interviewer receives before every stage

She sends interviewers "the job description, and the key capabilities you're seeking" and, for leadership loops, "a description of the leader's background, who has met the person already, and some brief feedback, but not so much that you create bias" (Book p.212). Session 5 made prep the house's duty: "Prep material for a stage reaches the interviewer before the stage, on the OS surface, in their language" (S5 9.2).

The prep note carries: the seat sheet, current version; the stage's interview kit, meaning the documented questions in order and the anchors per read (section 4); the reads this stage assesses and no other; the must-haves and disqualifiers from fields 6 and 9; the candidate's chosen language and format; the stage-expectation note the candidate received, so the interviewer knows what was promised; what the candidate has already been handed (the transparency sheet version, if the first interview has run); the candidate's fourth-read answer from a prior stage, because the building's obligations are cumulative and a practical assessor should know what accommodation was named; and the interviewer's own training record's currency.

It never carries another interviewer's scorecard, another interviewer's recommendation, the phone screen's line beyond the must-haves marked, or anything a prior runner concluded about the person. Her "brief feedback, but not so much that you create bias" is refused in favor of none: independence before submission is the mechanism that replaces the committee (S5 P46), and it does not survive a prep note that says what the last person thought. The stack enforces the order (S5 9.2), and it enforces it here too: a scorecard is visible to another runner only after that runner's own scorecard is submitted, and the calibration read (section 8) is the only place scorecards are read side by side before a decision.

**P8 (Recommended).** The interviewer prep note carries the sheet, the kit, the reads assigned, the must-haves and disqualifiers, the candidate's choices, what the candidate was told and handed, the cumulative fourth-read answer, and the interviewer's training currency. It never carries another runner's scorecard or conclusion. The stack hides every other scorecard on the candidate until the runner's own is submitted.

### 3.3 Her people ceiling, tested against the house

"Seek to have candidates only meet a maximum of eight people in the process ... If the role is more entry-level and very well understood, this could be reduced to four or five people" (Book p.193). Her ceiling is about diminishing returns per interviewer. At Sŏn the number of people a candidate meets is set by the seat's class, not by a ceiling, and the counts are these. A high-volume edge seat with a practical, pre-opening: the phone screener, the first interviewer, the practical's assessor and the seat holder or mentor beside them, and a partner at the founder stage: five people, four scorecards. The same seat after the mechanism reset ends the founder stage for edge seats (S5 P54): four people, three scorecards. A domain-owner seat adds one, the adjacent-domain interviewer. A lead seat pre-opening: a partner at the first interview, both partners at the substitute dinner, a partner at the founder stage: two people across three stages, with at least three scorecards; once a co-lead exists, three people. The chef seat under either branch: the two founders, and whoever's kitchen the cooking runs in.

So every seat sits under her ceiling and under her reduced number for well-understood roles, and the risk at Sŏn runs the other way: not too many reads but too few independent ones. The structural floor is that no packet is one person's read. Under Session 5's rules that floor holds for every class the inventory currently carries: every edge seat has a practical or a founder stage or both; every domain-owner seat has two first interviewers; every lead seat has a partner and a substitute dinner with both partners. It holds structurally rather than by a new rule, and this page adds no runner to secure it. If Session 8's inventory ever creates a seat whose class produces a one-scorecard packet, a seat with no physical skill, no leadership, and no adjacent domain, the founders decide whether that seat's first interview takes a second interviewer, and the question is filed to Session 8 rather than answered here by analogy.

Her "kickoff meeting in which everyone gets aligned on the role, the rubric, and their part" (Book p.194) has a home only in the sense that the seat sheet is the kickoff: it is read by every runner as prep, and a meeting to align on it would be a meeting about a document everyone can read. Her "first round of three interviews to narrow the field" before "the remaining three interviewers meet the finalists" (Book p.194) is her structure for a bespoke role with a panel; Sŏn's stages already narrow in sequence (phone screen, first interview, practical or dinner), and no seat has a panel to protect.

**P9 (Recommended).** Her interviewer ceiling is met by every seat class and is not the house's constraint; the house's constraint is the floor, that no packet is one person's read, which holds structurally under Session 5's runners for every seat the inventory carries. No runner is added. A future seat class that would produce a one-scorecard packet is routed to Session 8 for the founders to decide.

### 3.4 The phone screen, filled

Reads and runner per S5 P39: must-haves and availability only, run by a trained interviewer from the seat's domain who is not the domain lead, declining only for a reason on the sheet. Length: the record's "a few minutes" (WP p.17); the team sets the scheduled block at fifteen minutes, the check-in's length, so it is one unit on the schedule the stack already knows how to place, and the call itself is as long as the items take. The candidate has been told, in the note, that it is short and what it checks.

The documented items, in order, and every item asked of every candidate for the seat: a one-line statement of the seat and the strand in the sheet's words, so the candidate can say at once that they applied for something else; each must-have from field 6 as an open question (not "do you have X" but "tell me about the last time you did X"), marked present, absent, or not asked; availability against field 11's shift shape, period by period, asked as what they can work rather than whether they can work what the sheet says; the seat's legal requirements from field 9 as yes-or-no, asked once; the language and format they want the first interview in, confirmed; and the candidate's questions, with time held for them. The screener answers questions about the process and the sheet, and answers nothing about pay beyond that the transparency sheet carries the commitments and will be handed at the first interview.

What it records, per S5 I8. What it may not conclude: any of the four reads beyond the must-haves; presence, voice, manner, or accent; enthusiasm; whether the person "sounds like" the house; anything from the fact that they chose a phone screen. The Realist's note: the phone screen is the stage where the outgoing-profile distortion enters cheapest, because a voice on a phone is nothing but presence, and the fifteen-minute block leaves no room for a story to correct a first impression. The scorecard's field for manner does not exist, and the calibration read scans phone-screen declines for reasons that are not on the sheet.

**P10 (Recommended).** The phone screen's documented items are the seat statement, each must-have as an open question, availability by period, the legal requirements, the next stage's language and format, and the candidate's questions, in that order, scheduled as a fifteen-minute block. It concludes nothing about manner or voice, and a decline for a reason not on the sheet is a calibration flag.

### 3.5 The first interview, filled

Reads and runners per S5 P40: the four reads at the depth field 8 assigns, one trained interviewer from the strand, a second from the adjacent domain for domain-owner seats, a partner and the co-lead for lead seats, and two interviewers whenever the domain lead is one. Scorecards submitted independently before discussion. The transparency sheet handed.

Length. Her workbook's stages run thirty to forty-five minutes with "5 to 10 for candidate to ask questions" (Workbook p.44, p.46). The team sets the first interview at forty-five minutes for edge seats, sixty for domain-owner and lead seats, on the schedule as scheduled shift for the interviewer (S5 9.2), with the last ten minutes held for the candidate's questions and the walk-through of the sheet. Length is a parameter for the mechanism reset, and the rule that matters more than the number is that the block is kept: a first interview cut short because service called the interviewer away is re-run, at the house's cost in time and the candidate's convenience, exactly as an untrained-runner stage is (S5 12.2).

Structure, in order, and the same order for every candidate for the seat. One background question, hers, "at least one résumé-based question is a good way to break the ice" (Book p.195), asked as "tell me about the work you have been doing most recently," and never as a résumé walk. Then the first read, competency for the strand, one situational and one strict-competency question at least (section 4.3). Then the second read, growth potential, opening with her three-part self-awareness question (section 4.4). Then the third read, cultural alignment, with its behavioral questions that let the candidate bring up the customer, the team, and the process without being asked to (section 4.5). Then the fourth read, what the building must supply, asked last and asked plainly, because by then the candidate has heard what the seat is and can answer it honestly (section 4.6). Then the transparency sheet, handed physically in the candidate's language, walked through item by item with item 8, the range, read aloud, and item 6 stated as filled or as "being finalized" under P35's ruling. Then the candidate's questions. Then what happens next and by when, spoken.

For domain-owner seats the adjacent-domain interviewer is in the room for the whole interview and asks the section 4.7 block, which is the same reads from the interface's side. For lead seats the section 4.8 block replaces the strand-competency block, because a lead seat's competency is holding a domain without an apex (S5 P55).

What the scorecard records, per S5 I9, with this page's dimension layer (section 6). What the candidate is told about the sheet: that it is the version the offer would be made on and that any change between now and an offer will be stated in the offer (S5 7.4).

What it may not conclude: everything in P5. Two further refusals belong to this stage specifically. The interviewer may not ask the candidate why they left a prior job in a form that invites them to name a person; the question is asked as "what would you have changed about that place," which yields the same evidence for the third read (blame the process or blame a person) without soliciting a story about an individual the house may later hire. And the interviewer may not walk through the range as a warning; item 8 is read as what the seat holds and what the holder decides without asking, because a candidate who hears the range as a list of things they may not do has been told the opposite of what the record means by it.

The Realist's floor read on this stage. Forty-five minutes on scheduled shift is the cost, and pre-opening it is a founder's forty-five minutes at a time when the founders are also running a build. Her forty-hours-a-week of interviewing (Book p.193) is the number to hold in mind: a pre-opening cohort of every floor and kitchen seat, each with a first interview, and only the founders and then two leads to run them, is a load the hiring calendar (section 3.9) has to place as blocks, not as afternoons that turn out to be free. A first interview run between build meetings by a founder who has not read the prep note is an untrained stage in every sense but the record.

**P11 (Recommended).** The first interview runs forty-five minutes for edge seats and sixty for domain-owner and lead seats, on scheduled shift, in a fixed order: one background question, the first read, the second read opening on her three-part question, the third read, the fourth read last, the sheet handed and walked through with the range read aloud, the candidate's questions, and what happens next. A cut-short interview is re-run. The prior-employer question is asked as what the candidate would have changed, and the range is read as what the seat holds, never as a warning.

### 3.6 The adjacent-domain second interviewer, filled

Session 5 added this runner for domain-owner seats because "the adjacent nodes are the ones who will compensate if the node does not hold" (S5 8.1; WP p.9). The second interviewer is a trained person from the domain that receives the seat's output: for a Station Lead, someone from the floor's inward-facing strand or the pass; for the Head of Beverage, someone from the outward-facing floor strand; for the Lead Host as a designation, someone from the server seats. They assess the same four reads and no other, and their block asks about the interface: what the candidate needs from the seat next to theirs, what they hand on and in what state, and what they do when the adjacent node is short (section 4.7). Their scorecard is a second document, independent, and it carries no separate "interface" read; the interface evidence is filed under the first read, competency for the strand, because a domain owner's competency includes the handoff.

**P12 (Recommended).** The adjacent-domain second interviewer assesses the same four reads from the interface's side, asks the section 4.7 block, files interface evidence under the first read, and submits a second, independent scorecard. No interface read is added.

### 3.7 The lead seats' first interview, filled

For the Operations Lead and the Maitre d the first-read block is the section 4.8 block, built on S5 P55: the candidate must show they can hold a domain with no apex above and none below, read a problem as a system question first, and say what they would refuse to do. Her experience trap governs the technique: "Experienced leaders are usually very good at interviewing ... Don't be swayed" (Book p.214). Her counters are adopted as rules of the block: present a scenario and watch whether the candidate asks about context before answering, because "If you present them a scenario and they simply describe what they've done before and don't ask questions to understand your context, be wary" (Book p.214); ask "And what did you specifically do in that project?" (Book p.214) on every account; and "Don't forget to ask if the project was successful" (Book p.214), watching for "people staying too high-level or obfuscating outcomes" (Book p.214). The runners are a partner and, once one exists, the co-lead (S5 12.2); pre-opening, for the first lead, it is a partner alone at this stage, and the packet's second and third reads come from the substitute dinner with both partners and the founder stage (S5 P37, P53).

**P13 (Recommended).** The lead seats' first interview uses the section 4.8 block in place of the strand-competency block, run under her three experience-trap rules: context questions before answers, "what did you specifically do," and whether it worked. Runners per S5 12.2.

### 3.8 The dinner in the room and the founder stage, filled

The dinner in the room reads the candidate as a customer, their treatment of the server, their read of the room in their own register, and whether they hold the table without performing for the partners (S5 P45). It is not an interview and it has no question set. What it has is a small documented list of things the partners raise, so that two partners' scorecards are comparable: one open question about what the candidate noticed of the service, asked after the second course, to hear whether they read the room as a customer or as an inspector; one question about a night in a room they loved as a customer and why; and one deliberate silence, a stretch of the meal in which the partners do not fill the conversation, because "Copy does not rush to fill silence" (Canon v1.0, section 01) and neither does a lead who can sit at a table. The partners are told, in their prep note, that a candidate who manages the server, corrects the pacing, or narrates the food to the partners is showing them how they will manage the floor (S5 10.2), and that the evidence goes under the first read for a lead seat. Nothing else is documented, because a documented dinner is a scripted one.

The founder stage reads the non-negotiables and the fourth read only (S5 P53), in the check-in's form: fifteen minutes, on the clock where the candidate is already in the house's pay. Its documented questions are three: what the candidate needs from the building to do this work well, asked again even though the first interview asked it, because the answer changes once a practical has been run; whether there is anything about the house's terms, on the sheet they were handed, that they would want to be different, which is the non-negotiables read from the candidate's side (a candidate who wants the house to take something from another person has said something); and one question drawn from the candidate's prior stages' fourth-read answer, to confirm the founders know the building can supply it. The founder's no names a non-negotiable or a fourth-read gap or is not a no (S5 P53).

**P14 (Recommended).** The dinner in the room carries three documented prompts and one documented silence and no question set; playbook behavior at the table is filed under the first read for a lead seat. The founder stage carries three documented questions in the check-in's form, all on the non-negotiables and the fourth read.

### 3.9 The hiring calendar, as gates

Carryovers `86akh7qhr` and `86akh67gj` place the calendar on this page and say what shape it must take: gates, not dates, with three gates upstream of any hire, and a practical never scheduled to make a start date (S5 P42). The calendar is written here as an ordered list of conditions, each with the position or row that names it, and it is the instrument in I14.

Upstream of any hire, in order. The chef seat ruling is filed (S5 P57; row 59), because the timeline places the chef first under either branch and every kitchen sheet, practical, and hire waits on it (S5 P58). The hiring partner is ruled (S4 P4; S5 P49), because that seat opens the first seats and decides the first packets. The seat inventory's owner is ruled and the dinner inventory exists (S5 P15; row 36). The founders' interviewer training is complete and recorded (section 7.4; row 61), because the first stage cannot run without it (row 44). The compensation architecture is ratified, or P35 is ruled yes so that first interviews may run with item 6 reading "being finalized" (S5 P35; row 46), and either way no offer goes before the architecture supplies item 6 (row 47).

The two leads. Their seat sheets complete (S5 P55). Their first interviews, run by a partner with the section 4.8 block. Their substitute dinner, once P37 is ruled (S5 P37; row 56). Their founder stage. Reference notes for both seats (section 10). Their offers, which cannot go until item 6 is filled. Their start dates, set so that both are in the building at least six weeks before dinner's gate (row 56), because the leads' review must run six consecutive weeks with records (row 21) and the pipeline read must run at six consecutive leads' reviews (row 42). Working backward from dinner's gate, the leads' offers therefore fall no later than six weeks plus the notice a lead candidate carries plus the offer's open period (section 11.4) before the gate, and their first interviews fall earlier still by the window and the stages between. That places the compensation architecture's ratification months before dinner's gate, which is the sequencing conflict S5 15.3 named. It is restated here as a gate rather than a finding: the ratification date sits on the hiring calendar ahead of the leads' offers, and if the program's own sequence puts Session 13 after that date, the founders either pull the architecture's ratification forward of Session 13 or accept that the leads' offers wait for it and the six-week condition slides the gate. The kitchen chain, under either branch: ruling, chef seated, canon's Session 1B, the menu, kitchen seat sheets, kitchen practicals in training services, kitchen hires, their training, dinner's gate (S5 P58; row 59). ## 4. The questions, and the anchors

This is the session's center. It specifies, for every read at every stage, what the question set must contain and what the anchors must say, at enough resolution that a downstream cluster builds the interview kits without asking a question. It is a specification and not an instrument: the question forms below are forms, written to show what a question of that kind must ask and what evidence it must yield, and the kit that is built from them is gated on the document methodology (`86ajgn2z5`). Nothing here is a script.

Her workbook's framework is the worked form this section learns from and does not copy. It names competencies per stage, a runner, a length, documented questions, situation-action-result follow-ups, and a rubric in three or four columns from poor to strong (Workbook p.43 to 66). It is built for a recruiter at a technology company, and its competencies (collaboration, conscientiousness, willingness to be wrong, intrinsic motivation, structured thinking, resilience, accountability) are that company's. Sŏn's are the four reads, and the anchors are written for a person entering a seat at the outside edge of a web with no experience requirement on the sheet.

### 4.1 The rules the question set runs under

Consistency. "The same questions of every candidate, while also leaving space in the interview to probe their answers" (Book p.194). Every documented question is asked of every candidate for the seat, in the kit's order, in the candidate's interview language. Probes are free, and the kit lists the probes it expects so that an interviewer who has not thought of one has one. A question not asked is recorded as not asked with a reason; a blank line is a calibration flag (section 2.4).

Types. Every read's set contains at least one situational question ("How would you handle this scenario?"), one behavioral ("Tell me about a challenge you overcame"), and, where the read permits, one strict-competency question ("Describe the last ... you built and how you approached the design") (Book p.195). The fourth read has no strict-competency form, because it is not about the candidate's competence.

Probes take STAR's shape. Situation, task, action, result (Book p.194) is the probe order, and the workbook's follow-up sets (Workbook p.54 to 65) show the form: for every account, what was the situation, what was the candidate's own part, what did they do, what happened. The probe that never fails to be asked is "what did you hold," the "I" and "we" probe from P4.

Principles are worked in unnamed. "If one of your principles is 'Put customers first,' for example, you can ask the candidate to provide examples of work where they might have had to keep the customer top of mind. Don't ask about the customer-facing aspect of the work specifically, just see if they bring customers up at all" (Book p.196). Every third-read behavioral question is built this way: it presents a situation in which the non-negotiables or a principle would show and asks for an account, and the anchor scores whether the behavior appears unprompted.

Entry-level anchors. Every anchor states what evidence looks like for a person entering at the outside edge with no prior restaurant experience required, because the sheet refuses experience (S5 P13). An anchor that can only be met by a person who has worked a fine-dining floor is wrong for every seat except a lead seat, and is wrong there too if it names a house rather than a capacity. The anchors read the capacity the strand needs in any domain the candidate has worked in, a kitchen, a shop, a site, a classroom, a family.

Three levels. Every anchor is written at three levels: below the entry bar, at the entry bar, and beyond it. "At" is what the seat needs on the first day. "Beyond" exists so an interviewer can record evidence past the bar, which the lead reads when two candidates both meet it for one seat; it is never a requirement. "Below" is written as specifically as "at," because an interviewer who knows what a weak answer sounds like stops mistaking fluency for evidence. Her workbook's four-column form, poor, weak, good, strong (Workbook p.47), is collapsed to three because the decision is the lead's on evidence and not a sum, and a fourth gradation invites a composite (S5 I7 refuses "a weighting or a numeric composite").

No anchor names presence. No anchor at any level contains a word for how the candidate came across: energy, warmth, presence, personality, charisma, polish, natural, confident, bubbly, "would fit in." An anchor is about what the candidate said they did and what they would do. The scan for those words on a scorecard is a calibration check (section 4.9).

The candidate's vocabulary is not scored. A candidate who says "guest" has used the industry's word; the house teaches its own (WP p.25; Canon v3.0, p.5) and no anchor scores the word either way. The interviewer says "customer" throughout.

The question set is on the seat sheet's field 8 and is therefore readable by a candidate before the first interview (S5 P13). That is deliberate and it is not her rule: she withholds questions (Book p.193). At Sŏn the questions are public because the competency requirements are public (WP p.11), and a candidate who has read the questions and prepared an account has done the thing the second read looks for. The anchors are not on the sheet; the questions are.

**P17 (Recommended).** The question set runs under eight rules: every question asked of every candidate in order; at least one situational, one behavioral, and where possible one strict-competency question per read; STAR-shaped probes with "what did you hold" always asked; principles worked in unnamed; anchors written at entry level in any domain; three anchor levels; no presence word in any anchor; the candidate's vocabulary unscored. The questions are on the seat sheet and public; the anchors are not.

### 4.2 The anchor's form

Every anchor, at each of its three levels, states four things: what the account contains (a specific instance, a sequence, a named signal); what the candidate's own part in it was; what they did; and what they can say about what happened, including whether it worked. An anchor that cannot be written in those four parts is an anchor for something the interview cannot read, and it is removed rather than softened. The workbook's best anchors already have this shape: "Clearly explains what actions they took and what impact those actions had; can thoughtfully reflect on whether they would do the same thing" (Workbook p.64), and "Can draw upon past experiences and state how they have affected their goals" (Workbook p.59).

### 4.3 The first read: competency for the strand at entry level

The strand is the selection frame (S5 P7), so the first read's questions differ by strand and the anchors read the strand's intelligence in the record's words: outward-facing, "relational and expressive and emotionally attuned under load" (WP p.10); inward-facing, "architectural and coordinated and timing-precise" (WP p.11). For physical-skill seats the first interview reads the capacity and the practical reads the procedure (S5 P41); the interview's first-read block is therefore shorter for those seats and never asks the candidate to describe a procedure the practical will watch.

**Outward-facing floor seats (server, host, bartender's outward half).** Four questions.

Attention, behavioral. The question asks for a specific time the candidate noticed something a person needed before it was said, in any setting, and what they noticed and did. This reads the capacity nunchi is built from, "attention to what is happening around them before it is announced" (S5 3.2), and the anchor scores attention, never the register; the word nunchi appears in no anchor. Below: no specific instance; a habit stated in general ("I always check on people"); an instance in which the person asked. At: one instance, the signal named (a pause, a look, a plate untouched, a coat kept on), the action, and what happened. Beyond: an instance they read wrong, what told them, and what changed in how they look.

Felt knowledge, strict competency. The question asks the candidate to tell the interviewer about something they have served, sold, or made and know well, as they would to someone who has never had it. This is Mahk's service requirement, "service speaks to a dish with felt knowledge, not recited description" (Canon v1.0, section 01), read as a capacity in any domain, since Korean-cuisine fluency is not required at entry and the house trains it (S5 P27). Below: adjectives ("amazing," "really good," "high quality"); a list of features; a script. At: names what it is, how it is made or where it comes from, and one specific thing about it no other version has. Beyond: says what it is reaching for and for whom, or what people get wrong about it.

Under load, situational. The question presents a moment with three demands at once (a table gone quiet with one person no longer eating, two other tables signalling, a runner asking a question) and asks what the candidate does first and how they decide. This reads "emotionally attuned under load" in a chair, which is the most it can do; the practical reads it on the floor. Below: does everything at once; ignores the quiet table; asks the interviewer what the rule is. At: names what they would look at first and why, sequences the rest, says what they would hand to someone else. Beyond: names what they do not know and how they would find out without interrogating the table.

The customer's account, behavioral. The question asks for a time the candidate was on the receiving end of service, anywhere, that they still remember, and why. This reads whether the candidate can hold the customer's side, which the dinner in the room later reads for leads and which every outward-facing seat needs. Below: cannot produce one, or produces a complaint only. At: one instance with the specific thing that made it, in the candidate's words, not the industry's. Beyond: says what the person serving them must have noticed to do it.

**Inward-facing floor seats (runner, and the inward half of any seat the sheet marks as such).** Four questions.

Setup, strict competency. The question asks the candidate to describe how they set up for something they had to do fast and in order, in any job: what they laid out, in what order, and why that order. Below: "I just get going"; no order; order without a reason. At: an order with a reason, a dependency named (this before that because), and what they check before starting. Beyond: what they changed about the setup after it failed once.

The failed handoff, behavioral. The question asks for a time something the candidate was to receive did not arrive, or something they sent arrived wrong, and what they did in the moment and afterward. This reads the strand's output, "the operational sequence that makes the outward strand possible" (WP p.11), and the record's rule that the customer "feel[s] that everything arrived right" (WP p.11). Below: blames the sender; did nothing after; describes the failure as normal. At: what they did so the person downstream did not feel it, and who they told afterward. Beyond: what they proposed changing so it did not recur, and whether it was taken.

Three things, situational. The question presents three deliveries to three places with a fourth called while the candidate's hands are full and asks for their order and what they say to whom. Below: "faster"; drops one silently. At: an order with a reason, a word to the person waiting, a word to whoever called the fourth. Beyond: names the thing that would have prevented the fourth call.

The invisible standard, behavioral. The question asks for a time the candidate did something well that no one noticed, and how they knew it was well done. This reads whether a person can hold a standard the customer never sees, which is the inward strand's whole condition. Below: cannot produce one; measures by praise. At: one instance and their own measure of it. Beyond: describes a standard they hold that the place they worked did not ask for.

**Beverage seats.** The bar maps to both strands (WP p.11), so a bartender seat's block takes attention, felt knowledge, and the customer's account from the outward set and setup and the failed handoff from the inward set, with the bar's objects substituted in the forms. The Head of Beverage is a domain-owner seat and adds the section 4.7 block. The sheet says which strand's questions lead for each beverage seat.

**The early-morning window seat.** Drafted, not opened (S5 P10). Its work is "repetition, precision, prep, low interaction density, high consequence for small errors" (carryover `86ajgnhh3`), closer to the inward description though the customer is in front of it (S5 3.4). Its draft block takes the inward set whole and adds the felt-knowledge question from the outward set, because the person at the bar "pours, finishes, and serves" (WP p.5). ### 4.4 The second read: growth potential as self-directed mastery

The record's system "favors the people who want it most" (WP p.11) and its rule is "Advancement requires preparation, not permission" (WP p.14). The read is for prior evidence of that behavior, in any domain. Three questions, the same for every seat.

Her three-part question, behavioral. How would the people you have worked with describe you; what constructive feedback have you had; what did you do about it (Book p.195). Asked in that order, with the second part asked whether or not the first was all positive. Below: only positives, and when pressed, feedback attributed to an unfair source or a bad manager, with no action; or "nothing ever bothers me" (Workbook p.68 names this as the thing to avoid). At: one piece of feedback stated specifically, what they did, and whether it worked. Beyond: they asked for the feedback, or built their own way of measuring the thing afterward.

Mastery on their own account, behavioral. The question asks for something the candidate got good at that no one sent them to learn, how they went about it, and how they knew they were getting better. Below: cannot produce one, or names a course or program they were placed in. At: a chosen thing, a method, and a way of knowing. Beyond: a plateau and what they changed to get past it. The workbook's onboarding bonus question reads the same capacity, "Displays autonomy, resourcefulness, and self-reliance in their methods" and "Actively seeks out additional resources that are not included in the materials provided" (Workbook p.56).

The visible path, situational. The question describes the house's actual mechanism in one plain sentence: every seat has a written list of what the next thing to learn is, the material is self-paced, and no one assigns it or waits for a manager to notice; then asks what the candidate would do with that in their first month. Below: waits to be told what is expected; asks who decides when they are ready. At: opens it, picks something, asks who to watch. Beyond: asks how the practical assessment works and says what they want to be able to do by ninety days. This is the one question in the set that describes the house to the candidate before asking, because the mechanism is unusual and the read is about the candidate's response to it, not their prior knowledge of it.

**P20 (Recommended).** The second read's three questions for every seat: her three-part self-awareness question in her order, mastery on their own account, and the visible path. Anchors as stated, with the "below" level naming the unfair-source deflection and the wait-to-be-told answer.

### 4.5 The third read: cultural alignment

The third read has three parts fixed by Session 5 (P5): no evidence of the behavior the non-negotiables forbid, comfort with distributed authority, and, on the outward-facing strand, comfort with the customer as a customer. Each part gets its questions. A fourth part, alignment with the company principles beyond the non-negotiables, cannot be written yet, and section 4.5.4 says why.

**4.5.1 The non-negotiables, read unnamed.** Three behavioral questions, none of which names exploitation, toxicity, or disposability.

The best and the worst place. The question asks for the best place the candidate has worked and the worst, and what made each so. The interviewer listens for how the candidate describes the people who were "below" them or beside them, whether the worst was named as conditions or as a person, and whether exploitation is described as normal. Below: describes exploitation or toxicity as how the industry is, or as what they would do as a lead ("you have to be hard on people"); describes people as interchangeable ("we churned through a lot of kids"). At: names conditions rather than persons; can say what they would have changed. Beyond: describes something they did to protect another person at a cost to themselves.

The lowest-morale team. Her workbook's question, "Tell me about the lowest-morale team you've been part of," seeking whether the candidate "bring[s] levity," tried "to fix the situation," or "run[s] away" (Workbook p.67). At Sŏn the anchor is about what the candidate did and whether they saw the cause as the system or a person. Below: left, blamed a person, or made it about themselves. At: names what was wrong as a condition and one thing they did. Beyond: names what would have fixed it structurally and says whether they proposed it.

The shift that went badly. The question asks for a shift or day that went wrong and what happened afterward. This is the record's "Blame the process, fix the process" (WP p.17) read from the candidate's account: "when something goes wrong, the first question is not who failed. It is what allowed this to happen" (WP p.17). Below: a person failed, and the afterward was blame. At: names what allowed it and what changed, or what they wished had changed. Beyond: brings up the customer unprompted, which is her test of a principle worked in without naming it (Book p.196), and says what the customer was owed.

**4.5.2 Distributed authority.** Two questions.

Authority held, behavioral. The question tells the candidate in one sentence that a server here owns the table and resolves a problem with their own judgment and a generosity range without asking (WP p.13; Canon v1.0, section 08), then asks for a time they had authority like that, or wanted it and did not have it, and what they did with it or without it. This is her screening question, "Are you comfortable with owning your own career outcomes rather than having a clear progression of goals and milestones described to you by a single decision-maker?" (Book p.180 to 181), rewritten for the floor (S5 3.1). Below: wants a manager to decide; describes authority as risk only; "I'd rather check." At: one instance of deciding inside a range and living with it. Beyond: names the limit of the range they held, what they escalated, and why that was the line.

The wrong dish, situational. A customer says the dish is not what they ordered; the candidate knows it is; they have the authority to make it right without asking. What do they do? The record's answer is blame absorption: "Order the wrong dish, and it is on the house, which should have clarified" (WP p.26). The anchor does not require the candidate to know the house's rule; it reads whether they reach for it. Below: proves the customer wrong; asks a manager. At: makes it right without arguing and without making the customer feel at fault. Beyond: says what they would say and what they would change in how the order was taken.

**4.5.3 The customer as customer, outward-facing seats.** One question. The record's position is that "guest" language "subtly shifts accountability from host to visitor," that "Customer keeps the transaction visible," and that "You cannot truly belong somewhere that is performing for you" (WP p.25). The question asks what the candidate thinks a person is owed for what they pay, and for a time the candidate had to be honest with a customer about something that was not right. Below: performance language as the whole answer ("make them feel special," "they're always right"); no instance of honesty. At: names what was owed and one instance of saying what was true. Beyond: describes a recovery in which they led "with more than expected" (WP p.26) without being told to.

**4.5.4 The principles, held.** Company principles version one is a pre-opening artifact with a mandatory revision cadence (carryover `86akh3unw`), and the record's principles beyond the non-negotiables are not ratified. Her method for a principle, a behavioral question that presents a situation in which the principle would show and scores whether it appears unprompted (Book p.196), is the form. The form is specified here: one such question per ratified principle, appended to the third read's block, anchored at three levels on whether the principle's behavior appears in the account without the interviewer naming it. The content cannot be written until the principles are. Until then the third read runs on the non-negotiables, distributed authority, and the customer as customer, all of which the record already holds.

**P21 (Recommended).** The third read's question set: three unnamed non-negotiables questions (the best and worst place, the lowest-morale team, the shift that went badly), two distributed-authority questions (authority held, the wrong dish), and for outward-facing seats the customer-as-customer question. Anchors as stated, with "below" naming the industry-is-like-that answer, the wants-a-manager answer, and the performance answer.

**P22 (Founder-gated).** The principle-embedded behavioral questions. ### 4.6 The fourth read: what the building must supply

The fourth read is not about the candidate's competence and it is not scored on the candidate. It is recorded, in the candidate's words, and the interviewer marks each item as something the building can supply, cannot supply, or does not yet know whether it can (section 6). It is asked last in the first interview (P11), after the candidate has heard what the seat is. It is "Employees are the users" (WP p.17) at the point of entry: the process asks the person what the design must hold.

Six questions, the same for every seat, asked plainly and without follow-up beyond clarification.

Language: which language the candidate does their best thinking in, and which they would want training in. This is the record's language commitment (WP p.17) carried into the building.

Shift shape: what shape their life allows, period by period against field 11, and what would make the difference between staying a year and leaving. This is the retention read asked as a design question.

How they learn: watching, doing, reading, being shown, being told; and what has never worked for them. This feeds the training plan (WP p.18) and the mentor's method.

What got in the way: at the last place, what got in the way of doing the work well that had nothing to do with them. This is the inhibitor read; the record's own analogy is the broken computer everyone has adapted to (WP p.12).

What they would not ask for: what they would need on a first day that they would be embarrassed to ask for. The record answers most of the honest answers already, uniforms and shoes, a locker, a real employee area (WP p.19 to 20), and the question is asked so the sheet's promises can be matched to a stated need.

The work's demands: asked only in relation to field 13's stated physical demands, as whether anything about those demands is something the candidate wants the house to know so the building can hold it. The Operations Lead sets the wording with counsel, because the question sits next to a line the law draws; this page specifies the read and not the sentence.

The answers travel to the person page at the offer (S5 P16, I23) and to no other place, and the first check-in's "what is in the way" question is asked against them.

**P23 (Recommended).** The fourth read's six questions (language, shift shape, how they learn, what got in the way, what they would not ask for, the work's demands), asked last, recorded in the candidate's words, marked can supply, cannot supply, or unknown, and never scored on the candidate. ### 4.7 The adjacent-domain block

For domain-owner seats, three questions from the interface's side, filed under the first read.

What the candidate needs from the seat beside theirs, asked as a specific list for a specific night. Below: "communication"; a generality. At: two or three named things with the timing they need them by. Beyond: what they would hand back in return, unasked.

What they hand on and in what state, asked for the last place they held a domain. Below: describes their output without the receiver. At: names the receiver and what "right" looked like for them. Beyond: names a time the handoff was wrong from their side and what they changed.

The adjacent node short, situational. The seat beside theirs is down a person tonight; what do they do and what do they not do? This reads whether the candidate absorbs load that belongs to another domain, which is the record's node-overload failure (WP p.14). Below: takes it over. At: covers what they can, tells the person who owns it, and names what they cannot cover. Beyond: says what the system should have surfaced before service.

### 4.8 The lead seats' block

Built on S5 P55 and replacing the strand-competency block for the Operations Lead and the Maitre d. Her rubric-building method, interviewing holders of each half of the split in rooms the founders respect (S5 P55), may refine these; any criterion that rests on a practice at Coqodaq, Alinea, or Gracious is Brandon's to confirm and not the team's to assert. Five elements.

A live worked scenario, in her roleplay form (Workbook p.60 to 62). The interviewer sets a Friday: the kitchen down a station, two sections seated at once, a runner new that week, and the candidate in the seat. The candidate walks it aloud and may ask anything. This does the work her written exercise does, "put the candidate into a realistic work scenario, test how they structure their thinking" (Book p.196), in the room and on the clock, without the take-home Session 5 refused (S5 P38). Below: acts without asking about context (her first warning, Book p.214); takes the whole thing; assigns; reports upward to a person who does not exist. At: asks what the other lead holds and hands them their half rather than taking it; names what the stack should already be showing; sequences; says what they would refuse to do mid-service. Beyond: after the walk-through, names what allowed it and what to change in the process, unprompted.

What they would refuse to do in the seat. Below: nothing; "whatever it takes." At: names at least one thing the load-bearing model does that this seat does not (assign, review from above, take a cross-domain problem, decide a person's advancement). Beyond: names what they would refuse and what they would do instead.

The best person who worked beside or for them, and what happened to that person. This reads succession orientation against the record's goal that "the building rarely hires a manager from outside" (WP p.19). Below: the person is still there doing the same thing, or left and the candidate does not know where. At: the person moved to something better and the candidate can say how. Beyond: the candidate built the path.

Who they would report to. Asked as a plain question, to hear the candidate's answer before the interviewer says "no one; the two leads are parallel." The reaction to the answer is the evidence. Below: unease that persists, or a request for a person to escalate to. At: a question about how a cross-domain call is made. Beyond: a description of how they have held a parallel structure before, or an honest statement that they have not and what they would need.

**P24 (Recommended).** The lead seats' block: a live worked scenario in her roleplay form as the lead's work sample, what they would refuse, the best person and what happened to them, the two mandatory probes on every account, and who they would report to. ### 4.9 The service-period distortion, and what in the loop resists it

Carryover `86ajgnhh3` states the hazard and Session 5 answered it structurally (S5 P9, P10): inward-facing seats opened on their own requirements, measurement held by the stack, the practical scored by rubric. This page's half is whether the interview can detect the distortion it is prone to producing, and what in the loop resists it. Restated without the code names: early morning is repetition, precision, prep, low interaction density, high consequence for small errors; lunch is throughput under a clock and has no seats; dinner is the only period that wants the full range; late night is energy, presence, the room. Service pressure selects for the outgoing profile, and the people doing the selecting are the people the room rewarded.

What resists it, in the order it acts. The inward-facing anchors are written in inward words, sequence, dependency, timing, standard, so an interviewer reading for warmth has nothing on the anchor to write it against. No anchor at any level, for any strand, carries a presence word (P17). The phone screen has no field for manner and its declines are scanned for reasons not on the sheet (P10). The practical scores procedure and reproduction and has no column for how the room felt (S5 P41, I10). The scorecard's evidence lines are scanned, at the calibration read, for the presence lexicon: energy, presence, personality, charisma, polish, vibe, natural, confident, bubbly, "would fit in," "great guy," "one of us," and a hit is a re-training trigger for the interviewer and a re-read of the packet by the lead (section 8.2). And the calibration read carries a strand read: whether inward-facing seats decline at a rate outward-facing seats do not, whether inward-facing hires cluster on the same evidence words as outward-facing hires, and whether one interviewer's inward-facing scorecards read like outward-facing ones. No target, no figure.

What the interview cannot resist on its own, said plainly. An interviewer's own preference for their own profile is invisible to the interviewer, and the anchors make it harder to record, not impossible to hold. A domain lead who is the room's profile can, across years and a whole domain, hire in their own image one defensible packet at a time, and no single packet shows it. The only thing that shows it is the pattern across packets, which is the calibration read, and the only thing that stops it is a lead reading that pattern about themselves. The loop's resistance to the distortion is therefore the read, and a read that is not run is no resistance at all; row 62 checks that it runs.

Late night is not a hire and the interview does not read for it. It is dinner's later register (WP p.35), the same seats with the jacket off (Canon v1.0, section 08), a scheduling preference and a development read on the sheet (S5 P10). A candidate is read for the dinner seat, and "energy, presence, the room" is exactly the profile the anchors are built to keep out of a dinner scorecard, so a late-night preference is recorded under the fourth read's shift-shape answer and nowhere else.

**P25 (Recommended).** The loop's resistance to the outgoing-profile distortion is: inward anchors in inward words, no presence word in any anchor, no manner field on the phone screen, the practical scored on procedure, the presence-lexicon scan on every scorecard at the calibration read, and the calibration read's strand read. The interview cannot resist a lead's own preference across a domain over years; only the read can, and row 62 checks that it runs. Late night is never read for.

**P26 (Team-filled).** The anchor set's first revision. The anchors are written here at entry level from the record and her chapter, and no candidate has answered them. At the mechanism reset after dinner's first quarter, the trained interviewers who ran the cohort's stages revise the anchor wording against the calibration read's record, keeping the four-part form, the three levels, and the no-presence rule, by versioned change. The team fills it once it exists.

## 5. The paid practical, filled in

Session 5 set the practical as the record's replacement for the stage and fixed what it reads, who scores it, who stands beside the candidate, its range condition, and its pay record (S5 P41 to P44, I10, I11). This section fills what Session 5 left: the practical's block and length, its scoring rows and marks, what it may not conclude, what a candidate is owed, and what it costs a running shift. The Realist owns the last.

### 5.1 The block

A practical is not a shift. It is a scored block inside a service or a training service, and its length is set by what it must read. It reads procedure mastery, reproduction under load, and the candidate's treatment of the people around them (S5 P41). Procedure is read at setup, because the station's or section's state before the first ticket is where a person's procedure shows before pressure hides it; the Realist's anchor is that survival is decided before doors open, in the mise (profile, hospitality operations realist). Load is read when the first wave arrives. Treatment of people is read throughout. So the block is the pre-service setup window plus the first seating wave, and it ends at a stated time before the second turn. A first practical is never a whole service, because a candidate who has held a station for four hours has been staffing, not assessed, and the assessor stopped reading after the first hour. The hours are on the sheet's field 8 for the seat, stated in the candidate's note (P7), and paid as told (S5 I11).

For the floor, the block is the section's setup, the pre-service brief (which the candidate attends and does not speak at), and the first wave on a section the seat holder is holding, with the candidate carrying what the holder hands them. For a kitchen station, the block is the mise and the first wave on the station, chef-gated in every specific (S5 P44). For the bar, setup and the first wave at the well. For the early-morning window, when opened, the setup and the first hour of the window.

**P27 (Recommended).** The practical's block is the setup window plus the first seating wave, ending at a stated time before the second turn, never a whole service on a first practical, with the hours on the sheet's field 8 and in the candidate's note and paid as told.

### 5.2 The scoring rows and the marks

The scoring form is S5 I10; this page fills its rows. Every row is a procedure or a condition from the seat sheet's field 6 at the entry horizon, and the assessor scores nothing the sheet does not name and nothing the candidate was not told they would be scored on (S5 I10).

Procedure rows. Each entry-level procedure from field 6 for the station or section is a row (for a server section: the setup sequence, the walk, "We walk, we never point" (Canon v1.0, section 06), the step-back coverage position, water first and spoken only (Canon v1.0, section 08), clearing without narration, handing a runner a clean problem; for a runner: the setup, the handoff, the carry order, the return; for the bar: the well's setup, the sequence, the close of a ticket). Each row is marked reproduced, reproduced with correction, or not reproduced, with a count of corrections where corrected. "Reproduced" means the candidate did it the way they were shown, the second and third time as the first; the standard is the Realist's, that line work is reproduction performed the same way every time. Two procedure rows are on every form regardless of seat: asked before guessing (marked asked, guessed and was right, guessed and was wrong), and cleaned as they went (marked yes, with prompting, no).

Load row. One row: held the sequence as the pace rose, marked held, held with a dropped step, or did not hold. On a training service or any practical run without load, the row is marked not read, and the form carries the S5 P42 mark that the practical ran without load. A load row on a without-load practical is never filled by analogy to how the candidate seemed.

People rows. One row each for the steward or porter, the runner or the person one step downstream, and the person training them: an evidence line of what the candidate said and did toward that person, and a mark against the non-negotiables: no evidence of exploitation or toxicity, or a flag with the evidence. This is the non-negotiables "read in the one place it cannot be performed" (S5 8.4). A flag ends the candidacy at any stage (S5 12.3).

The debrief line. At the end of the block, the assessor asks the candidate one question, what they would do differently if they ran the block again, and records the answer in the candidate's words under procedure mastery, as evidence of whether the candidate knows what they did. It is not a separate read.

No composite. The rows are not summed and there is no score. The assessor's recommendation is advance or decline with the read named (procedure, load, or the non-negotiables), and a decline on procedure names the rows not reproduced.

What is not on the form, and the form refuses: presence, charm, how the room felt about the candidate, how fast they were relative to the seat holder, whether they "have it," nunchi, and any row for a competency the house develops (field 7).

**P28 (Recommended).** The practical's scoring rows: one row per entry-level procedure from field 6 marked reproduced, reproduced with correction, or not reproduced, plus asked-before-guessing and cleaned-as-they-went on every form; one load row marked held, held with a dropped step, did not hold, or not read; one people row each for the steward, the downstream node, and the trainer, with a non-negotiables mark; the debrief line under procedure. No composite; the recommendation names the read and, for a procedure decline, the rows.

### 5.3 By whom, against what, and the first practical an assessor scores

The assessor is a trained person from the seat's strand who does not decide the hire (S5 P41), and the seat holder or mentor who works beside the candidate is a second person (S5 I10). They are never the same person, because a person teaching a station cannot watch it. The assessor scores during and immediately after the block, never the next day (S5 I10), against the rows on the sheet, and submits before speaking to the domain lead.

The first practical any newly trained assessor scores is shadow-scored: a second trained assessor scores the same block independently, both forms are submitted, and the Maitre d reads them side by side as the assessor's training completion (section 7.2). This is training, not a change to who runs the stage; the shadow is the same runner class, and the candidate's packet carries both forms with the shadow marked as such. Once an assessor's first form has been read against a shadow, they score alone.

The Realist's condition on the assessor: the assessor is on the schedule as an assessor, an extra body placed by the stack for the block, and is not pulled off a station at the start of service to score. An assessor who is also covering a station scores nothing true, because the block's first wave is exactly when their own station needs them. This is a scheduling rule and it is on the schedule, not in a norm.

### 5.4 What the practical may not conclude

It may not conclude that the candidate will hold the station in month six; it reads one block. It may not conclude load on a without-load practical (P28). It may not conclude presence, nunchi, or how the room felt (S5 P41). It may not conclude a competency the house develops. It may not conclude speed relative to the seat holder, because the seat holder has held the station for months and the standard is reproduction, not pace. It may not conclude anything about a customer's reaction, because the customer is never told a candidate served them and the candidate never carries a recovery or a generosity decision (S5 P41). It may not conclude a level or a rate. And it may not conclude that a candidate who was corrected three times is worse than one corrected once, unless the rows say so; a correction is teaching, and the mark is reproduced-with-correction, not failed.

**P29 (Recommended).** The practical may not conclude month-six performance, load on a without-load run, presence, nunchi, a developed competency, speed relative to the holder, a customer's reaction, a level, or that corrections are failures.

### 5.5 What the candidate is owed

Before: the note (P7) with the hours, the station or section, the date and service, whether it runs with load, who will be beside them, what they will be scored on (the rows, in plain words, since field 8 is public), what to wear and what the house supplies, that it is paid and how, and that they will hear inside the window. A candidate who has not received the note does not run a practical.

During: a person beside them the whole block; a station or section that is set up for a practical, meaning the seat holder has the mise or the section to teach with and is not themselves short; the pre-service brief attended; never being the only person a table has; never a recovery or a generosity decision; never being introduced to a customer as a candidate; a stop if the service turns short mid-block, with the block paid for the hours told (S5 I11). And the house's word, which is customer, in every sentence spoken near them.

After: pay through Rippling for the hours told or worked, whichever is greater (S5 I11); the answer inside the window naming the read and, for a procedure decline, the rows not reproduced (S5 P47); the pool offered if the house would read them again; the candidate pulse's one question (S5 P30). A candidate declined on procedure is told which rows, because the rows are public and the house teaches them, and a candidate who returns from the pool a season later is read on the same rows.

**P30 (Recommended).** The candidate is owed, before, the full note; during, a person beside them, a set-up station, no customer-facing exposure, no recovery or generosity, and a paid stop if the service turns short; after, pay as told, the answer naming the rows, the pool if offered, and the pulse.

### 5.6 What it costs a running shift, plainly

The Realist's read, on a real Friday with the crew the house will actually have. A practical costs: the seat holder's slack for the block, since they are teaching and watching a station they would otherwise run at pace; the assessor as an extra body on the schedule for the block; a section or a station one degree slower through the first wave; the pass owner's attention on one more variable if the practical is in the kitchen; and the brief's time to say that a practical is running and where, which is a readiness item and goes in the readiness block the rush cannot delete (S4 13.1). None of that exists on a service the leads' review flagged short, and a practical on that night reads nothing true and costs the room (S5 P42).

So the conditions, restated as scheduling rules the stack holds: a practical runs only on a service the leads' review has not flagged short (S5 P42); the schedule for that service carries the assessor as an extra body and the seat holder as a teaching body, both placed by the stack; no more than one practical per section or station per service, and no more practicals per service than the house has seat holders and assessors free that night; never on a new period's first service, never on a streamed service, never as sole coverage (S5 P42); never scheduled to make a start date (S5 P42).

Pre-opening the cohort's practicals run in training services and read without load (S5 P42), and the Realist's addition is what happens after: the first four weeks of dinner service are the cohort's load read, recorded at the check-in on the person page as a line per week, and a cohort hire who does not hold under load in those weeks is a training question and a slack question, never a hiring failure, because the practical said in writing that it did not read load. Blame the process (WP p.17) applies to the hiring process too, and this is where it applies.

**P31 (Recommended).** A practical's cost to a running shift is the seat holder's slack, the assessor as an extra body, a slower section, the pass's attention, and a brief line; the schedule carries all of it as placed shift or the practical does not run. One practical per section or station per service, no more than the house has free holders and assessors, and the cohort's first four dinner weeks are the load read the training-service practicals could not give, recorded at the check-in and read as a training and slack question.

**P32 (Chef-gated).** The kitchen practical's rows: the station's entry procedures, the mise standard, the plate, and the load row's content are the chef partner's, written in this page's row form (reproduced, with correction, not reproduced; asked before guessing; cleaned as they went; the people rows; no composite). ## 6. The scorecard

### 6.1 Why the dimensions are set here, and once

Brandon's constraint on this session is that Session 13's review rubric must score against the same dimensions this page sets, because a hiring bar and a performance bar that measure different things produce people who were hired correctly and reviewed unfairly. The record already carries the seam the constraint closes: interviews "assess specific competencies, cultural alignment, and growth potential" (WP p.18), and reviews "focus on development, growth, and the path forward rather than a verdict" (WP p.19), and nowhere does the record say those are the same things measured at two horizons. A carryover already routes the scorecard-and-review-rubric question to Session 13 as one instrument (`86ajgmmkd`), and levels and ladders are routed to Sessions 8 and 13 (`86ajgnj4e`). This section names the dimensions once, deliberately, in the form Session 13 must score against, and says what a review rubric must not add.

The dimensions are the four reads (S5 P5), given a second name because a read is what an interview does and a dimension is what a record holds across time. They are not new. Session 5 fixed "the four reads assigned per stage and no other" (carryover `86akh7qdf`), and a fifth dimension would be a fifth read. What this section adds is the in-role meaning of each, which hiring does not need and Session 13 does, and the statement that these four are Session 13's inheritance.

### 6.2 The four dimensions, named

**Dimension 1: competency for the seat.** At entry: the strand's intelligence, read as a capacity in any domain (section 4.3), and for physical-skill seats the entry procedures reproduced on the practical (section 5.2); the bar is field 6's entry horizon. In role: the seat's documented competency requirements at the horizon the person holds (WP p.11), field 6's second horizon and each unlock after it, validated by the practical assessment the invisible advocate schedules (WP p.11) and signed off by the domain lead (WP p.19). For a lead seat: holding a domain without an apex (S5 P55; section 4.8). Session 13 scores this dimension against the seat sheet's field 6 at the person's horizon, which is the same document the hiring read used, so the hiring bar and the review bar are the same list read at two points on it.

**Dimension 2: self-directed mastery.** At entry: prior evidence of learning something to mastery on one's own account and acting on feedback (section 4.4). In role: the invisible advocate's own record, unlocks opened and taken, modules completed, practical assessments passed, connections added to the node (WP p.11), and the person's part in teaching, since "Any qualified team member can create training modules" (WP p.19). Session 13 scores this dimension from the system's record, not from a lead's impression, because "No one's advancement waits on a manager's attention or judgment. The system is the advocate" (WP p.11), and a review that scored mastery from a lead's impression would put the manager's judgment back where the record removed it.

**Dimension 3: conduct under the non-negotiables and inside the range.** At entry: no evidence of the behavior the non-negotiables forbid, comfort with distributed authority, and on the outward-facing strand the customer as customer (section 4.5). In role: the non-negotiables held in the building, toward every person including the ones the load-bearing model would place below; the seat's decision-rights range used, not exceeded, and not left unused, with escalations made where the triggers name them (S5 field 10; Canon v1.0, section 08); and the customer as customer in every sentence spoken and written (WP p.25; Canon v3.0, p.5). Session 13 scores this dimension against the range on the seat sheet and the non-negotiables, which are the same terms the candidate was handed on the transparency sheet's items 8 and 9 (S5 P34).

**Dimension 4: what the building supplies.** At entry: what the candidate says they need, in their words, marked as something the building can supply, cannot supply, or does not yet know (section 4.6). In role: whether the building supplied it, read at every check-in's "what is in the way" question and at the review, as the upward half of "Feedback runs both directions" (WP p.19). This is the dimension that reviews the house rather than the person. It is in the hiring scorecard because the record put it there, "whether the building is equipped to create the environment this person needs to succeed" (WP p.18), and it is in the review rubric because a review that scored only the person would have dropped the record's fourth read at exactly the point the person can say whether the building kept its word.

These four are what Session 13 inherits. The review rubric scores dimensions 1 through 4, in these terms, against the same seat sheet, with dimension 4 scored on the building. The marks Session 13 uses at review are Session 13's to set, and the record constrains them already: a review is "development, growth, and the path forward rather than a verdict" (WP p.19), so the review's marks carry no verdict either.

What a review rubric must not add, and why. Cross-strand fluency and cross-period fluency are development markers recorded on the person page as connections (WP p.11; S5 P62); they are not dimensions, because they are not read at hiring, and a dimension scored at review that was never read at hiring is exactly the mismatch the constraint forbids. Their recognition is Session 13's (carryover `86akh3wjw`, `86akh7qxy`) and their structure Session 8's. Leadership is not a dimension; for a lead seat it is dimension 1. "Fit," presence, and nunchi are not dimensions at hiring and cannot become dimensions at review; nunchi is what the house develops and the training platform validates, and it is read by the practical assessment at each unlock as procedure that has become judgment (Canon v1.0, section 01), which is dimension 1 at a later horizon. Her workbook's competencies (collaboration, conscientiousness, resilience, accountability, and the rest) are not dimensions; where they are real they live inside these four, as section 2.1 mapped.

**P33 (Recommended).** The scorecard's four dimensions, which are the four reads given their in-role meaning: competency for the seat (field 6 at the horizon held); self-directed mastery (the invisible advocate's record); conduct under the non-negotiables and inside the range (the sheet's field 10 and item 9); and what the building supplies (the check-in's upward half). These four, in these terms, against the same seat sheet, are what Session 13's review rubric scores. Cross-strand and cross-period fluency are markers, not dimensions; nothing is added at review that was not read at hiring.

### 6.3 How evidence is recorded

Every scorecard, at every stage, has the same dimension layer, and the stage-specific fields Session 5 specified (I8 to I13) sit inside it.

Per question, an evidence line: what the candidate said they did, in the interviewer's plain words or the candidate's, with the specific instance named. Not an impression. An evidence line that could have been written before the interview ("seems experienced," "good energy," "would fit in") is not evidence and is the presence-lexicon flag (section 4.9).

Per dimension, a mark: does not meet the entry bar; meets it; meets it with evidence beyond it; or not assessed, with the reason (the stage does not assess this dimension, or the question was not reached). "Meets with evidence beyond" is never required and is read by the lead only when two candidates meet the bar for one seat. For dimension 4, the marks are different in kind: per item, can supply, cannot supply, unknown, and no mark on the candidate.

Per scorecard, a disqualifier flag: none, or a flag naming the disqualifier from field 9 with the evidence. A flag is not a mark and it is not weighed; it ends the candidacy (S5 12.3).

Per scorecard, a recommendation: advance or decline, and for a decline the dimension named and the evidence line it rests on. A recommendation with no dimension named cannot be submitted (section 6.5), and if one reaches the record it is the re-training trigger (S5 P52).

Per scorecard, the runner's name and training record reference, the sheet version, the stage's date and length, and for the first interview the transparency sheet version and language handed.

### 6.4 Why there is no free-text verdict

Four reasons, stated once. First, the decision is the domain lead's on the packet (S5 P46), and a verdict on a scorecard is a second decision by a person the structure did not give one to. Second, the profile's anchor on interviewing is that most of an assessment is made in the first minutes and the rest of the interview confirms it; a verdict field is where that first impression is written down, and an evidence field is where it is made to answer for itself. Third, a verdict cannot be calibrated: two interviewers' verdicts on one candidate can be compared only by asking who was right, and two interviewers' evidence lines can be compared against the anchor. Fourth, a verdict cannot be answered: a decline that says "not for us" cannot be sent to a candidate, and a decline that names a dimension and an evidence line can, in one sentence, in the correspondence register (S5 P47). Her own rule agrees: disagreement should be "specific and clear" and "tie directly into one of the capabilities" (Book p.176, cited by S5 8.6); this page removes the place a non-specific disagreement would go.

**P34 (Recommended).** Every scorecard carries per-question evidence lines, a per-dimension mark (does not meet, meets, meets with evidence beyond, not assessed with reason; for dimension 4, can supply, cannot supply, unknown), a disqualifier flag or none, and a recommendation that names a dimension for a decline. No free-text verdict, for the four reasons stated.

### 6.5 What the stack does and refuses

Her chapter names ATS mechanics directly: feedback responses of "definitely," "yes," "no," and "not sure" (Book p.197), and the offer-approval routing with a turnaround commitment (Book p.197). This page says what is configuration, what is a build, and what is to confirm, and nothing here asserts a tool feature as fact.

Configuration. One scorecard template per stage per seat, generated from field 8's kit, with the questions in order as the evidence fields and the four dimensions as the attribute groups; the three-state mark per dimension; the dimension-4 marks; the disqualifier flag with an evidence field; the recommendation limited to two values, advance and decline, with a required dimension field on decline. Interviewer visibility rules: no runner sees another scorecard on a candidate until their own is submitted (S5 9.2). The interviewer pool per seat limited to people with a training record for that stage (S5 I21).

To confirm. Whether the bought surface's overall-recommendation field can be limited to two values or hidden; if it cannot, the field is configured to its narrowest form and the training teaches that only advance and decline are used, and the calibration read scans for anything else. Whether the per-dimension mark can carry "not assessed" as a value with a required reason. Whether reporting on marks per interviewer per seat exists for the calibration read, which is a report if the surface carries it and otherwise a small build.

Refused, by configuration. The "not sure" response, and any equivalent (neutral, no decision, mixed). It is honest and it is not permitted, because an interviewer who is not sure has a dimension they could not assess, and the honest record of that is "not assessed" on the dimension with the reason, which the packet then treats as incomplete by content (section 9.2). A numeric scale on any dimension. A composite or an average. A scorecard field that writes to the person page or to Rippling; scorecards stay in Greenhouse (S5 P16), and any integration that would carry them across is refused rather than configured. An approval chain on the offer beyond the stack's own refusals (section 9.7).

**P35 (Recommended).** Greenhouse's scorecards are configured as one template per stage per seat from field 8, with the four dimensions as attribute groups, three-state marks, the disqualifier flag, and a two-value recommendation requiring a dimension on decline; the "not sure" response, numeric scales, composites, and any sync of scorecards to the person page or Rippling are refused. Three items are to confirm and are resolved at configuration and recorded.

### 6.6 The coupling, filed

The coupling to Session 13 is filed as a carryover: the review rubric scores dimensions 1 to 4 as named in P33, against the seat sheet's field 6 and field 10 and item 9, with dimension 2 read from the invisible advocate's record and dimension 4 scored on the building; the review adds no dimension; cross-strand and cross-period fluency are markers whose recognition Session 13 prices. The ninety-day calibration read (section 7.6) is the first place the two bars are tested against each other, and its record is Session 13's evidence base alongside the check-in record (carryover `86akh67yy`).

**P36 (Recommended).** The coupling to Session 13 is filed: the review rubric scores the four dimensions of P33 against the same seat sheet, dimension 2 from the system's record, dimension 4 on the building, with no dimension added and the fluency markers priced but not scored. The ninety-day calibration read is the evidence that the two bars agree.

## 7. Interviewer training and calibration

Carryover `86akh7qey` is this section's charge. The record states that "Interviewers are trained, because asking a good question and reading the answer is a learned skill, not an instinct that arrives with a title" (WP p.18), places the training on a learning platform that does not exist before the team does (WP p.19), and leaves the first interviewers, the founders and then the two leads, untrained by its own definition (S5 15.13). Session 5 fixed the record on the person page, the stack's refusal to schedule an untrained runner, the training on scheduled shift, and the re-training trigger (S5 P52, I21). This section writes the training's content, its form, and how the first interviewers are trained before a platform exists.

### 7.1 What the training teaches

The training is a set of modules, written as a specification here and built under the document methodology, and once the platform exists they are modules on it like any other. Every module is short enough to be taken on scheduled shift in the check-in's unit of time, and the set for a first-interview runner is taken in one scheduled block.

Module 1, the system. The record's two paragraphs (WP p.17 to 18) read whole; the four reads and their in-role names as the four dimensions (section 6); what an interview may conclude and may not (P5); why there is no committee and no verdict (S5 P46; section 6.4); the assessor and the decider as different people; the bar held when the floor is short.

Module 2, the seat sheet. Field 8 read for the seat the person will interview for; fields 6, 7, 9, and 10 read aloud; the difference between a competency at entry and one the house develops, and the rule that field 7 is never scored as absent.

Module 3, question discipline. Her consistency rule (Book p.194); the kit's order; the three question types (Book p.195); STAR as the probe's shape (Book p.194) with the workbook's follow-up sets as the model (Workbook p.54 to 65); "what did you hold" as the mandatory probe (P4); what to do when a candidate's account runs long, which is to let the situation finish and probe the action; what to do when a question is not reached, which is to mark it not asked with the reason and never to invent a mark.

Module 4, the anchors. The three levels read for every question the person will ask; the four-part form (section 4.2); written worked examples of a below, an at, and a beyond answer for each question (P42); the rule that fluency is not evidence and that a specific weak answer outranks a general strong one.

Module 5, writing the scorecard. Evidence lines, not impressions; the presence lexicon and why a hit is a re-training trigger (section 4.9); her four mistakes as the four failure modes (P6) plus the first-minutes bias the profile names; the marks and what "not assessed" is for; the recommendation and the named dimension; what happens to the scorecard after submission (it stays in Greenhouse, it is read by the lead, it is read at the calibration read, and it never travels to the person page).

Module 6, the hand-off. For first-interview runners: walking the transparency sheet, reading item 8 aloud as what the seat holds (P11), stating item 6 as filled or "being finalized" under P35's ruling and saying nothing more about the mechanics; what may never be said (a figure beyond the sheet, an offer or the shape of one, a question about what the candidate earns, any reading of the format they chose); the language constant, customer never guest, in every sentence, "Internal communication included" (Canon v3.0, p.5); "we" for the house and "you" to the candidate.

Module 7, the disqualifiers and the flag. Field 9 for the seat; what evidence a flag needs; where a flag goes the same day (S5 12.3); that a flag is never weighed and never overridden; that a flag without evidence is not a flag and is itself a training item.

Module 8, the commitments. The mutual-commitments document (S5 I20) read whole, as the reference a scorecard without a named read is measured against.

Module 9, for assessors only. The scoring rows for the seat (section 5.2); scoring in the moment and never from memory; the debrief line; the shadow-scored first practical (section 5.3); what the practical may not conclude (P29).

Module 10, for phone screeners only. The items in order (P10); the manner refusal; the fixed decline list; what to say about pay, which is that the sheet carries the commitments and will be handed at the first interview.

Module 11, for the partners only. The dinner's three prompts and one silence (P14); the playbook tells at the table (S5 10.2); the founder stage's three questions; the rule that a founder's no names a non-negotiable or a fourth-read gap or is not a no (S5 P53).

Module 12, the correspondence rule. The interviewer never delivers the no and never hints at the outcome; the house sends every answer through the stack (S5 9.2, P47); what the interviewer says at the end of a stage is what happens next and by when, and nothing about how it went.

Module 13, the record. What stays in Greenhouse, which is everything about a candidate; what may never be said outside it, which is anything; the consent record for content (S5 I19); the rule that a runner may not run a stage for a person they referred (S5 P24).

**P37 (Recommended).** The training is thirteen modules as specified: the system, the seat sheet, question discipline, the anchors, writing the scorecard, the hand-off, the disqualifiers, the commitments, and the correspondence and record rules for every runner; the assessor, phone-screener, and partner modules by stage. Each is a specification built under the document methodology and, once the platform exists, a module on it.

### 7.2 The training's form and sequence

Reading the modules is not the training. The sequence for a first-interview runner is: the modules read, on scheduled shift; a live session with the Maitre d (pre-opening, with a founder) in which the person asks the kit's questions of the trainer and is probed on their probes; an observed stage, in which the person sits in on a trained runner's stage and submits no scorecard; a paired stage, in which the person runs the stage with a trained runner as the second interviewer, both scorecards submitted independently and read side by side by the Maitre d, with the pairing itself the record of completion; then a solo stage. Assessors add the shadow-scored first practical (section 5.3). Phone screeners take modules 1, 3, 5, 10, 12, and 13 and a paired call. Every step is on scheduled shift, on the clock, and the stack writes the record when the paired stage's two scorecards have been read (S5 I21).

The paired stage is not a new runner. For a domain-owner seat it is the adjacent-domain second interviewer's slot filled by the trainee under a trained runner; for an edge seat it is the second interviewer Session 5 already requires whenever the lead is one of the two (S5 P40), with the trainee as the second; for a seat where the lead is not interviewing, the paired stage runs with the trainee beside a trained runner as a second scorecard on that packet. A packet with a paired scorecard carries the pairing marked, and the lead reads both.

Re-training. A scorecard returned without a named read, a presence-lexicon hit, a decline reason not on the sheet, or a calibration read finding on the interviewer's marks sends the interviewer back to the relevant module and to one paired stage before their next solo stage (S5 P52). The re-training is not a sanction and is not on the person page as anything but the training record's re-training line; the record's stance is that the process is fixed before the person is (WP p.17).

**P38 (Recommended).** Training runs as modules read, a live session, an observed stage, a paired stage read side by side, then solo, all on scheduled shift, with the record written by the stack when the paired stage is read; assessors add the shadow-scored practical; re-training is a module and a paired stage before the next solo stage and is never a sanction.

### 7.3 Her STAR, her consistency rule, and her four mistakes

Each is inside a module rather than a position of its own. STAR is module 3's probe shape. The consistency rule is module 3's first sentence and row 68's check. The four mistakes are module 5's four failure modes (P6). Her line that "It never hurts to refresh your interviewing skills, even if you've been conducting interviews for over 20 years" (Book p.194) becomes the rule that a training record has a currency: an interviewer who has run no stage in a full season takes the paired stage again before a solo one, and the record carries the date of the last stage run. This is a parameter for the mechanism reset.

### 7.4 The founders, before the platform exists

The problem, stated exactly. The first stages the house runs are the two leads' first interviews, substitute dinners, and founder stages, and all of them are run by the founders. Row 44 requires a training record on the person page before any stage runs, and I21 requires one for every partner before the first stage. The platform does not exist, the person page may not exist, the Maitre d who owns the training does not exist, and I21 refuses "a record written by a lead rather than the stack." Read literally, the founders cannot be trained and cannot interview. That is a conflict between Session 5's specification and the pre-opening condition, and it is surfaced here rather than resolved by quietly letting the founders interview untrained.

The team's recommendation, in four parts. First, the content exists before the platform: the thirteen modules are built as documents under the document methodology, ahead of any other instrument on this page, because nothing else on the hiring calendar can run before they do (row 61 sits ahead of the leads' first interviews in P15). Second, the founders train each other on the sequence in 7.2 with the platform's steps replaced: modules read; the live session run by each founder on the other; the observed and paired stages run as practice stages on consenting people who are not and will not be candidates, drawn from the founders' own reach, with both founders scoring each practice stage independently and reading the two scorecards side by side as the calibration; the practice scorecards kept on the partners' page and never in Greenhouse, since the people are not candidates. Third, the interim training record is a versioned entry on the partners' page per founder, stating the modules read, the practice stages run with dates, the paired read, and the languages the founder can run a stage in; the stack copies it to the person page when the page exists; and I21's refusal of a hand-written record is read as applying from the moment the stack can write one, not before. Fourth, the founders' first real stages are calibrated by the structure they already have: the first lead's first interview is run by one partner (S5 12.2), the substitute dinner by both with independent scorecards (S5 I12), and the founder stage by a partner; the two dinner scorecards, read side by side, are the first real calibration and are read by both founders together before the decision, as the one place on this page two people read scorecards together before a decision, because there is no Maitre d yet to do it.

What the team does not recommend, and marks. An outside trainer is a spend this program does not price and an interviewer-training product would teach the industry's reads; the team recommends none and marks it, because it is the founders' call and it is a spend. And the languages: the record promises that a candidate can "interview with someone who speaks it" (WP p.17), and pre-opening the only interviewers are the founders, who can run a stage only in the languages they hold. A first interview requested in a language neither founder holds has no trained runner. The options are that the first lead hired holds it, that a trained interviewer is hired into the cohort early for it, or that a third person in the room interprets, which the record does not name and which changes what the interviewer hears. This is founder-gated and is also finding 18.4.

**P39 (Founder-gated).** How the founders are trained before the platform exists. The team recommends: the modules built as documents first; the founders training each other on the 7.2 sequence with practice stages on consenting non-candidates scored independently and read side by side; an interim training record as a versioned entry on the partners' page per founder, with I21's refusal of a hand-written record read as applying once the stack can write one; and the two dinner scorecards on the first lead read together by both founders as the first real calibration. The founders close it, and with it whether an outside trainer is engaged (the team recommends none) and how a first interview is run in a language neither founder holds. This is the one place this page reads a Session 5 instrument (I21) as not yet applicable, and it is surfaced as such.

### 7.5 The leads, and then the pool

The two leads are trained by the founders on the same modules, with the founder-run stages as their observed stages and a founder as their paired second. Then the leads train the pool: the Maitre d owns the training and the record from the day the Maitre d is seated (WP p.10; S5 I21), the Operations Lead owns the compliance content in modules 6, 7, and 13 with counsel, and every trained person in the cohort is a potential runner for the stages their record covers. There is no elite interviewer class. Her Elevate interviewers "were high performers and some of our best interviewers" (Book p.197), a selected group; at Sŏn every trained person is in the pool for their strand's seats, because the record's leadership count has no room for a class of people whose job is to interview, and because a selected class of stewards of the culture is a load-bearing idea in a code-bearing house. The trained pool's roster, per seat and per language, is team-filled inside I21 and not a position.

**P40 (Recommended).** The leads are trained by the founders on the same sequence with founder-run stages as their observed and paired stages; the Maitre d owns training and the record from the day the seat is filled; every trained person is in the pool for their strand's seats; there is no elite interviewer class.

### 7.6 Her Elevate selection method, and what stands in for it

Her method for selecting Elevate interviewers read "historical feedback in our applicant tracking system," looked at "whether the interviewer was opinionated enough to submit a 'no'" on candidates not hired, and, for candidates hired, "whether the interviewer's assessments of 'definitely' or 'yes' compared favorably with how those candidates ultimately performed" (Book p.197). The function is interviewer accuracy measured against outcomes. It requires three things Sŏn does not have: a volume of hires large enough to read a pattern per interviewer, performance data from reviews, and years.

What stands in is the ninety-day calibration read. For every hire, at ninety days, the Maitre d (pre-opening the calibrating partner, section 8.3) opens the packet in Greenhouse and the person page side by side and reads: did the dimension-1 marks correspond to a person who reached the first unlock or is on track for it; did the dimension-2 marks correspond to a person who opened the platform and moved without being asked; did the dimension-3 evidence correspond to the range used and the non-negotiables held, as the check-in record shows; did the dimension-4 marks of "can supply" correspond to a building that supplied. Where the lead hired against a "does not meet," was the lead right. Where an interviewer marked "meets with evidence beyond," was there anything beyond. The read produces one line per interviewer per hire on the calibration record, anonymized by candidate, and over seasons the lines become the pattern her method reads at volume. It is a leading indicator available at ninety days, where hers is a lagging one available after a review cycle, and the profile's rule applies: the ranking, not a decimal.

What it does not do. It does not select an interviewer class (P40). It does not merge scorecards onto the person page; the two records are read side by side and neither is written to (S5 P16). It does not reach statistical significance at Sŏn's volume and the record never claims it does; it reads a pattern and names it, and an interviewer whose pattern diverges takes the paired stage again. And it reads the hiring system as a system: a hire who did not hold under load in the first four dinner weeks (P31) is first a question about the practical's rows and the training plan, and only after that a question about the interviewer.

**P41 (Recommended).** Her Elevate selection function, interviewer accuracy against outcomes, is done by the ninety-day calibration read: the packet and the person page read side by side per hire, one anonymized line per interviewer on the calibration record, as a leading indicator. It selects no class, merges no records, claims no significance, and reads the process before the person.

### 7.7 Training examples are written, not taken

Module 4's worked examples of below, at, and beyond answers are written by the team from the anchors and revised at the mechanism reset (P26). They are never drawn from a real candidate's scorecard, anonymized or not, because everything about a candidate stays in Greenhouse (S5 9.2) and a training module that quoted a candidate's answer would have moved it out. The calibration read may say that an anchor is producing the wrong marks; it may not quote the answer that showed it.

**P42 (Recommended).** Worked examples in the training are written from the anchors and never drawn from a real candidate's scorecard.

## 8. Calibration, bias control, and the function inside candidate review

### 8.1 Her sidebar at full strength

"The mission of CR is to ensure that all engineering roles at Stripe are filled by extraordinarily high-caliber candidates through a consistent, efficient, and transparent decision-making framework that seeks to control for bias" (Book p.205). Four ways: a standardized interview process "that tests for specific capabilities and experiences related to our ladders and levels," verified by "applying a consistent decision-making framework to each packet"; consistency, which "enables efficient decision-making as we develop a shared understanding of how interview performance maps to employee impact against ladder and level expectations"; transparency, "bidirectional feedback to and from CR," with the CR lead sending interviewers feedback on "interview feedback unrelated to the rubric," "obvious cases of bias," "use of gendered pronouns," or feedback "that focuses narrowly on role fit rather than broadly on fit for Stripe"; and bias control "through the consistent application of interviews and CR processes and, critically, by measuring and analyzing our results," with results reviewed against "Equal Employment Opportunity Commission self-identification" to confirm "no statistically significant difference in outcomes by self-identified group" (Book p.205 to 206). The framework's definition of the correct decision is a person "expected to at least successfully meet expectations [SME] in their first performance review based on ladder and level expectations for the approved level" (Book p.205 to 206). Six outcomes, three of them leveling outcomes and one "TBD: sent back for additional information" (Book p.206). Five steps: submission, scorecard review, scorecard verification, discussion and decision and résumé review, submission of the CR scorecard (Book p.206; Workbook p.73 to 77). Three roles: a lead who "ratifies the outcome," reviewers who ask whether "the scorecards reflect the rubrics, or is the feedback subjective," whether they are "too lenient or strict," and whether "the correct interview process [was] followed," and an admin who queues packets and records outcomes (Book p.206 to 207; Workbook p.75).

Beside it, three other checks from the same pages: the Bar Raiser, "never from the team for which they're performing the interview," who "run[s] the equivalent of the hiring committee meeting" and "can also single-handedly veto a candidate" (Book p.197); Stripe's early practice of "an executive interview every candidate before a final hiring decision was made" (Book p.198); and Google's founders reviewing "every hiring packet before an offer could be extended" (Book p.198).

Session 5 refused the panel, the committee, and the meeting (S5 I28), and refused the form. The brief's instruction is to work the functions one by one, say where each lands or that it lands nowhere, and surface rather than overwrite if an S5 position should change. That is this section.

### 8.2 The function map

Consistent framework application. Lands. Field 8 of the seat sheet is the framework, the kit is generated from it, the stack shows the questions in order, and row 68 checks that every question was asked. Nothing new is needed.

Scorecard verification: do scorecards reflect the rubric, are they subjective, are they too lenient or strict, was the process followed. Lands in three places, one of them new. The stack's shape is the first: a scorecard with no verdict field, evidence lines per question, and a required dimension on decline cannot be filed any other way. The lead's reading at decision is the second, and it is the weak one, because the lead is the decider and a decider verifying their own evidence is the fusion Session 5 separated (S5 P46). The calibration read (section 8.3) is the third and it is where "too lenient or strict" lives, because leniency is a pattern across scorecards and no single packet shows it.

Verification of the outcome by someone not the decider. Lands partially. Pre-opening, the founder stage is a scored stage in the packet, not a review of the lead's outcome, and it reads the non-negotiables and the fourth read only (S5 P53); it is a check on two dimensions and not on the decision. After the reset, for edge seats, no person verifies a decision (S5 P54). Two triggers do part of the work: a decline that contradicts every scorecard goes to the leads' review as a calibration read (S5 12.3), and this page adds its mirror, a hire on a packet carrying a "does not meet" (P45). Neither is a verification of every outcome. The gap is named in 8.4.

The decision standard, "expected to at least successfully meet expectations in their first performance review." Lands, as the coupling in section 6: Sŏn's standard is the entry bar on field 6, and the ninety-day calibration read tests whether meeting it at hiring predicted meeting it in the building, which is her SME test run early and against the same list.

Leveling outcomes: hire at a higher or lower level. Lands nowhere, and nothing is needed there; Sŏn has no level to place a hire on (section 12).

Transparency: feedback to interviewers. Lands on the Maitre d, with one new instrument. Every interviewer is told the decision on every packet they scored, through the stack, with the lead's dissent line where the lead decided against their recommendation (section 9.5); the calibration read produces a note per interviewer (I8); the ninety-day read produces a line per interviewer. Her CR lead's email is the Maitre d's note, and her list of what it addresses, feedback unrelated to the rubric, obvious bias, gendered pronouns, narrow role fit (Book p.205), becomes the note's four standing items, with "role fit" read as the presence lexicon and the developed-competency error.

Bias control through measurement. Lands partially, and the limit is stated. The stage-rate read exists (S5 P26): whether any stage declines a group of candidates at a rate the others do not, with no target and no figure. It runs on what Greenhouse holds, and what Greenhouse holds about who a candidate is depends on whether the house asks for self-identification, which is founder-gated and routed to Session 11 with the position on the team's composition (S5 P26). And at Sŏn's volume, one house hiring a cohort and then on tension loss, "no statistically significant difference" is not a test the house can run honestly; it can read a pattern and name it. Her method's strongest clause therefore lands as a pattern read with an honest statement that it is one, and the position it would enforce lands nowhere until Session 11 and the founders.

Pattern-matching from experience. Her CR participants "have seen a large volume of candidates and interview feedback and can pattern-match accordingly" (Book p.204). Sŏn has no volume. What stands in is the anchors written down (section 4) and her own rule for infrequent roles, "the same people interview all of the candidates" (Book p.196), which Session 5's runners already produce for lead and domain-owner seats (the lead and the co-lead, the partners). Lands in the anchors.

The outside-team steward, the Bar Raiser. Lands for domain-owner seats as the adjacent-domain second interviewer (S5 8.1), who is from outside the domain by construction, assesses the same four reads, and cannot veto. Lands pre-opening for every seat as the founder stage. After the reset, for edge seats, it has no home. Named in 8.4.

The single-hand veto. Lands, narrowly, as the non-negotiable flag (section 9.6).

"TBD: sent back for additional information." Lands as the incomplete-by-content re-run (P49), which is a stage re-run rather than a packet sent back to a committee.

The CR admin, queuing and recording. The stack.

The recruiting member on the panel. No recruiting seat (S5 P48); the Maitre d holds the candidate-experience standard.

The regular review of aggregated outcomes. Lands as the calibration read's record at the monthly partners' domain read and its reading at the mechanism reset.

**P43 (Recommended).** The functions inside candidate review, the Bar Raiser, and the executive check land as mapped: framework application in field 8 and the kit; scorecard verification in the stack's shape, the lead's reading, and the calibration read; outcome verification partially, in the founder stage pre-opening and two calibration triggers after; the decision standard in the ninety-day read; feedback to interviewers on the Maitre d with the interviewer note; bias measurement as a pattern read with the composition position routed to Session 11; the veto as the non-negotiable flag; the send-back as the incomplete-by-content re-run. Leveling lands nowhere and needs nowhere. The outside-domain read for edge seats after the reset has no home and is surfaced in 8.4.

### 8.3 The calibration read, specified

What it is. A standing read of scorecards across packets, by one person, with a record, and without a meeting. It is not a committee: no one decides anything in it, no packet is reopened by it, and it produces notes to interviewers and proposals to the reset, not outcomes.

Who runs it. The Maitre d, who owns "Team development, training, and the invisible advocate function" (WP p.10) and therefore the interviewers' training. Pre-opening, before the Maitre d exists, the partner who is not the hiring partner, so that the person deciding the first packets is not the person calibrating them; that turns on S4 P4's ruling and is stated here as the team's recommendation inside that ruling.

When. Monthly, at the partners' domain read, beside the candidate pulse (S5 P30), reading the month's completed packets; and at ninety days per hire, the outcome read (section 7.6). At the mechanism reset, the whole record.

What it reads, per month. Every completed packet's scorecards, for: the presence lexicon (section 4.9); questions not asked without a reason; evidence filed under the wrong dimension; a developed competency scored as absent; a decline reason not on the sheet (P10); per interviewer, the rate of "does not meet" and "meets with evidence beyond" against other interviewers on the same seat, read as a pattern and not a number; the strand read (P25); hires against a "does not meet" and declines contradicting every scorecard (P45; S5 12.3); a disqualifier flag raised without evidence; and the source-dominance and stage-rate read's monthly view, which the leads' review already carries weekly (S5 I22). It reads no candidate's name into its record.

What it produces. A record on the partners' page, per month, anonymized by candidate: the findings by kind, the interviewers noted (by name to the Maitre d, by count in the record), the re-training flags issued, and any anchor or question the read proposes to revise at the reset (P26). An interviewer note per interviewer with a finding (I8). Nothing else.

What it refuses. A candidate's name in the record. Reopening a decision. A target for any rate. A figure. A meeting; the read is one person's scheduled time and its record. A verdict on an interviewer; a finding sends them to a module and a paired stage, and that is all it can do.

Four-part entry test. Cost: one person's scheduled block a month, and the ninety-day read per hire. Buys: the only mechanism on this page that can see the room's read becoming the hiring read across packets, and the only test of the hiring bar against the building's bar before Session 13's review exists. Runs: the Maitre d. Record: the partners' page.

**P44 (Recommended).** The calibration read: monthly by the Maitre d at the partners' domain read, pre-opening by the partner who is not the hiring partner, reading every completed packet for the presence lexicon, unasked questions, misfiled evidence, developed competencies scored, off-sheet declines, per-interviewer mark patterns, the strand read, hires against and declines against, and unevidenced flags; producing an anonymized record and interviewer notes; refusing names, reopenings, targets, figures, meetings, and verdicts.

### 8.4 Where this page found Session 5 positions that may need revisiting

Two, surfaced and not overwritten.

The hire-against trigger. Session 5's decision-rights entry 12.3 sends "a decline at the packet that contradicts every stage's scorecard" to the leads' review "as a calibration read." It has no mirror: a hire on a packet where a scorecard marked a dimension "does not meet" goes nowhere, and Session 5's rule that "The lead may not hire below the bar to fill a hole" (S5 P46) has no mechanism behind it. This page adds the mirror as an extension of 12.3, not a change to it: a hire on a packet carrying any "does not meet" on an assessed dimension goes to the leads' review as a calibration read, with the lead's dissent line (section 9.5) attached, and pre-opening to the calibrating partner. It does not stop the hire and it does not review it; it makes the pattern visible. The team marks it as an extension for Brandon to ratify alongside the S5 entry it extends.

The outside-domain read for edge seats after the reset. Session 5 P54 ends the founder stage for edge seats at the mechanism reset after dinner's first quarter. Her Bar Raiser's function, a trained read from outside the domain's urgency on every packet, then has no home for edge seats: the domain lead decides, the interviewers and the assessor are from the domain's strand, and the only outside eyes are the calibration read's, after the fact and across packets. Three options, argued. Keep P54 and rely on the assessor-decider separation, the scored practical, the calibration read, and the hire-against trigger; that is the lightest and it leaves the function to a read that runs monthly rather than per packet. Reverse P54 and keep the founder stage for edge seats; that keeps a founder at the last stage of every hire in a house "built to run without them" (WP p.6) and is the load-bearing answer. Make the adjacent-domain second interviewer the rule for every seat; that changes the runners Session 5 fixed and costs a trained person's block on every packet. The team recommends the first, ratified at the mechanism reset against the calibration read's record from dinner's first quarter, and names it as the one place this page found a Session 5 position that the function inside her chapter argues against. It does not change P54.

**P45 (Recommended).** The hire-against trigger, as an extension of S5 12.3: a hire on a packet carrying any "does not meet" on an assessed dimension goes to the leads' review as a calibration read with the lead's dissent line attached; pre-opening, to the calibrating partner. It stops nothing and reviews nothing; it makes the pattern visible. Ratified alongside the entry it extends.

**P46 (Founder-gated).** Whether an outside-domain read survives for edge seats after the founder stage ends at the mechanism reset (S5 P54). The team recommends keeping P54 and relying on the separation, the practical, the calibration read, and the hire-against trigger, ratified at the reset against the calibration record. The alternatives, keeping the founder stage for edge seats or making the adjacent-domain interviewer universal, are argued above. Surfaced as a possible revision to P54, not made.

**P47 (Founder-gated).** Bias measurement's data. The stage-rate read runs on what Greenhouse holds; whether the house asks candidates for self-identification is the founders' and is routed with the composition position to Session 11 (S5 P26). Until then the read is a pattern read on stage and source, stated as such, with no claim of significance.

## 9. The decision, and the packet

Session 5 fixed the decision: the domain lead's, on a complete packet of independently submitted scorecards, made the day the packet completes, with the answer sent the same day; no committee; a decline names a read or a must-have; Greenhouse refuses a decision on an incomplete packet; a hole is held rather than filled below the bar and the leads' review carries it as a slack question (S5 P46, I14; carryover `86akh7qkj`). This section specifies what the packet contains, how it completes, what the lead reads and in what order, what the lead may not do, how a dissent is carried, what the veto becomes, and what replaces her offer-approval channel.

### 9.1 What the packet contains

Session 5's I14 list, held whole: every scorecard the seat's class requires, each independently submitted; the sheet version handed; the transparency sheet version handed; the candidate's fourth-read answer; the completion date; the decision with the read named on decline; the lead's line on a hire against a founder's fourth-read flag; the decision and send dates; the deciding lead's name; the co-lead's scorecard for a lead seat's replacement. This page adds four items, each an extension named as such. The shadow-scored practical form, where the assessor's first practical carried a shadow (section 5.3), marked as the shadow. The paired stage's second scorecard, where a trainee ran the stage paired (section 7.2), marked as paired. The lead's dissent line, where the lead decides against any scorecard's recommendation (section 9.5). And, for lead seats, domain-owner seats, and the chef seat under Branch B, the reference note (section 10), which is the extension surfaced as a conflict in 10.2 because Session 5's packet did not carry one.

### 9.2 How the packet completes, and incomplete by content

The packet completes when the last required scorecard arrives; the stack date-stamps it (S5 I14). It is also incomplete by content, and the stack treats it so, when a dimension the stage was assigned to assess is marked "not assessed" on every scorecard from that stage, because the stage did not do what field 8 assigned it. The remedy is her "TBD: sent back for additional information" (Book p.206) with no committee to send it to: the stage is re-run, by the same or another trained runner as the Maitre d decides, at the house's cost in time and the candidate's convenience, inside the window, with the candidate told the same day why (the house did not reach a question it owed them), and the window pausing for the candidate as it does for a cancelled practical (S5 12.5). A single scorecard's "not assessed" on a dimension a second scorecard from the same stage did assess does not make the packet incomplete; the lead reads the one that did.

**P48 (Recommended).** A packet is incomplete by content when a dimension assigned to a stage is "not assessed" on every scorecard from that stage; the stage is re-run inside the window with the candidate told why. There is no send-back to a committee.

### 9.3 What the lead reads, in what order

The order is fixed so that evidence is read before recommendations and flags before everything.

First, the disqualifier flags. Any flag with evidence ends the candidacy; nothing else is read; the answer names the non-negotiable as the read (S5 P47); the line goes to both leads that day (S5 12.3). A flag without evidence is read as no flag and goes to the calibration read as a training item.

Second, dimension 4. Every item the candidate said they need, with each stage's mark. Where any item is marked "cannot supply," the lead writes on the packet what the building will do instead, or declines on dimension 4 with the gap named, which is the record's own read ("whether the building is equipped," WP p.18) and is not the candidate's failure; the answer says so in those terms. Where an item is "unknown," the lead resolves it before deciding, the same day, by asking the Operations Lead or the Maitre d, and writes the resolution.

Third, dimensions 1 to 3, scorecard by scorecard, evidence lines first and marks after, against the anchors on field 8. The lead reads the practical's rows before the interview's lines for a physical-skill seat, because the practical is the work sample.

Fourth, the recommendations, last, so that the lead has formed a reading of the evidence before seeing what each runner concluded.

Fifth, the reference note where the seat carries one (section 10).

The lead does not read a résumé; there is no résumé stage (S5 P38), and an optional parsed résumé exists only for the phone screen's must-haves (S5 I4). The lead does not read the candidate's format choice (S5 P31). The lead does not read another candidate's packet while deciding this one.

Then the decision. The lead may hire a candidate whose packet meets the sheet's bar, meaning every assessed dimension marked "meets" or beyond on the scorecards that assessed it, or, where scorecards disagree, on the evidence the lead reads as meeting it with the dissent line written (9.5). The lead may decline any candidate with a dimension named. The decision is made the day the packet completes and the answer goes the same day (S5 P46, P47).

One seat, two packets that meet the bar. Her final round has "at least three candidates" met by the panel (Book p.194). Sŏn decides packets as they complete, because "Speed is respect" (WP p.18) and a candidate never waits for another candidate. A packet that meets the bar while the seat's count on the inventory is open (S5 I1) is hired. A packet that meets the bar after the count fills is not declined on a read, because there is no read to name; it is answered with the stage reached, the fact that the seat's count filled, and the pool offered by name (S5 P47), and the answer set gains that template. Where two packets complete on the same day for one count, the lead reads "meets with evidence beyond" and the dimension-4 marks, decides, and the other is answered as above. This is an extension of S5 I15's templates and is named as one.

**P49 (Recommended).** The lead reads flags, then dimension 4, then dimensions 1 to 3 evidence before marks with the practical's rows first, then recommendations, then any reference note; hires a packet that meets the bar while the seat's count is open; declines with a dimension named; and answers a bar-meeting packet after the count fills with the stage reached, the count filled, and the pool offered, which adds one template to the answer set.

### 9.4 What the lead may not do

Session 5's list holds: may not hire below the bar to fill a hole; may not override a non-negotiable flag; may not decide on a packet missing a scorecard; may not decide their own seat's replacement without the co-lead's scorecard (S5 P46). This page adds: may not edit, annotate, or return a scorecard, except the incomplete-by-content re-run (P48); may not ask a runner to change a mark or an evidence line, before or after submission; may not discuss a candidate with a runner before that runner's scorecard is submitted, including when the lead is the other interviewer (S5 P40); may not hold a completed packet open to see another candidate; may not decide on anything not in the packet, including what the lead heard about the candidate from anyone outside a stage; may not decide the day after completion; may not decline on "fit," presence, format, or a developed competency (S5 12.3); may not decline a candidate who asked about the mechanics, the range, or the schedule (S5 12.3); may not make the offer's terms anything but the sheet's (section 11); and may not carry a candidate's identity into any conversation outside Greenhouse, including with the other lead, except the lines the triggers route (S5 12.3, P45).

### 9.5 How a dissent is carried

There is no committee to carry a dissent, so the packet carries it. A scorecard whose recommendation the lead decides against stays in the packet unchanged, permanently. The lead writes a dissent line on the packet: the dimension the runner named, the evidence line it rested on, and the evidence in the packet the lead read as outweighing it, in that form and no other. The runner receives the decision and the dissent line through the stack the same day the answer goes (section 8.2, transparency). The runner may not appeal; the runner's scorecard is the appeal and it is already in the record. Repeated dissents by one runner overridden by one lead, in either direction, are a calibration pattern (P44), read as a question about the anchors first, the runner second, and the lead third.

Her "Really listen to interviewers' input" (Book p.204) is therefore a system property: the input is read, answered in writing, and kept. Her "Bring the organization along" (Book p.204) has no organization to bring pre-opening, and after opening it is done by the seat sheet being public and the domain's nodes reading it, not by a meeting. Her "Decide based on what's best for the company, not for the team or individual" (Book p.204), with its warning that this "is easier said than done, especially when the candidate has extensive experience or was referred by someone at the company," is the held hole (S5 P46) and the referrer's exclusion from every stage (S5 P24).

**P50 (Recommended).** A dissent is carried by the packet: the overridden scorecard stays unchanged, the lead writes a dissent line naming the runner's dimension and evidence and the packet evidence read as outweighing it, the runner receives it the same day, and repeated overrides in either direction are a calibration pattern read anchors first.

### 9.6 The veto, under a structure with no committee

"We also have a norm that anyone on the hiring committee can veto a hire. This is an extremely rare occurrence. I can only think of one instance of this happening, when a candidate's answers made an interviewer feel personally uncomfortable" (Book p.203). Her norm is a standing right held by everyone on a committee, exercised on conviction, and rare by culture.

At Sŏn the veto exists in exactly one form and is rare by structure. The non-negotiable flag: any runner at any stage, including the phone screener, the assessor, the seat holder beside the candidate on a practical (through the assessor's people rows), the adjacent-domain interviewer, the partners at the dinner, and a founder at the founder stage, may flag a disqualifier from field 9 with evidence, and the flag ends the candidacy regardless of every other scorecard (S5 12.3, 12.4). No one may override it, not the lead and not a founder. Her one instance, a candidate whose answers made an interviewer personally uncomfortable, is at Sŏn a toxicity flag with the answer as evidence, and it ends the candidacy the same way. The flag is not a conviction and not a right; it is evidence against a named line, and a flag without evidence is not a flag (P44). No other dissent blocks: a competency dissent is carried (9.5), never a veto.

**P51 (Recommended).** The only veto at Sŏn is the non-negotiable flag, raisable by any runner at any stage with evidence, ending the candidacy, overridable by no one. Competency dissent never blocks. Her personally-uncomfortable case is a toxicity flag here.

### 9.7 Her offer-approval channel, refused

Stripe's channel: recruiters post the planned offer in a consistent format, "the proposed job level, a link to the hiring committee summary, and all of the individual interviewer and reference feedback," and a leader commits to "a 24-hour turnaround time for approval," with the leader's role "to review the information and approve the hire or ask clarifying questions," and an occasional veto "when the interview feedback and references were lukewarm or when the panel indicated that the person was not top talent" (Book p.197). It is a review from above with a turnaround.

Sŏn has no approver above the lead: the ledger forbids a person standing above another reviewing their decision, and Session 5 made the founder's part a stage rather than a review for that reason (S5 10.1). What does the channel's work is the stack's own refusals, which are not approvals: no decision on an incomplete packet (S5 I14), no hire with a non-negotiable flag (S5 I14), no offer before item 6 is filled (S5 P35, row 47), no start date before the mentor and the modules are ready (S5 12.6); and the two calibration triggers, which make a pattern visible without stopping a hire (P45; S5 12.3). Her turnaround commitment survives as the thing it was protecting, speed: the decision the day the packet completes and the answer the same day, which is faster than twenty-four hours and needs no approver to be fast. Pre-opening, the hiring partner decides (S5 P49) and a disagreement between partners on a lead seat is held on the interim two-partner decision rule (S5 12.4).

**P52 (Recommended).** Her offer-approval channel is refused as a review from above. Its work is done by the stack's refusals (incomplete packet, non-negotiable flag, item 6 unfilled, start-date conditions) and the two calibration triggers, and its speed by the same-day decision and answer.

## 10. References

### 10.1 Her material, and what the record says

"Not making reference calls is a mistake" (Book p.207). Her reasoning is that the interviews were "only a set of 30- to 45-minute interviews, and you now have a chance to collect more data" (Book p.207). Her mechanics: the recruiter and hiring manager share the calls; an entry-level role "may only need to make one call" and the recruiter makes it; the more senior the role the more references, and "the hiring manager makes at least one of those calls, ideally to the candidate's current manager" (Book p.207); the candidate chose the references, so "think what a signal it is if they're just lukewarm. Do not hire that person" (Book p.207); the percentile question, "Would you say this person is in the top 50 percent of folks you've worked with?" then twenty, ten, five, because "99 percent of the time, people will be honest and accurate when asked for data" (Book p.207); and seven behavioral questions: where in three years, the last time you did not see eye to eye, ways they were helpful to others, a time you coached them, a 1 to 10 with no 7, a skill you saw them grow, and advice for me as their manager (Book p.207 to 208). For leaders: the candidate will not want their current company called and "it's generally not okay to press on this"; go deep on prior companies and ask for someone they trust at the current one; back-channel references are "risky" and are done only with the candidate's permission, "I'll only talk to folks I know we can trust" (Book p.215).

The record says nothing about references. The Talent acquisition section names every stage it wants and does not name a reference check (WP p.17 to 18). Session 5 did not touch it, and its stage inventory has no reference stage and its packet no reference note (S5 P38, I14). The brief's phrasing, "the record's own bar, that a lukewarm reference is disqualifying," is her bar (Book p.207), not the record's, and this section treats it as hers and tests it.

### 10.2 Whether Sŏn checks references, and for which seats

The question splits by seat class, and the Realist owns the edge.

Edge seats, high-volume and physical-skill. The Realist's read of a floor hire in a tight Austin market (WP p.16): the prior employer is often unreachable inside five days; the prior manager in this industry is, with some regularity, the person the candidate's third-read account described, so the reference is the toxic actor being asked about the person who left them; the industry's habit of answering a reference call with dates of employment and nothing else makes the call a formality that costs the window; and a candidate who left a room because it exploited them cannot name that room's manager as a reference and is then read as having no references, which punishes exactly the person the non-negotiables exist to protect. Against that, the profile's ranking of predictors places the work sample first and reference checks near the bottom, and the record's work sample is the paid practical in a real environment (WP p.18), which reads the thing a reference would be asked about, whether the person does the work, directly. And "Speed is respect" (WP p.18): a reference call is a stage the candidate does not see and waits on. So for edge seats, no reference calls. The practical is the reference. Her entry-level one call, made by a recruiter (Book p.207), has no recruiter to make it and nothing to read that the practical did not.

Lead seats, domain-owner seats, and the chef seat under Branch B. Here her reasoning holds and the Realist agrees: a lead seat has no practical, its work sample is a worked scenario in a chair (section 4.8) and a dinner as a customer, and a person who will hold a domain with no apex above them is a person about whom the house should hear from someone who watched them hold something. For these seats the house makes reference calls.

The form is not a stage. Session 5 fixed the stage inventory and this page adds no stage (carryover `86akh7qdf`). References are part of the decision's due diligence, carried out by the deciding party between the first interview and the last round, so that the note is in the packet when the last scorecard arrives and the decision is still made the day the packet completes (S5 P46). The note is an item in the packet like the sheet version and the fourth-read answer, not a scorecard, and it carries no mark on any dimension. This is an extension of Session 5's packet (I14), which carried no reference note, and it is surfaced as such: Session 5 refused her recruiter and hiring manager sharing "the reference call responsibilities" (Book p.207) implicitly by having neither, and it did not decide references at all. The team recommends the extension and Brandon ratifies it with I14.

Who calls. For the two leads pre-opening, a partner; the hiring partner where S4 P4 is ruled, and the team recommends both partners each make one call so the note carries two readings. For a lead seat after opening, the co-lead and a partner. For a domain-owner seat, the domain lead who will decide, which is the one place on this page the decider gathers evidence directly; it is permitted because the note is not a scorecard, is read by the co-lead at the calibration read, and cannot be the basis of a decline (10.3). For the chef under Branch B, both founders. Never an edge interviewer, never the adjacent-domain second interviewer alone, and never without the candidate's consent to each person called, by name, because "Bear in mind that the candidate provided these references" (Book p.207) and at Sŏn the candidate also approves them.

What is asked. The documented questions, the same for every reference for the seat, in the candidate's interview language where the reference speaks it. Her percentile question is kept, because a percentile is not a financial figure and because her observation that people answer data questions honestly is one the Realist's experience confirms; it is asked once, in her ascending form, and the answer is recorded as given. Her seven questions are adapted to the seat: where the person will be in three years (kept); the last time you did not see eye to eye (kept, as the third-read evidence from outside); ways they were helpful to people who were not above them (adapted, because the non-negotiables are about the people the load-bearing model places below); a time you coached them and what they did (kept, as the second dimension from outside); the 1 to 10 with no 7 (kept, recorded as given); a skill you saw them grow on their own (adapted to self-directed mastery); and the advice question, adapted from "as a manager" to "for the people who will work beside them and for the building," because the answer feeds the fourth read and the ninety-day plan, not a manager's transition. Two questions are added for lead seats: what this person took on that belonged to someone else, and whether they could say what they would refuse to do, both from the section 4.8 block, so the reference is asked the same things the candidate was.

What is recorded. The reference note: who was called, their relation to the candidate and the years, the candidate's consent by name, the answers to each documented question in the reference's words, the percentile answer and the 1 to 10 as given, and the caller's name. No mark, no recommendation, no verdict. It stays in Greenhouse with the packet and never travels to the person page.

**P53 (Recommended).** No reference calls for edge seats. The paid practical is the work sample and reads what a reference would be asked; a call in a tight market inside a five-day window costs the candidate time, often reaches the actor the candidate left, and predicts less than the practical.

**P54 (Recommended).** Reference calls for the two lead seats, domain-owner seats, and the chef seat under Branch B, made by the deciding party between the first interview and the last round, with the candidate's consent to each person called, asking the documented questions (her percentile question, her seven adapted to the seat, and two from the lead block), recorded as a reference note in the packet with no mark or verdict. This extends Session 5's packet (I14), which carried no reference note, and is surfaced as an extension for ratification with it.

### 10.3 What a lukewarm reference means, tested

Her rule is absolute: lukewarm, do not hire (Book p.207). Tested against the seats Sŏn calls references for, it is right in one case and wrong in two.

Right: when the candidate chose the reference, the reference speaks freely, and every answer is general, no instance for the disagreement question, nothing they were helpful with, no skill grown, a 6. That is a reference saying what they can and it is read as evidence, and the lead may read it as outweighing.

Wrong, first: when the reference is the current employer of a lead candidate who could not, for confidentiality, be asked directly (Book p.215), and the lukewarmness is the reference protecting themselves or the candidate; that is a note to read for what it does not say, not a rule. Wrong, second: when the reference is a person from the load-bearing model asked about a person who held a domain in it. A manager whose report resolved problems without asking, refused to take work that was not theirs, and left for a room that would let them hold a table, will often describe that person as difficult, and "the last time you didn't see eye to eye" will produce the instance. At Sŏn that account is evidence for dimension 3, comfort with distributed authority, and it reads the other way from her rule.

So a lukewarm reference is never disqualifying by itself. It is evidence in the packet, read against the rest, and the lead may decline on the dimension the reference's account bears on, naming it, or hire with a dissent line that says what the reference said and why the packet outweighed it. A reference note cannot be the sole basis of a decline, because the candidate cannot be sent an answer that names a read they were never asked about; a decline that rests on a reference names the dimension and the evidence from a stage the candidate sat.

**P55 (Recommended).** A lukewarm reference is evidence, not a disqualifier: read against the packet, it may support a decline on a named dimension or be outweighed with a dissent line, and it is never the sole basis of a decline. A load-bearing manager's account of a person who held a domain is read for what it shows about distributed authority.

### 10.4 Back-channels, and confidentiality

Her rule on back-channel references is adopted whole: they are done only with the candidate's permission, asked in her form, "Is it okay if I reach out to them confidentially? I'll only talk to folks I know we can trust," with the candidate free to ask who (Book p.215). A back-channel without consent is refused, and a person who ran one has carried a candidate's identity outside Greenhouse (S5 12.7). For a lead candidate currently in a top role elsewhere, her confidentiality rules apply in full: "keep calendars private, and only provide high-level updates" (Book p.210). At Sŏn that means Greenhouse access to a lead seat's pipeline limited to the runners and the deciding party, no candidate's name in any leads' review or partners' review record (S5 I22), the dinner in the room booked under the partners' name and the server told nothing (S5 P36), and the build showing the search as the founders' search and never a candidate (S5 P18).

**P56 (Recommended).** Back-channel references only with the candidate's consent, asked in her form, and refused otherwise. A lead candidate's confidentiality is held by limited Greenhouse access, no name in any cadence record, the dinner booked as the partners' table, and the build showing no candidate.

## 11. The offer, and the close

### 11.1 Her rules, and what the record already fixed

"An offer letter is a critical document, and any stock plan associated with an offer is a legal agreement. Take these seriously and seek to automate generating and sending the correct documents early in your company development" (Book p.208). "You don't send an offer letter to a candidate until after the recruiter or hiring manager has extended the offer verbally, so if anything about that offer is a surprise, your process has failed" (Book p.208). "Ultimately, nothing at this stage should come as a surprise" (Book p.208 to 209). "Time kills all deals" (Book p.209), and the sell chat with "a person who is critical to their role or even an executive" (Book p.209).

The record fixed most of it before the offer: the transparency sheet at the first interview with "everything needed to decide" and "No surprises and nothing withheld" (WP p.18). Session 5 fixed that the sheet is "the version the offer is made on, with any change between interview and offer stated in the offer" (S5 7.4), that no offer goes while item 6 reads "being finalized" (S5 P35), that the offer carries the sheet version, the start date's conditions, the range as accepted, and the mentor's seat (S5 I15), that the range travels with the offer (carryover `86akh5ut1`), and that the person page is created at the offer (S5 P16). Her "nothing a surprise" is therefore a system property at Sŏn: the offer is the sheet the candidate has held since the first interview, and the only new content is the start date and the mentor.

### 11.2 The offer conversation

The offer is extended aloud, by the domain lead (pre-opening the hiring partner), the day the decision is made, in the format the candidate chose for their stages, before any written document goes. What is said, in order: that the house is offering the seat, by its working name and strand; the sheet version, and that nothing in it has changed since the first interview or, where something has, what; the domain lead the person will work with, by seat (S5 P33); the range the seat carries, from item 8, read as what the holder decides without asking (P11); the start date and its conditions (S5 12.6), and that the mentor is being connected; the mentor's seat; that the written offer follows through the stack and that it will say exactly this; how long the offer stands open (11.4); and that the candidate may ask anything and may ask for a conversation with a partner or the other lead before answering. Nothing about pay is said that item 6 does not say, and item 6 is filled or the offer does not go. "Welcome" is not said, because canon forbids it in written copy and the offer conversation is the written offer read aloud (Canon v1.0, section 09); the sentence is "The seat is yours if you want it," or its equivalent in the candidate's language, and it ends there.

Who extends it. The domain lead, because the lead decided and the lead is the node the person will work with. A partner does not extend an edge offer, because a founder extending an offer the lead decided puts a person above the lead at the one moment the candidate is deciding whom they work for. For the two lead seats pre-opening, the hiring partner extends it and the other partner is on the call. For the chef seat, under either branch, both founders.

**P57 (Recommended).** The offer is extended aloud by the domain lead the day of the decision, in the candidate's chosen format, stating the seat, the sheet version and any change, the domain lead, the range as what the holder decides, the start date and its conditions, the mentor's seat, that the written offer follows and matches, how long it stands, and that questions are welcome. A partner extends only lead and chef offers.

### 11.3 The written offer

The written offer is generated by the stack from the packet and the sheet: the seat, the sheet version, the transparency sheet version, the range, the start date and its conditions, the mentor's seat, the service periods, and item 6's content as ratified. Its legal content, the employment terms the law requires it to carry, is the Operations Lead's with counsel and is not this page's; this page fixes only that the legal document and the offer conversation say the same thing, and that a discrepancy between them is the failure her flipped-figures anecdote describes (Book p.208). Document generation is configuration to confirm: whether Greenhouse's offer templates carry the fields, or Rippling generates the document from the new-hire record, or both, is resolved at configuration and recorded; the seam is the offer accepted (S5 P31). The written offer goes the same day as the conversation, in the candidate's language, in the correspondence register (S5 I15), and its acceptance creates the person page (S5 P16) and opens Rippling's onboarding.

**P58 (Recommended).** The written offer is generated from the packet and the sheet, matches the conversation word for word in substance, carries the legal content the Operations Lead sets with counsel, goes the same day, and its acceptance is the seam to Rippling and the person page. Document generation is configuration to confirm.

### 11.4 The sell chat, and the dinner in the room

Her sell chat is a fifteen-minute call after the offer with a leader who will "share their enthusiasm about the role and the candidate," in which the candidate "wants to be inspired" and wants to know how the leader views the role, the company's prospects, what excites the leader, and the challenges (Book p.209). The dinner in the room is not that. The dinner is a scored stage before the decision, with independent scorecards and no offer at the table (S5 P45); the sell chat is after the decision, unscored, and its purpose is the candidate's decision, not the house's. They are not the same thing and the dinner does not do the sell chat's work.

What does. The transparency sheet answers her first and fourth questions, the role and its challenges, in writing, before the offer. The documented build answers the second and third, the company's prospects and what excites the founders, at a depth no call can (S5 P17), and a candidate who has not watched it is pointed to it. What remains is the candidate's own questions after the offer, and those are answered by a conversation the candidate asks for, with the domain lead, the other lead, or a partner, inside the offer's open period, on the clock for the person answering. It is not offered as a performance. Canon's master register is "present without performing" (Canon v1.0, section 01; Canon v3.0, p.27), the record's belonging thesis is that "You cannot truly belong somewhere that is performing for you" (WP p.25), and a leader "sharing their enthusiasm" to close a candidate is the house performing for a person it is about to ask to stop performing for customers. Her "Most of all, the candidate wants to be inspired" (Book p.209) is refused in that form; the candidate is answered.

Her "Time kills all deals" (Book p.209) is turned around: the house's speed is a rule (the same-day decision and answer), and the candidate's time is theirs. The offer stands open for the house's response window, five days (S5 P29), and a candidate who asks for more is given it once, with a date, and told that the seat's count stays open for them until that date. A candidate who does not answer by the date is asked once, and then the seat's count reopens and the candidate is offered the pool. The house does not chase.

**P59 (Recommended).** The sell chat and the dinner in the room are different things: the dinner is a scored stage before the decision; the after-offer conversation is unscored, asked for by the candidate, with the lead, the other lead, or a partner, and never a performance. The sheet and the build answer her four sell-chat questions in advance.

**P60 (Recommended).** The offer stands open five days, extended once at the candidate's request to a stated date, after which the candidate is asked once and the count reopens with the pool offered. The house does not chase.

### 11.5 No negotiation

Her leadership section says compensation negotiation should be handled by "someone who is not the CEO or the hiring manager," because a candidate "may assume that you can overrule any compensation framework to get them what they're asking for" (Book p.215). At Sŏn the answer is structural: there is no negotiation, because the mechanics are published on the sheet at the first interview (WP p.18), the same for every candidate for the seat (S5 I5), and the architecture is Session 13's to ratify and no one's to overrule at an offer (carryover `86akh3vrq`). What no one may change at the offer: the range, the commitments, item 6, the seat's name, or anything on the sheet. A candidate who asks for a term the sheet does not hold is told that the sheet is the seat and that the house does not make different terms for different people in the same seat, which is the record's "no hierarchy of importance" (WP p.20) applied to people, and the answer is written in the correspondence register without apology.

**P61 (Recommended).** There is no offer negotiation. ## 12. Levels, and what hiring needs from them

### 12.1 Her structure, and her caution

"The next decision required for an offer is where to place the candidate in terms of job level, which then determines their compensation" (Book p.198). Her Table 3 gives six levels by years of relevant experience plus scope, from entry-level postgrad through senior director or executive (Book p.199), and her Table 4 describes the same six, ENTRY, DEVELOPING, CAREER, ADVANCED, EXPERT, PRINCIPAL, across an analogy, knowledge, job complexity, and supervision (Book p.200 to 201; the tables are column-fragmented in the extraction and no cell is quoted here). Her own caution runs against her tables: "the years of experience can be a red herring" (Book p.199); and "I would caution against overworking job ladders because they can quickly turn into checklists that employees feel they can meet in order to get promoted. Remember the distinction between skills and capabilities ... skills are easier to check off a list. Capabilities, such as the ability to collaborate and partner with others, are not as easy to capture" (Book p.199). Her sales leader's line is the sharpest thing in the section: "You need to help me by finishing the work on the role expectations and company job level structure. How am I supposed to set expectations and compensation without that?" (Book p.198).

Everything after the tables is compensation: salary bands, target offer points, the compa-ratio and its worked figures, the pay parity review, set salaries with motivators as bonus and equity, compensation as the largest expense, and planning by heads or by budget (Book p.199 to 202). None of it enters this page. Where the section below needs what that material would supply, it says the figure is not a context source and states the gap.

### 12.2 What the record has instead

The record has no levels and says so structurally. "Progression develops connections, not altitude. A person who has been in the system longer does not move toward the center. Their node develops more threads" (WP p.11). "New people always enter at the outside edge. The web never acquires altitude, only reach" (WP p.9). Below the leads "the web is composed of strands, not levels" (WP p.10). What it has in place of a ladder: "Every position has documented competency requirements visible to anyone" (WP p.11), the first unlock "After a set period in any role" (WP p.11), the invisible advocate, and the ready-now pool; and one open item, "Role naming convention, a separate session, titles held to be scale-independent and non-concept-specific" (WP p.15). Levels and ladders as a question, whether a single house needs them at all, are routed to Sessions 8 and 13 (`86ajgnj4e`), and this page does not close them.

### 12.3 What the hiring loop needs from them, exactly

Three things, and no more.

An entry bar per seat. Field 6's first horizon, what must be demonstrable at entry, as competencies not credentials (S5 P12). This exists as soon as the seat sheet does, and the loop reads against it at every stage.

A fixed unlock period per seat. Field 6's second horizon and the set period after which it unlocks, fixed on the sheet before anyone is hired (S5 P60), because the transparency sheet's item 7, the advancement path, cannot be handed without it and the second read's visible-path question (P20) describes it to the candidate.

From Session 13, the entry pay rule. Whether a person entering a seat is paid at one point for that seat, or whether anything about them varies it; and for the lead seats, what "salaried" means on a day-weighted point system (finding 18.7). The loop can run first interviews without it under P35 and cannot make an offer without it (S5 P35). This is the one place the loop needs a compensation fact, and the fact is not a context source for this program; the gap is stated and it is Session 13's.

What the loop does not need. A placement decision. Her sequence, select the candidate, set the level, then set the compensation (Book p.199), has no second step at Sŏn: everyone enters at the outside edge (WP p.9), the seat's entry point is the seat's, and a candidate who arrives with more than the entry bar requires is not placed higher; they are hired into the seat and the invisible advocate's self-paced unlocks are how a fast mover moves fast ("Advancement requires preparation, not permission," WP p.14). Her leveling outcomes (hire at a higher or lower level, Book p.206) have no object. Her compa-ratio has no band to divide. Her "very experienced Level 3" placed "at the higher end of the salary band" (Book p.200) is, at Sŏn, a person hired into a seat at the seat's entry point who reaches the first unlock sooner.

### 12.4 What breaks if the answer is that Sŏn has none

Nothing in the hiring loop breaks, provided the three things above exist. That is the honest answer and it is stated plainly: a house with documented competency requirements at two horizons per seat, a fixed unlock period, and a ratified entry pay rule can run every stage on this page, decide every packet, and make every offer without a level or a ladder.

What breaks is elsewhere, and hiring is where it would be felt first. If Session 13 answers "none" and does not also supply the entry pay rule, no offer can go (S5 P35), and the hiring calendar's leads' offers slide (P16). If the unlock periods are not fixed on the sheets, item 7 cannot be handed and the second read's visible-path question describes a mechanism that does not run, and the candidate has been told a path that is not there, which is the transparency thesis failing at the first interview. If the review rubric (Session 13) scores against anything but field 6 at the person's horizon, the mismatch Brandon named appears: hired against one list, reviewed against another. And the record's leadership seats, the two leads and the chef, are the one place "no levels" has a real cost: they are hired into the center, not the edge (finding 18.3), and the record has no word for what they enter at, which the compensation architecture will have to give a name to without giving it altitude.

Her ladder caution is absorbed rather than refused. The record's competency requirements are, on their face, the checklist she warns against; what keeps them from becoming one is the practical assessment that validates each unlock (WP p.11, p.19) and the playground's requirement that training progress "from procedure mastery to autonomous judgment" (Canon v1.0, section 01). Her capabilities, "the ability to collaborate and partner with others," are what the practical assessment reads at each horizon when it reads reproduction under load beside other people; they are not on a checklist because they are not checked, they are watched. Her set-salary idea, "jobs should have set salaries, and that all compensation motivators should exist solely as bonus and equity vehicles," with a role's salary changing "if market data changes" and otherwise only by changing roles (Book p.201), is noted as a shape only, since it resembles a seat carrying its own point rather than a person carrying a negotiated one, and the shape is Session 13's to consider with no figure attached.

**P62 (Recommended).** The hiring loop needs three things from levels or their absence: an entry bar per seat (field 6's first horizon), a fixed unlock period per seat, and from Session 13 the entry pay rule including what "salaried" means for a lead. It needs no placement decision; every hire enters at the seat's entry point and moves by unlock. Levels and ladders are Sessions 8 and 13's and are not closed here.

**P63 (Recommended).** If Sŏn has no levels, nothing in the loop breaks, provided the three things exist. What breaks without them is the offer (no entry pay rule), item 7 and the visible-path question (no unlock period), and the review (a rubric scored against anything but field 6). The lead seats are where "no altitude" carries a real cost and the architecture must name what they enter at.

**P64 (Recommended).** Her ladder caution is absorbed: the competency requirements are kept from becoming a checklist by the practical assessment at each unlock, which watches capability rather than checking it. Her set-salary shape is noted for Session 13 with no figure.

## 14. Hiring internally

### 14.1 Her process, and the record's mechanism

"The first step is to evaluate whether you have just one internal candidate or whether multiple people might step forward ... If there really is just one person for the job, even after putting up an internal job posting, don't run a sham process" (Book p.213). Where there might be more than one, "run an internal selection process," lightweight, "the hiring manager and maybe one or two other leaders to interview each candidate and then have a hiring committee discussion," with "a feedback loop to help them understand what they should develop" and confidentiality for the unselected (Book p.213 to 214). And "One way to reduce friction in this process is to meet with a few external candidates and make clear that you did so" (Book p.213).

The record's mechanism is different in kind. "After a set period in any role, the next skill set unlocks automatically. It is self-paced. When complete, the practical assessment auto-schedules with the appropriate domain lead. If no position is open, the employee enters a ready-now pool" (WP p.11). "When a key person leaves ... the ready-now pool surfaces who is furthest along on the required track" (WP p.14). Session 5 fixed that the pool is read before any external source on tension loss (S5 P60), that a pool candidate for a critical-path seat takes the seat's paid practical in a real service scored like any candidate's (S5 P61), and that an outside lead hire after dinner's first quarter carries a written reason (S5 P60). It also fixed the pre-opening condition: there is no inside yet, the pool is empty on day one, and the commitment binds from the first tension loss after dinner opens (S5 11.2).

### 14.2 What an internal loop looks like when it arrives

The pool read. When a seat opens by tension loss or period expansion, the stack surfaces who in the pool is on the seat's track and how far along, before any external source is read (S5 P60, I24). The domain lead reads it and the sheet records the reading. If the pool holds no one on the track, the seat opens externally and the sheet says why (S5 P60); no sham process is possible because no internal process was run.

One person in the pool. Her rule, "don't run a sham process," holds and has no work to do, because the pool is the process. The person's competencies are documented and validated; the practical assessment that put them in the pool was real and could have been failed; and the seat's paid practical in a real service (S5 P61) is a stage, scored on the rows (section 5.2), that can also be failed. There is no interview, because the person's dimensions 1 to 3 are on the person page from ninety days of check-ins and each unlock, and an interview would be the house asking a person it already knows to perform themselves. There is a dimension-4 conversation, the fourth read asked again by the domain lead, because what a person needs from the building changes with the seat. The decision is the domain lead's, on the practical's form and the person page, the day the form is submitted. A person who does not reproduce the seat's rows on the practical is not declined; they are told which rows, the rows are what the platform teaches, and the pool entry stands with the date of the next practical. That is her "feedback loop to help them understand what they should develop" (Book p.214) as a system property.

More than one person in the pool. Every person on the track takes the seat's practical, scored by an assessor who does not decide, on the same rows, in real services the leads' review has not flagged, one per service (P31). The domain lead decides on the forms and the person pages; the hire-against and decline-against triggers apply (P45; S5 12.3). Those not chosen are told the rows and the dimension, and their pool entries stand.

Lead seats from inside. The pool surfaces a person on a lead seat's track. The internal loop for a lead seat runs the co-lead's first-interview block (section 4.8), because holding a domain without an apex has not been demonstrated by holding a node, and the dinner in the room, because the record names it for leadership seats and does not exempt a person who has worked the floor (WP p.18); the read, whether the person can be the customer the house is built around, is not answered by having served customers. It does not run references, because the person page is the reference. The decision is the partners' on the packet; the written line on the partners' page is not needed, since the hire is from the pool.

Cross-domain. A person surfaced for a seat in another domain, a server on the kitchen's track, takes that domain's practical scored on that domain's rows, chef-gated for the kitchen (S5 P44), and the domain lead of the receiving domain decides. Who signs off a cross-domain competency on the way into the pool is a finding (18.8).

**P69 (Recommended).** The internal loop is the pool read first, then, for one person, the seat's paid practical in a real service and a dimension-4 conversation with the decision on the form and the person page; for several, the same practical for each with the decision on the forms; for a lead seat, the lead block and the dinner in the room, with the person page as the reference. No interview re-reads what the person page holds, and a failed practical is rows named and a pool entry that stands.

### 14.3 Her external validation, refused

"Meet with a few external candidates and make clear that you did so" (Book p.213), to show "that you selected the best candidate across all potential avenues." At Sŏn this is refused, and it is refused because it is a sham of the opposite kind: it runs external candidates through stages the house does not intend to decide on, which is a breach of "Respect for time is the first principle" (WP p.17) and of no ghosting in spirit, since a candidate interviewed to validate someone else has been ghosted with an answer. The record's rule is the reverse: the pool is read first and an external search opens only when it holds no one on the track (S5 P60). The signal her practice sends, that the internal choice was rigorous, is sent at Sŏn by the practical the internal candidate took and could have failed.

**P70 (Recommended).** External candidates are never met to validate an internal choice. The pool is read first, the external search opens only on an empty track, and the internal candidate's real-service practical is the rigor.

### 14.4 What the first internal loop owes the people who do not get it

Her list: confidentiality, since "the others will likely want to return to their current roles without having to explain" (Book p.214); a feedback loop on what to develop; and the announcement that explains "what you were looking for, the process you ran, and why the person you selected emerged" (Book p.214).

At Sŏn, the first internal loop owes the unchosen four things. The rows and the dimension, in writing, the day the decision is made, which is the answer every candidate is owed (S5 P47) and is here also the person's next unlock. Their pool entry unchanged, with the date of the next practical, so that not being chosen this time is a date and not a verdict. Nothing on their person page but the practical's date and the rows, as the invisible advocate would write them, and no scorecard (S5 P16). And the house's own record: the internal promotion rate is a committed measure (WP p.23), and the first internal loop is where it starts being real rather than stated, so the loop's record on the partners' page is what the founders read to know whether the succession commitment held.

Confidentiality for an internal candidate is partial by structure and the page says so. The pool is visible: "Every position has documented competency requirements visible to anyone" (WP p.11), and a person's progress is on their own page. The team recommends that each person sees their own pool entry, the domain leads and the Maitre d see the pool for their tracks, and no one else sees who is in it; that a practical for an internal candidate is scheduled as a practical without the seat named on the schedule the floor reads; and that the announcement, which the record does not describe, names the person and the seat and says nothing about who else was in the pool. Her "celebrate" (Book p.213) is not the house's register; the brief's one thing for the week (S4 P26) carries the seat's new holder as a fact.

**P71 (Recommended).** The unchosen in an internal loop are owed the rows and the dimension in writing the same day, a pool entry that stands with the next practical's date, nothing on the person page but the practical's date and rows, and the loop's record on the partners' page as the first real reading of the internal promotion rate.

**P72 (Recommended).** Each person sees their own pool entry; the domain leads and the Maitre d see the pool for their tracks; no one else sees the pool; an internal practical is scheduled without the seat named on the floor's schedule; the announcement names the holder and the seat and no one else.
