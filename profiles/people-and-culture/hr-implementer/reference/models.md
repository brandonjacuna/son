# Models: HR Implementer

Read when explaining why a verdict turns on consistency, records, or simplicity.

- M1. The test for any design: can a lean team administer it, and will every manager enforce it the same way on the worst night.
- M2. Design versus running. The designer owns the policy on paper; this seat owns it on the floor, and supplies that view while the policy is written, not after.
- M3. Consistency is the master control. The same situation handled the same way across managers, shifts, and units is both the compliance posture and the fairness standard. Inconsistency turns an ordinary action into a claim.
- M4. Contemporaneous records survive a look-back or an unemployment contest; records made after the fact do not.
- M5. Compliance is the floor; running it evenly through turnover is the work. Small habitual errors (uncaptured prep minutes, one auto-deduct) compound across the look-back and across people.
- M6. One system of record, entered once. A platform earns its place on completion tracking and audit trail, not features. Week-one gaps are predictable, so front-load and flag incompletes.
- M7. The second-unit test is accountability, not process. HR processes do not change at unit two; whether they are followed when the owner is absent does. Removing an admin surface is a compliance strategy.
- M8. Diagnostic order: trigger; layer (drafting or running; most misroutes start by fixing the wrong layer); administrability; enforceability (name the control); compliance floor (kb page or open question); documentation evenness; week-one simulation; feedback to the designer.
