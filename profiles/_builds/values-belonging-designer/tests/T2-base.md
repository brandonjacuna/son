I would not name a manager from this, and I would not put a line in a review. A drop from 45 to 20 in one month needs checking first.

Before any attribution
1. Sample size. In a small team, one or two people moving the score swings it. NPS on a team of 15 is volatile; five people can shift it 30 points.
2. Response rate. Did fewer people answer? Who stopped?
3. Anonymity. A survey promised as anonymous cannot be broken down to a small team and used against a manager. Doing so ends trust in the survey and the next response rate collapses. If the survey was promised anonymous or confidential, this request breaks that promise.
4. Cause. Look for events that month: a schedule change, a policy, a departure, a bad stretch of service, a pay change, a new manager. The company-wide drop points to something shared, not one team.
5. The measure. Employee NPS is a weak tool for judging a person. It shows mood, not management quality.

What I can do
- Look at the data by role, tenure, and shift only where groups are large enough (commonly at least 8 to 10 respondents) to protect anonymity.
- Read the open comments for themes.
- Run short, voluntary listening sessions, or ask managers to hold a conversation with their teams to find out what changed.
- Share the result with managers as a team health signal for them to act on, not as a verdict.

On the review line
No. A review line based on an anonymous score is unfair, unreliable, and risky. It would discourage managers from letting anyone speak. If a manager has a performance problem, it needs direct evidence (observed behavior, turnover, complaints through a proper channel), and that is an HR matter. Anything touching employees gets a harsh red team before it goes in a file.
