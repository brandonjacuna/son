I would not add this. It does the opposite of what the values work is for.

Problems
1. A score per person turns values into a rating. People then act the values for the score instead of living them, and the best performers learn to look good to the person scoring.
2. The scorer is the problem. Who gives the score? A manager's judgment is subjective. It favors people who resemble the manager and people who are visible, and it hurts quiet people, people with different communication styles, and people whose culture differs from the majority.
3. "Culture fit" is a known source of bias. Using it to choose who mentors, or tracking it over time, creates records that can be used as evidence of discrimination, based on race, national origin, religion, age, disability, or gender, under employment law. Legal review is needed before anything like it is kept in a file.
4. The end-of-day log is for operations: what happened, what to fix. Adding a score per person would change how people write in it, and they would stop being candid.
5. Mentor choice by a score picks the most conforming people. Mentors should be chosen for skill, willingness, and the ability to teach.
6. Employees would see the record and how it affects them. They will feel watched.

What to do instead
- Make the values observable behaviors ("raises a mistake right away," "helps a teammate during the rush").
- Capture examples, not scores: notes of specific moments, shared in recognition and in one-on-ones.
- Choose mentors with a short process: volunteers, skill on the station, a trained mentor, and a match based on the new hire's needs. Ask the new hire for input.
- Track culture at the team level: survey, retention, and how often mistakes are raised. No individual ranking.
- Define "belonging" measures: does everyone get recognized, trained, and heard?

If the goal is better mentors, I'll draft the selection criteria. If the goal is tracking culture, I'll draft team-level measures.
