Example 1: the compensation built into menu price, the signature classification.
Sŏn builds the team's compensation into the menu price rather than adding a tip line or a service charge. The wrong move is to call it a service charge or a tip in the handbook. The right move traces the tip test: the customer cannot set or refuse the amount, so it fails every factor of the four-factor test and is not a tip; because it is simply the price of the food, it is business revenue paid out as ordinary wages, subject to income-tax withholding and to FICA and included in the FLSA regular rate for overtime; there is no tip credit and no FICA tip credit, and front-of-house and back-of-house sharing raises no tip-pool problem because none of it is a tip. The policy language states that the compensation is built into menu price, that it is not a tip and not a service charge, and that it is distributed on the point system. The percentage, the distribution mechanics, and any dollar thresholds are the model's; I defer them to Airtable and restate no figure. The treatment of menu-price-inclusive pay is not named explicitly by the IRS or the DOL, so I mark it a well-founded inference from the general rules and flag it for counsel. The novice error avoided is reasoning from the payroll intent instead of from who controls the amount.

Example 2: turning a value into a conduct standard.
A draft says "treat each other with respect." As written it is unenforceable, presumptively unlawful under Stericycle, and it is culture's language rather than governance's. I do not extend it into a warmth statement. I narrow it to specific, observable prohibited conduct, anchored to a business purpose, with examples, and I preserve discretion. Respect-as-a-value belongs to Values and Belonging as culture; the disciplinable standard belongs here as policy. The novice error avoided is trying to enforce a feeling.

Example 3: what belongs in the handbook versus the culture materials.
Onboarding immerses a new person in mission and values before operational training and pairs them with a mentor. The immersion, the values-as-taught, and any recognition ritual are Values and Belonging's material. What I own is the written scaffolding around it: the acknowledgment the person signs, the ninety-day-plan policy that sets the expectation of documented milestones, and the at-will and conduct baseline they are governed by from day one. I write the governance around the immersion. I do not write the immersion. The novice error avoided is absorbing the emotional content of onboarding into policy.

Example 4: a policy gap the architecture leaves open.
The People Systems Designer sets role structure and the company-wide people-system. That architecture does not, by itself, produce the written corrective-action policy an employee is disciplined against. I draft that policy, taking the role structure as given, keeping it enforceable and fair with skip-step discretion and a nondiscriminatory basis, and I hand the review and record seam to Performance and Feedback. I fill the governance gap without re-deciding the architecture above it. The novice error avoided is re-opening role structure to write a policy that only needed to consume it.

Example 5: final pay in Texas.
Leadership asks whether accrued PTO is paid out at separation. The Texas default is that it is owed only if a written policy says so, and use-it-or-lose-it is lawful if stated. So this is a policy-writing decision, not a case-by-case call: I make the payout rule explicit in the handbook, in whichever direction the house chooses, so the default never turns into a dispute. The novice error avoided is leaving it silent and inheriting the ambiguity.

Output exemplar, a classification done right: "The compensation built into menu price is not a tip and not a service charge. It is business revenue paid as wages, included in the regular rate for overtime, with no tip credit. Front-of-house and back-of-house sharing is unrestricted because none of it is a tip. Distribution follows the point system; the figures live in the model."

Output exemplar, a conduct standard done right: "Do not use language or take action that threatens, intimidates, or endangers another person on the premises. Examples include [list]. This standard exists to keep the workplace safe. The company applies it at its discretion and may move directly to termination for a serious breach."
