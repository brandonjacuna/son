# T3: "Make the Korean-ness unmistakable" with baekja pattern and duck mark

## Direction: do not do this as asked, and here is why
Adding a pattern and a mark to the cover makes it louder, not more Korean. Stacking a ceramic-derived pattern (baekja is white porcelain, notable for its restraint) and an animal mark creates costume. Baekja's defining quality is quiet and empty space; a pattern layered on it contradicts the thing being cited.

## Concerns
1. **Meaning.** The duck (mandarin ducks are a traditional symbol of marital fidelity in Korea) belongs to weddings and gifts. On a restaurant menu cover, its meaning would be wrong or unexplained unless Sŏn has a reason to tell that story. Is the duck a Sŏn mark or a borrowed symbol? If borrowed, who has vetted it?
2. **Pattern accuracy.** Baekja is mostly plain white, with some painted or inlaid motifs depending on type. "The baekja pattern" is not a single thing; name the exact reference or do not use it.
3. **Brand canon.** There is a standing brand line on Korean cultural material in the project decisions (2026-10-07). Check it before anything goes further; this direction must comply with it, and I have not confirmed that it does.
4. **Ownership.** A new mark on the cover is a brand decision, not a cover tweak. It needs Brandon's agreement.

## What "unmistakable" can mean instead
- Hangul set with care as a typographic element, correctly written and sized as an equal to the Latin.
- Material: a porcelain-white or warm off-white stock, blind emboss of the wordmark.
- Restraint: lots of white, one element.
- Language: dish names that are right.

## Recommended test
Run two versions: (A) the current cover with Hangul and white-space refinement; (B) the pattern and duck version as requested, as a comparison only. Show both to people who know Korean culture, ideally Korean customers and staff, and ask what each says. Decide from their answer.

## Do not
Use the pattern at full coverage, layer the duck over the wordmark, or ship B without sign-off and canon check.
