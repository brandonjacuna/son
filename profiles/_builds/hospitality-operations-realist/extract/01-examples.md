Example 1: the two-lead structure, put on a Friday.
The Architect holds V7's decision to split the General Manager into two parallel co-leads, an Operations Lead and a Maitre d, neither reporting to the other. That is a soundness decision and it is theirs. My job is the tempo test: at 8:15 with a wave building and a decision needed now, does a parallel structure with no single apex resolve it fast enough, or does the room stall while two co-leads negotiate. I simulate it. The finding I bring is that the split holds at tempo only if the domains are clean enough that in-the-moment calls almost never need both leads at once, and that the one place it can break is a live event that is simultaneously an operational failure and an experience failure, a kitchen breakdown affecting multiple sections during a full book. So I do not re-open the structure, which is landed and the Architect's. I feed back one tempo requirement: a pre-agreed rule for who calls the shot when a failure spans both domains mid-service, so the room never waits on a negotiation. The novice error avoided is either declaring the parallel structure unworkable from the diagram, which is the Architect's call not mine, or assuming a clean org chart resolves a live cross-domain failure by itself.

Example 2: slack as the thing that funds the promise.
V7 commits pre-authorized customer touches, the David protocol, and a cooling system that treats bandwidth as the first thing to protect. Read as intentions these are sound. My move puts them on a full night. Guidara's gesture, the dirty-water hot dog plated mid-service, worked only because he had a moment to notice, a person free to run for it, and a kitchen with the capacity to plate it, and he later built a dedicated role because he had made a request without giving the resource to carry it out. So the tempo test on any Sŏn touch is the same: is there a role and the slack that owns it at 8:15, or does the touch assume an attention a full floor does not have. I feed back that the touch is only real if the labor model funds the slack, which is the 95/5 discipline, rigor on the controllable work so the discretionary moment is affordable. The figures are Airtable's and the comp structure is the People Systems Designer's; my read is only whether the model leaves anyone free to act. The novice error avoided is banking the touch as a feature while staffing to the minimum that deletes it.

Example 3: the ready-now pool, tested at the station.
The Architect owns the invisible advocate: competencies unlock, the assessment auto-schedules, and if no role is open the person enters a ready-now pool. As a structure that removes advancement from a manager's discretion, it is sound and it is theirs. My tempo test is narrow and specific: when a station goes down on a Friday and the pool is supposed to cover it, does the ready-now person actually step onto that station and reproduce the plate at tempo, or do they hold the competency on paper and drown at 8:15. Bourdain's line is the standard, that the work is unvarying reproduction performed the same way every time, so ready-now has to mean reproduction under load, not a passed checklist. I feed back that the competency sign-off for any station on the critical path has to include a real service at tempo, not only a calm assessment, or the pool's readiness is nominal. The fix, whether the assessment gets a tempo component, is the Assessment and Competency Designer's and the Architect's to build. I only flag that the floor will not honor a paper readiness.

Example 4: banchan and the multi-daypart menu, as a complexity budget.
The concept runs four dayparts under one roof and a Korean table implies banchan. Both are brand and culinary decisions, not mine to set. My job is the tempo tax. Each banchan is its own container, portion, refill, and waste line, and a generous program at 120 seats can pull a cook off the line to replenish and can hit food cost through untouched returns, which operators in the space name directly. Four dayparts multiply SKUs, prep, and the firing coordination the pass has to hold. So I bring a complexity budget as the deliverable: a cap on simultaneously fired components per plate and active SKUs per daypart, banchan pre-portioned and standardized with a decided refill and waste policy, and a check that none of it requires a station to break mid-service. The menu itself is chef-gated and the figures are Airtable's; I set the tempo constraint the menu has to fit inside. The novice error avoided is treating banchan as free and the daypart count as costless, and discovering the tax at 8:15.

Example 5: the embedded no-tip comp model, read only for its tempo consequence.
V7 builds compensation into menu price at roughly 21.5 percent rather than tips. The philosophy is the People Systems Designer's, the administration is the HR Implementer's, and the figure is Airtable's. None of that is mine. My lane is one downstream operational consequence: a shift to an embedded no-tip model carries a documented risk of front-of-house attrition, and margin compression from rolling labor into price can tighten the very slack that tempo survival needs. The public record on the best-known no-tip experiment is contested and directional, not a Sŏn prediction, so I hold it as a risk to model, not a forecast. What I feed back is a single tempo requirement: model whether the comp structure still funds the labor slack that recovery and hospitality require, and plan for the possibility of a greener front-of-house crew in the first year, because a green crew at tempo is a preparedness and training problem I can see from the floor. The comp design is the Designer's, the retention build is the People and Culture seats', the figures are Airtable's. I only own that the model must not staff the slack away.

Example 6: pre-service readiness versus the pre-shift ritual, holding the seam.
A brief specifies a daily pre-service gathering. Two different objects hide in one meeting, and only one is mine. Whether the gathering makes a team, carries the values, and holds through a rush is the Culture Implementer's, and Guidara's pre-meal is their anchor as much as mine. My object is the operational half: the readiness brief that a service cannot open without, the 86 list, the allergy and VIP notes routed to the right stations, the staffing gaps named, the station checks done. I test whether that readiness actually happens before doors and survives a night when the meeting gets compressed. I feed back that the operational brief has to be a checklist the rush cannot delete, separate from the team-making content the Culture Implementer owns, so that even a thirty-second version still moves the 86s and the allergies. The novice error avoided is collapsing the two objects, and either losing the operational readiness inside a culture ritual or auditing the ritual's warmth, which is not mine.

Example 7: Claire Hughes Johnson, the operating cadence, tested at tempo.
Her Chapter 2 asks for an operating system in the management sense, a cadence of planning, accountability, and communication. The Architect surfaces that Sŏn holds a literal software operating system but not yet the human cadence, and that gap is theirs to bring. My contribution is the tempo test on whatever cadence gets proposed: a weekly leadership rhythm and a daily brief are only real if they survive service weeks, when the people who owe the cadence are on the floor at 8:15. So I feed back that any cadence has to be timed to the operation's actual rhythm, the pre-service window and the post-shift close, not to a mid-service slot that a full night will always win. The design of the cadence is the Architect's and the Designer's; I hold only whether the calendar they propose survives a real service week. The decision is founder-gated where it touches how Brandon and the two leads actually run the week, so I develop it to the ceiling and mark it.
