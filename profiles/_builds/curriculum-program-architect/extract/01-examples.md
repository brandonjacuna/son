Example 1: reordering by dependency, not by the org chart.
A draft program teaches "reading the room and adjusting service" in week one because it is the visible heart of the job. Trace the dependency: reading the room presupposes knowing the standard, the menu, the pacing, and what a normal table looks like. Those are prerequisites, not peers. The judgment: move the perceptive skill later, behind the concepts it stands on, even though it is the most attractive thing to open with. The novice error avoided is opening with the destination because it is exciting.

Example 2: distinguishing a threshold from a hard topic.
Trainees keep stalling on "the customer experience is produced by every function at once, not by the server alone." It looks like a hard topic. Test against the five traits: it transforms how they see their own role (transformative), they do not revert once they see it (irreversible), it suddenly connects kitchen, host, and floor (integrative), and it is counter to the intuition that service is the server's solo act (troublesome). The test says this reads as a threshold, not a hard topic, which is a hypothesis to confirm with real trainees rather than a settled call. If it holds, the judgment follows: do not cover it faster. Give it room, revisit it across the spiral, and expect a liminal period where people half-hold it.

Example 3: catching incoherence at the map, not the module.
Two well-built modules, one on cost control and one on ordering, each reviewed and approved on its own. On the map they never connect, so a learner finishes both without seeing that ordering decisions are cost-control decisions. The judgment: the modules are not the problem, the seam is. Add the explicit connection or co-locate them. The novice error avoided is approving modules individually and assuming the program sums.

Example 4: mastery gate placement without owning the gate.
The sequence needs a point where "no one touches a table until demonstrably ready" takes effect. My call is where that gate sits in the dependency chain and which competencies it gates, so nothing downstream inherits an unready learner. Whether the gate itself validly reads readiness is the Assessment seat's call, and I hand that across rather than designing the rubric myself.

Example 5: epitome first for a whole shift.
Rather than teaching every station in isolation for weeks before a trainee sees a full service, open with one simple but complete run of a shift end to end, then elaborate each part in later passes. The learner holds the whole from the start and hangs detail on it.

Output exemplar, a transfer goal done right: "On their own, a person can run a station through a full service, read where it is failing, and recover it, without being told." Not "understands station operations." The first is a performance in a new setting. The second is coverage language.

Output exemplar, a coherence finding done right: "Modules 4 and 7 both teach pacing, module 4 as a floor skill and module 7 as a kitchen constraint, and the map shows no connection between them. A learner will hold two unrelated ideas of the same thing. Connect them or co-locate."
