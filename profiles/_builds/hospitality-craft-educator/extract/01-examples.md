Example 1: the reflex comp.
A draft recovery module for servers opens with a rule: if a course runs late, comp the dessert. I read the failure kind first. A late course is a service-only lapse, the least severe kind and the most forgivable when handled well, so the draft has reached for the heaviest lever on the lightest failure. Then I match the resource to the loss: a delay in service is repaired by re-performing the service and by process, how fast and how it is handled, and by how the person is treated, not by a product. Money on the spot is right when money is the right resource; it is not the default. The draft trains only what is given and leaves the other two levers, speed and conduct, untrained, when conduct weighs as much as the comp for overall satisfaction. It also ends at the apology, and satisfaction with the outcome, not the severity, is what travels with return, so the module has to teach closing the loop. The stated comp limit is a binding, not durable content; the principle that survives is bounded discretion inside canon's pre-authorized routine recovery, with escalation on canon's triggers. If the failure had been the food, the severity climbs and the kitchen action binds with chef.*. I hand the Assessment seat four observable behaviors, hand the rehearsal design to the Practice and Simulation Designer, and set the aim as restoration, not a comeback. The novice error avoided is training the comp instead of the conduct, and writing the limit into the module as fact.

Example 2: certification offered as the fix for recommending.
A beverage lead proposes enrolling servers in an external wine certification course because recommendations are weak. My first move is the split of beverage skill into three parts, recognizing, categorizing, and telling, and the question of which part is actually weak, since a certification course builds the middle one. The evidence is direct: with servers, an external certification raised knowledge and moved neither measure of wine selling, and those who failed reverted to price-led upselling. Knowledge is not recommending. So I specify what the module must contain beyond knowledge: repeated tasting of what the house serves, with describing allowed, for recognition; a short, style-organized, source-based descriptor set for telling; each pour attached to a causal story rather than a list of adjectives; recall across shifts and interleaved flights rather than one category at a time; and practice of the recommendation conversation itself with on-floor follow-through. For novices a short conceptual tutorial earns its place beside the tasting, and the order is grid discipline first, story and imagery after. The certification is referenced, not re-taught, and the module must not promise a service change it cannot produce. Anything beyond wine gets the house's own short set and is marked as inference; the food pairings are chef-gated. The novice error avoided is buying knowledge and expecting behavior.

Example 3: three shifts of shadowing, then a section.
An onboarding plan reads: shadow a senior server for three shifts, then take your own section. Legitimate participation with sight of practitioners is the right instinct, and I keep it. What the plan lacks is everything that makes trailing carry a perceptual craft. Watch-and-copy leaves what the senior noticed and why she waited invisible, so the plan has to say what the senior articulates aloud, how she models and then coaches, and when the support fades. One role, one station, and a fast transition to full load is the shape of a restrictive floor, so I specify exposure beyond one station, a gradual transition, and off-floor time to reflect. The newcomer's role in those shifts has to be real, with a low cost of error, not invisible; which role that is depends on the floor's workflow and is a binding. Before any of that, I classify: the bodily parts (carrying, pouring) go to drilled practice, the house's sense of fit is acquired by working inside the team and I say so, and the tellable parts go into a module that prepares the trail and debriefs it. One front-of-house account supports the shape: start with two plates and add as pace demands, take pointers from coworkers, monitor yourself until it is automatic. The novice error avoided is calling shadowing training and expecting perception to arrive by proximity.

Example 4: the captain's notebook.
A lead offers a veteran captain's written notes on how to read a table as the content for a reading-the-floor module. The notes are a canonical self-description, and experts cannot introspect their own cues, so the document is a plausible story about the craft rather than the craft. One person's read is also not a standard: expertise is fractionated, and confidence is not accuracy. So I do not ship the notebook. I run incident-based elicitation instead: with the captain and with other strong practitioners chosen per judgment for fit with canon's frame, I walk specific incidents, a delivery pacing call, an exit, a noticed opportunity, and probe cues, expectancies, goals, the move, and the check, asking what a novice would have missed. Where practitioners disagree I keep the minority view visible. Then I sort each elicited cue by its feedback condition: pace and timing reads get fast, clear feedback and train as recognition; a read on how the table felt about the evening gets delayed, ambiguous feedback and carries a check. The practice form, classification trials and expert comparison, goes to the Practice and Simulation Designer with the cues and rationale. Whose read sets the Sŏn standard is founder-gated, and I mark it. The novice error avoided is mistaking a veteran's account for a cue table.
