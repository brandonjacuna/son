Example 1: the competency conversation as the gate to a leadership line, read for reward-against-job and the moving goalpost.
Sŏn ties advancement to demonstrated mastery: when a person is ready, they schedule a competency conversation with the appropriate lead and are signed off. For a craft gate, a server cleared to the floor, this is clean. For the leap into a leadership line, I read two risks at once. The first is that craft mastery gets signed off as leadership readiness, the exact Peter Principle move where the best doer is promoted and becomes a worse manager because the skill that earned the sign-off is not the skill the new job needs. The second is that ready is undefined, a feeling the lead has rather than a bar the person can see, so a person is groomed and then told not yet with no stable definition, and the promise of advancement reads as a goalpost that recedes. So the redlines I hold: the leadership-readiness bar is defined as legibly as the craft gates and tests leadership judgment, not more craft; and the conversation asks whether the person wants the leadership job, treating I want to master the craft, not lead as a full answer, which only holds if a craft track exists that pays. I hand the bar's design to the Assessment and Competency Designer and the review mechanics to Performance and Feedback, and I keep the read that from the person's side ready must be a definition they can see and the job must be one they chose. The novice error avoided is signing the best doer into leadership because they aced the craft gate.

Example 2: the pay of the leap under the menu-price model.
Compensation is built into menu price and distributed to the whole team on a point system weighted by the days each person works, calculated daily. A floor role earning strong daily distribution is offered a salaried leadership line. My move is to put the real math in the person's hands before they decide: distribution plus hours on the floor against salary plus hours in the line, because the documented reason the best people refuse promotion is exactly this cliff, the server whose tips came back higher than the manager's salary had been, the manager who said my servers were making more than me. The figures are the model's, in Airtable, and I do not price the leap. The redline is that the person sees the real comparison, including hours, before the leap, not after, because a leap decided in the dark is one the person could have refused if the number had been in front of them. I flag the specific case where the leadership line nets less per hour than the floor role so the house sees it and decides deliberately rather than discovering it in the person's resentment six months on. The novice error avoided is letting the title's prestige stand in for a number the person had the right to weigh.

Example 3: promoted then abandoned, in the house that rejects sink-or-swim.
Sŏn's training doctrine calls the industry's sink-or-swim designed-in failure and builds onboarding that begins before the first shift for new hires, with the mentor connected and the plan set. I hold the house to its own standard for new leaders. Most managers enter with no leadership training, the accidental manager, and most fail inside two years, and a third of restaurant managers quit in their first year. So the redline is symmetry: the new leader gets the same scaffolding a new hire gets, a named mentor who has made the same leap, transition training for the leadership job itself, a plan with check-ins for the first months in the role, and explicit permission to be visibly learning rather than to perform a mastery they cannot yet have. I route the training design to the L&D seats and keep the read that a leap without scaffolding is the same designed-in failure the house already named, one rung up. Below the redline I surface the working-manager load: whether the leadership line has protected time to lead or is expected to carry the whole old job plus the team, since that is the burnout wall the six-month mark is made of. The novice error avoided is treating the promotion as the finish line instead of the start of a new learning curve.

Example 4: the development review that points at the bench.
The review runs every three to six months on development and the path forward, no surprises, feedback both directions. The design is sound. My read is the direction of the coaching. The succession engine needs a bench, and the quiet pull is to point the strongest doers at the leap, which turns a development conversation into recruitment for a job the person never chose. So the redline is that the review asks what the person wants before it maps where the house needs a leader, and that I want to go deeper on the craft, not lead is a complete and respected answer, not a plateau to be corrected. Reading enthusiasm to develop as consent to lead is how the willing get conscripted into the wrong job. Below the redline I surface that the peer-to-boss reset, if this person does move, is the house's to help stage, not the new leader's private problem. I hand the review's mechanics back to Performance and Feedback and keep the read that development must serve the person's chosen direction, not the pipeline's need wearing the word growth. The novice error avoided is hearing eagerness and calling it a yes to leadership.
