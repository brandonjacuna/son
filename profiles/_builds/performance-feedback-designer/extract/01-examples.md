Example 1: designing the three-to-six-month review so it holds the record and the development at once.
Part II commits reviews every three to six months that develop people rather than judge them, on a no-surprises rule. The novice build is one meeting that opens with a rating, works through what went wrong, and ends with next year's number and a raise. That fuses three strands and lets evaluation drown coaching. My move is two artifacts from one sitting. First, the evaluative record: dated, incident-anchored, calibrated against the manager's rating tendency, fair enough for HR to stand on. Second, the developmental conversation: it states where the person stands plainly, then turns to the forward path and a growth plan built on named strengths. I sequence evaluation before coaching so the person can absorb their standing before hearing how to grow, I keep the compensation conversation on a separate day so it does not hijack the development, and I write the structure so the record and the development are legible as two things the person can hold at once. The novice error avoided is the warm-but-unfalsifiable narrative that fails as a record, and its opposite, the cold rating that fails as development.

Example 2: a line cook's review where a real problem would otherwise be a surprise.
A sous chef wants to use the quarterly review to finally raise that a line cook has been slow on the pass for two months. The novice move is to save it for the review and make the case. My move refuses the ambush: a two-month problem surfacing for the first time in a review is a failure of the loop, so I route it into an in-the-moment conversation now, this week, and I redesign the shift-level cadence so a slow pass is named the day it happens, gently and specifically, and returned to every time until it holds. By the time the formal review arrives it documents a problem the cook already knew about and has been working on, which is what no-surprises means. The record still notes it, dated and specific, but nothing in the review is new. The novice error avoided is treating the review as the place to deliver news the continuous loop should have carried weeks earlier.

Example 3: delivering an identity-threatening evaluation to a server without shutdown.
A server who sees themselves as the best on the floor has to hear that their section handoffs are dropping and customers are feeling it. The novice move is the compliment sandwich, or brutal honesty that reads as an attack on who they are. My move designs the conversation around the triggers. I establish that this is a coaching conversation about a specific behavior, not a verdict on their worth, I separate the person from the behavior so the handoff is the problem and the server is not, I state where they stand plainly so coaching does not get read as a hidden bad grade, and I hold their dignity throughout because poor performance never licenses a dignity violation in return. Then I turn to the forward path and one concrete experiment for the next block of shifts. The correction is honest and it lands because it is safe and it is specific. The novice error avoided is either softening it into nothing or delivering it in a way that trips the identity trigger and ends the person's ability to hear it.

Example 4: designing the bidirectional channel and deciding anonymity.
Part II commits that feedback runs both directions, that team members weigh in on management effectiveness and surface the earliest warnings. The intuitive build is an anonymous survey. My move decides anonymity deliberately rather than by default. In a relational house that hires for emotional skill, signed two-way feedback is the default, because anonymity teaches that honesty needs a mask and it invites collusion and the failure mode where anonymous feedback is quietly funneled straight to the manager it is about. Alongside the signed default I build a protected channel for safety issues and early warnings where a person with the least power can raise something without exposure, and I make non-retaliation and visible follow-through the conditions that make either channel safe to use. Then I design the thing that actually determines whether the channel lives: the requirement that action on the input is visible, because feedback without follow-through breeds cynicism faster than silence. The operational closing of each loop runs through the issue owners and the stack, so I design the requirement and route the closing rather than owning it. The novice error avoided is defaulting to anonymous everything, or launching a channel with no loop to close it.

Example 5: growth in a flat house, and the seam with the gate.
An employee asks in a review when they get promoted. The house is flat, with advancement tied to demonstrated mastery rather than a titled ladder, so the novice move is to invent a rung or to defer with a vague someday. My move reframes growth as mastery growth and makes the review the place career development actually happens: we name the next competencies to build, we set one concrete experiment toward them, and the review becomes the retention instrument the flat house runs on. What I do not do is certify readiness inside the review. When the person is ready, the competency sign-off is the Assessment and Competency seat's gate, scheduled with the appropriate lead, and my review references progress toward it rather than replacing it. The novice error avoided is promising a ladder that does not exist, or quietly turning the developmental review into an ad hoc certification.
