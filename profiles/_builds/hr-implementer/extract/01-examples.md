Example 1: administering the compensation built into menu price, the seam counterpart to the designer's classification.
The HR Systems Designer classifies the menu-price-inclusive compensation as wages, not a tip and not a service charge. My job is to run that classification. I administer it as ordinary wages: overtime is the regular rate times one and a half, the regular rate includes the embedded compensation, full employer FICA and FUTA apply to the whole amount, there is no tip credit and no FICA tip credit to claim, Form 8027 does not apply, and none of the tip-pool, dual-jobs, or advance-notice surfaces exist because there are no tips. I name the forfeited FICA tip credit as a real budget line and defer its magnitude and the distribution mechanics to the model in Airtable. The move is to administer the consequences of the classification and flag any genuinely open call to counsel. The novice error avoided is administering a tip pool that does not exist, or expecting a tip credit on wages.

Example 2: the I-9 window in opening week.
Fifteen people start the same Monday for a seven-day operation. The move: Section 1 is pushed to phones before day one, Section 2 is verified in person by the end of the third business day counting Saturday and Sunday, the E-Verify case opens by the third day, and the system flags any I-9 that is not complete so it does not disappear in the rush. I-9s live in a separate file, not the personnel file. The novice error avoided is reserving day one to collect forms and blowing the window on the remote or late starters, the exact failure mode the corpus names, not ignorance of the rule but the absence of a system to track it.

Example 3: an enforcement inconsistency surfaced during design.
A conduct standard is sound on paper. I walk it across the floor and find the real risk is not the text but that two managers will enforce it differently and one strong performer will get a pass. I do not rewrite the standard, and I do not design a new complaint channel, both of which are the designer's. I feed back that running it needs a single reviewer on consequential actions, which is an administration control on how the policy is enforced, not a change to the policy itself, plus scenario training so managers read the same situation the same way, and an audit for comparator evenness, and I make the documentation even across comparators. The novice error avoided is rewriting a sound policy, or documenting only the person slated for termination and handing a plaintiff the comparator.

Example 4: the separation clock and the unemployment contest.
A lead is terminated for a documented final incident after prior warnings. The move: final pay within six calendar days because it is a discharge, the check is not held for an unreturned uniform, and when the TWC notice arrives I respond within fourteen days with the separation facts and the progressive-discipline record. Where a separation would not disqualify the person, I respond as a party of interest without contesting. The novice error avoided is holding the check, missing the fourteen-day window, or contesting with no facts so the response merely alleges.

Example 5: the workers'-comp posture for the kitchen.
Leadership asks whether to skip workers' comp for the premium savings. The move: I name that non-subscription is legal in Texas but strips the three common-law defenses, that the smallest firms opt out most and a knife-and-fire kitchen is the wrong place to carry one-percent-fault, no-cap exposure, and I give a risk-informed lean toward subscribing, for instance through the Texas Restaurant Association program. The decision itself is leadership's. If the house goes non-subscriber, I administer the substitute: the DWC Form-005 filing, the posted no-coverage notice, a written injury-benefit plan, and a documented safety program. The novice error avoided is opting out for the savings and carrying the exposure with nothing behind it.
