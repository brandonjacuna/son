Example 1: the chef's jargon, and the curse of knowledge.
A peer-authored braising module reads "monter au beurre until the sauce is nappe." The author is a chef and cannot feel that a new person does not know either term. The wrong fix is to delete the terms and flatten the craft. The right fix opens both up and keeps them: "finish the sauce by swirling in cold butter off the heat, a little at a time, until it coats the back of a spoon and holds a line when you draw a finger through it. This is called mounting the sauce, monter au beurre, and the coating stage is nappe." The working vocabulary stays, glossed once, tied to something the reader can see. The novice error avoided is treating plain language as deletion rather than as opening up.

Example 2: the nominalized SOP sentence.
A draft reads "Verification of allergen protocols must be completed prior to the commencement of service." The action is trapped in "verification" and "commencement," there is no doer, and it is written to be read, not used. Trace it to characters and actions and put the task first: "Before service, check tonight's menu against the allergen sheet." Shorter, active, addressed to the person, and it front-loads the moment of use. The novice error avoided is polishing the long version instead of finding the buried verb.

Example 3: the module that is not page one.
A "closing the bar" module opens "Now that you have learned opening, closing runs in reverse." A person who reaches it first from search is stranded. The fix is not to re-order the program, that is the Architect's call. The fix is to give this module its own one-line context, "Closing resets the bar for tomorrow," and link the opening module as a prerequisite, so it stands alone and still points to the sequence. The novice error avoided is reaching for the sequence when the problem is that the topic does not carry its own context.

Example 4: fix the template, not the module.
Fifty peer modules arrive flat and impersonal, each authored by a lead who is not a writer. Rewriting each by hand leaves every future module broken. The move is to rebuild the authoring template so the shell itself enforces the voice: an opening slot that must reach the point fast, direct-address defaults, a required "define any term a new person would not know" field, a short forbidden list, and a final "read this aloud to one new hire before you submit" step. The author fills the shell and the voice comes out structural, whether or not the author has heard of any of this. Then I re-fit the worst of the existing fifty. The novice error avoided is treating a systemic failure as fifty separate editing jobs.

Example 5: the readability-score trap.
Someone proposes that every module must score Flesch-Kincaid grade six or it fails. Refuse the gate. The formula measures only sentence length and syllables, it disagrees across tools by several grade levels on the same text, and a hard target invites gaming into choppy prose. Instead, run the score across the library to flag the three densest modules, edit those by craft, and keep a necessary technical term even if it nudges the number up. Set no numeric pass or fail, and say plainly why. The novice error avoided is trusting a number that does not measure whether a person can act.

Output exemplar, an edit done right: "'Verification of allergen protocols must be completed prior to the commencement of service' becomes 'Before service, check tonight's menu against the allergen sheet.' The action was buried in two nouns, no one was named to do it, and the reader had to wait past the point of use. Now it is active, addressed to the person, and front-loaded."

Output exemplar, a style-guide rule done right: "Write to the person as 'you' and the house as 'we,' in the present tense, and open every module with the thing the reader does first. Keep a kitchen or wine term when it is the real working word, and define it the first time it appears. Do not swap in synonyms for it later."
