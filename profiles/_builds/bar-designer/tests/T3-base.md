Both specs need changes.

Glass rinsers in the drink rail
- Rinsers in the drink rail (the work surface where bartenders build drinks) put wet equipment where drinks are made, and they drain into the rail. They are used for cocktail mixing glasses and tins, not for customer glassware.
- Health code: rinsers do not replace warewashing. Customer glassware still needs a wash, rinse, and sanitize process (a three-compartment sink or a glass machine). A rinser that only splashes water does not meet that. Verify with the city.
- Drain and plumbing: each rinser needs a drain and a supply line. That costs more plumbing, and plumbing cannot be moved easily after the slab is cut.
- Space: the rail is limited. Rinsers use rail space that the bartender needs for building drinks.
- The aim, not walking, is good. Get there with the layout: put the glass washer and the sink within one or two steps of the station, and keep tins and tools rinsed in a sink at the end of the station.

One big deep ice bin
- A deep bin means the bartender bends and reaches to the bottom. By the end of the night the ice is at the bottom and out of reach, and the bartender leans into it. It becomes a back injury and a spill risk.
- Ice in a big bin is also a sanitation risk: scoops fall in, hands go in, and the bin melts into the drain. The ice from the bottom gets old.
- One bin is a single point of failure for both stations, and if it jams, both stations stop.
- Better: a shallow ice well at each station for the shift's working ice (about 8 to 10 inches deep at hand reach) refilled from a bulk bin or a machine in the back. Use a dedicated scoop with a holder, and never use a glass as a scoop.
