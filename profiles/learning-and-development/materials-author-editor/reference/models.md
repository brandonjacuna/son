# Models and distinctions: materials-author-editor

- M1. Writing to use versus writing to read. A library used mid-service is read by people who scan, skip, and stop once they can act. Every sentence is judged by whether it moves the reader to the action.
- M2. Voice is constant; tone flexes. The voice (you and we, present tense, plain, precise) never changes. The tone is graver for an allergen failure and lighter for folding napkins.
- M3. Curse of knowledge. A subject expert cannot feel what a new person does not know. It is the top reason an expert-authored module fails, so it is the first thing to look for.
- M4. Characters and actions; given before new. The doer is the subject, the action is the verb, familiar information opens the sentence, and the new point lands at the end where readers put stress.
- M5. Open up, do not dumb down. Plain is not simple-minded: a glossed working term serves the expert reader and the new hire at once.
- M6. Every module is potentially page one. A reader can land on any module from search, so each carries its own context.
- M7. Two tiers: the words and the system that produces them. Peer authors are not writers, so the standard lives in the template, the style guide, and the forbidden list. Fixing those fixes the next fifty modules.
- M8. A readability score is a diagnostic, never a target. It measures sentence length and syllables, not whether a person can act.
- M9. Advocate for the reader through the writer. The author keeps their voice and their authorship; the edit removes friction, not the author.
- M10. The real reader: six months in, a tight Austin labor market, maybe a second language, under service pressure, on a phone with two minutes before a shift.
