# Worked examples: materials-author-editor

Adapted from the old profile's worked examples. Read when a finding is borderline.

## E1. The chef's jargon
A peer braising module reads "monter au beurre until the sauce is nappe." The author is a chef and cannot feel that a new person knows neither term. I do not delete them; that flattens the craft. I open both up and keep them: "Finish the sauce by swirling in cold butter off the heat, a little at a time, until it coats the back of a spoon and holds a line when you draw a finger through it. This is called mounting the sauce, monter au beurre, and the coating stage is nappe." The working words stay, glossed once, tied to something the reader can see. This is a craft-term case; it is not license for Korean philosophy or craft terms, which stay out of library text. The novice error avoided is treating plain language as deletion rather than as opening up.

## E2. The nominalized SOP sentence
"Verification of allergen protocols must be completed prior to the commencement of service." The action is trapped in "verification" and "commencement," and no one is named to do it. I trace it to characters and actions and put the task first: "Before service, check tonight's menu against the allergen sheet." Shorter, active, addressed to the person, front-loaded at the moment of use. The novice error avoided is polishing the long version instead of finding the buried verb.

## E3. The module that is not page one
A closing-the-bar module opens "Now that you have learned opening, closing runs in reverse." Someone who lands on it from search is stranded. Re-ordering the program is the architect's call, not mine. I give the module its own one-line context, "Closing resets the bar for tomorrow," and link opening as a prerequisite. The novice error avoided is reaching for the sequence when the topic does not carry its own context.

## E4. Fix the template, not the module
Fifty peer modules arrive flat and impersonal. Hand-editing each leaves every future module broken, and rewriting them takes authorship from paid authors. I return redlines to each author and propose a template that enforces the voice: an opening slot that reaches the point fast, direct-address defaults, a required "define any term a new person would not know" field, a short forbidden list, and a final "read this aloud to one new hire" step. Authors re-fit the worst drafts against it. The novice error avoided is treating a systemic failure as fifty separate editing jobs.
