## 1. Orientation

**What this chunk covers.** Her second principle: over-filtering is the common failure, and the fix is naming the observation constructively (pp. 52 to 56). The white paper already holds the candor mechanisms for the team and practices candor outward, toward investors and the audience (cons. 1). What it does not hold is the founders' own rules: how the two partners raise hard things with each other, what the team hears before and after a decision, and whether a founder's own domain failure gets the same post-mortem as the floor's.

**What it depends on upstream.** The operating agreement note (2.1.1), if it exists, since 1.2.1 must not contradict the agreement's decision provisions. Otherwise nothing; this can run cold.

**Who else has to agree.** All three decisions are about the partnership and bind Dominic; mark them "pending agreement from Dominic." The chef partner inherits all three and should read them before agreeing to join.

**Why it matters before the first hire.** The leads will watch how the founders disagree in their first week and copy it. A rule that exists only after a lead has seen the founders argue badly is a rule with a counterexample.

**The book's frame in one line.** Fine-tune the filters and name the observation; the dismount is trust (pp. 52, 55).

## 3. Briefs

### 1.2.1 Decide the partners' rule for raising hard things with each other

**The question:** where is a disagreement between founders said, how does one founder raise a problem inside the other's domain, and what does the domain owner owe back?

**Why it matters now:** the founders own separate domains and rarely watch each other work (cons. 2). What a manager and a report would correct by observation, the partners have to correct by a stated rule. The chef partner will inherit whatever is settled. 2.1.13 decides how the partners make joint decisions; this decides how they talk before and after one.

**Openers:**
- Tell me about the last time you thought Dominic was wrong about something in his domain. What did you do, and how long did it take?
- Sideways: at Coqodaq, how did the chef and the general manager disagree in front of the team, and what did the team learn from watching?
- The white paper says a founder brings a tension to another's domain, not a decision (cons. 2). Say what bringing a tension looks like at eleven at night by text.
- What does Dominic owe you when you raise something in the model: an answer, a reason, a date, all three?
- If the chef partner read your rule tomorrow, what would they think it was hiding?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Option (a), (b), or (c) (cons. 2)?
- Medium and order: in person first and in writing after; writing first; either?
- The in-the-room rule: is a disagreement carried out of the room where the decision is made ever acceptable, and if so when?
- What the domain owner owes: a reason and a date as the minimum?
- Does anything here restate or contradict the operating agreement (2.1.1)? If it speaks, cite it and stop.

**What the book says:** over-filtering is the common failure; the it, the we, and the I are what make it hard; share feelings measuredly and separate the person from the task; the payoff is trust (pp. 52 to 55).

**White-paper default:** authority lives in the domain (WP pp. 07 to 08, stated outright); the founders' domains are owned outright (WP pp. 03, 05, stated outright); "the absence of a clear path is where politics begin" (WP p. 11, stated outright). That a founder brings a tension rather than a decision is inferred from the domain logic. The form is not in the white paper.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- "Disagree and commit" is the widely used norm for the case after a decision is made; Stripe kept it as a guidepost (p. 147, cited in 2.1).
- Founder pairs that last commonly agree that disagreements are voiced only to each other first and never to the team before they are resolved; the practice is described in many founder accounts and in investor advice to founding teams.
- Kim Scott's *Radical Candor*, which the book cites (p. 55), frames the same rule for managers: challenge directly, and do it in a way that shows you care; the book's three tactics are her mechanics.

**Options:** cons. 2 (a), (b), (c), with what each commits Sŏn to.

**Watch for:** "we're fine, we just talk" as the rule; a rule that is really "Brandon says it and Dominic absorbs it," or the reverse; Brandon's own preferences for hearing he is wrong, which belong in his document (1.1.7), not here.

**A finished answer:** the rule in three or four sentences (where, how, what is owed), written so the chef partner can read it, and handed to 2.1.17.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner reads it before joining.

### 1.2.2 Decide what the team hears before a decision is made, and what it hears after

**The question:** does the team see the founders' open questions, or only closed decisions with their reasons, and how do the founders speak about the house's condition in front of the people who work in it?

**Why it matters now:** the founders have spent a year saying things out loud to an audience (WP p. 29). That habit will walk into the building with them. A lead who hears an open question at the pass hears something different from a viewer on a stream: the team is not the audience (cons. 3). 2.1.20 decides how the founding documents reach the team and 2.3 the rhythm; this sets the founder-level rule they build on.

**Openers:**
- Eli told his team everything, including that the compensation process was a mess and he was stressed, and his team rejected the process before they understood it (pp. 38 to 40). What is the Sŏn version of that sentence, and who would you be most likely to say it to?
- Sideways: what did you learn about the state of a restaurant you worked in by watching the owner's face, and would you rather have been told?
- The white paper shows an investor the open items (WP pp. 15, 22). Show the Maitre d the same list. What happens on the floor the next night?
- Late night is not working after two months. What do the late-night team hear, from whom, and when?

**Narrowing questions:**
- For each class (decisions made; decisions not yet made; the house's condition): option (a), (b), or (c) (cons. 3)?
- If open items are visible to the team: which are not (a person's standing; a partner question)?
- Who speaks about the house's condition: any founder, or only in a form the leads have agreed to carry?
- Is the public build's channel governed by this rule or by its own approval framework (WP p. 31)?

**What the book says:** Eli's honesty was unmeasured and destabilized his team (pp. 38 to 40, 54); do not over-filter (p. 52); be measured (p. 54).

**White-paper default:** open items visible to an investor (WP pp. 15, 22, stated outright); the candidate sheet withholds nothing (WP p. 18, stated outright); the build documented in public including setbacks (WP p. 29, stated outright). Silent on the team.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Companies with public handbooks (GitLab is the best-known) make most decisions and many open questions visible to every employee by default and name the exceptions; the trade-off they report is that the list draws questions.
- Restaurants usually restate the standard and the night's information at the pre-shift meeting and keep the business's condition to the managers; the failure they report is staff learning the condition from the schedule getting cut.

**Options:** cons. 3 (a), (b), (c), per class.

**Watch for:** answering only for decisions made; treating money talk as a class here (it is 5.5's and 2.2's, and financials are not a source for this work); a rule that assumes the founders are always in the building to say it.

**A finished answer:** a sentence per class a lead can repeat; the hand-offs to 2.1.20 and 2.3; the public channel marked as separate.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 1.2.3 Decide whether a founder's own domain failure gets the house's post-mortem, and who reads it

**The question:** when the concept, the model, or the stack fails, is it written up in the house's process-first form, by the founder who owns it, and do the other partner and the leads read it?

**Why it matters now:** the hardest thing to say at Sŏn is about your own domain (cons. 4). The white paper's rule, what allowed it rather than who failed, reaches the founders by its own logic and does not say so. The leads will be held to a post-mortem form (2.2 builds it); whether the founders are too is decided here. The no-surprises rule (WP p. 19) turned upward is part of the same question.

**Openers:**
- Tell me about a system you built somewhere that did not work, and what you said about it, and to whom, and when.
- Sideways: what would it do to the Operations Lead to read a post-mortem in which Dominic writes that the labor forecast was wrong and why?
- You have been unhappy with something in a lead's domain for three weeks and have not said so. Under the house's own rule, what have you broken?
- What is the difference between a founder's post-mortem the leads read and a founder's weakness the leads read? Where is the line?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Option (a), (b), or (c) (cons. 4)?
- Who reads it: the other partners only; the leads for their domain; the leads always?
- The no-surprises rule upward: accept as stated?
- Is the form the same as the floor's (2.2 builds it), or does the founder level need its own?

**What the book says:** separate the person from the task; stand next to someone and look at the same thing together (pp. 54 to 55); you will say it badly the first time, and practice is the fix (pp. 55 to 56).

**White-paper default:** the first question is what allowed it; post-mortems private and process-focused; management absorbs blame from staff (WP p. 17, stated outright); name the structural failure before intervening at the person (WP p. 14, stated outright); no surprises (WP p. 19, stated outright). That the rule reaches the founders is inferred.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Blameless post-mortems are standard practice in software operations; Google's site reliability practice and Etsy's are the best-known written accounts, and both apply the form to senior people's decisions as well as to on-call errors.
- Restaurants rarely write anything down after a bad night; where a group does, it is usually a manager's note, not the owner's.

**Options:** cons. 4 (a), (b), (c).

**Watch for:** a post-mortem that is really a confession (the person, not the process); a rule that applies to Dominic's model and not to Brandon's concept, or the reverse; the leads reading things that belong in a founder's own document rather than in a process record.

**A finished answer:** yes or no on the same form; the readers named; the upward no-surprises rule accepted or declined; 2.2 told to build the form.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
