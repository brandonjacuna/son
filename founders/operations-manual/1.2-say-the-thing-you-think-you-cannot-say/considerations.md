# 1.2 Say the thing you think you cannot say: considerations

What is worth knowing before doing the work in `tasks.md`. Topics follow the book's order. "WP p. N" is the white paper (`extraction/s01/record.md`). Nothing here is a decision; where the old synthesis argued a position it appears as an option with its reasoning and what it commits Sŏn to.

## 1. What the house already says out loud, and to whom

### What the white paper holds

The white paper practices candor on itself, outward. It keeps its open items visible: "Naming these keeps the account honest: the foundation is built, and a handful of pieces are still being decided rather than presented as settled" (WP p. 15). It names the open stack decisions "because a settled-looking answer to an open question is the tell of a deck that has not been pressure-tested" (WP p. 22). It refuses to name future brands because "the discipline is to never assert the unproven, to a customer or to a reader" (WP pp. 36, 38). It documents the build in public, "the real problems, the real decisions," and says setbacks are the strongest content (WP p. 29). Crisis communication is "transparency first" (WP p. 31). Recovery at the table is "be direct about what happened, acknowledge the real impact" (WP p. 26).

Inside the house, the white paper holds several candor mechanisms for the team: the no-surprises rule, "a formal review never contains new information" (WP p. 19); feedback that "runs both directions," including on management effectiveness (WP p. 19); a structured feedback channel where "the loop gets closed" (WP p. 22); post-mortems that are "private and process-focused," where "the first question is not who failed. It is what allowed this to happen" and "management absorbs blame from staff" (WP p. 17); and the candidate sheet with "nothing withheld" (WP p. 18).

What the white paper does not hold: how the two founders tell each other hard things; how a founder raises a problem inside the other founder's domain; what a founder says in front of the team and what they hold back; and whether a founder's own domain failure gets the same post-mortem the floor's does.

### Considerations

**The standard failure and the Sŏn failure are different.** Her failure is the manager who filters so heavily the real issue is never said. The likelier failure at Sŏn is subtler: a founder so fluent that an open question sounds settled. The white paper's own discipline against that is aimed outward; the same discipline turned inward means "I have not decided" is a complete answer to a lead, and a disagreement between founders is said in the room where the decision is made, not carried out of it. Fluency is the risk this principle guards against here.

**Her three tactics already have Sŏn forms.** Sharing a feeling measuredly is what the recovery standard asks of a server (WP p. 26). Separating the person from the task is the process post-mortem (WP p. 17). Standing next to someone and looking at the same thing is what the shift close is for (WP p. 13). The house has the tactics for the team. It has not written them for the founders.

## 2. How the partners raise hard things with each other

### What the white paper holds

Authority "lives in the domain. The person who owns the domain is accountable to its outcomes" (WP pp. 07 to 08). Domain ownership is "small, specific, explicitly defined, interlocking" (WP p. 09). The founders own separate domains outright (WP pp. 03, 05). One joint decision is stated (lunch, WP p. 36). Nothing on how a founder disagrees with the other inside the other's domain.

### Considerations

**Separate domains mean the founders rarely watch each other work.** A manager and a report see each other daily and correct by observation. One founder is in the room and on camera, the other in the model and the build; the chef partner will be in the kitchen. What observation would supply, a stated rule has to supply instead. The working-with-me document (1.1) asks each founder how they want to hear that they are wrong (prompt E in the old spine); this chunk decides the rule between them that those individual preferences sit under.

**The old synthesis argued one form.** A founder does not decide inside another founder's domain; they bring a tension to it. The concept founder does not choose the point-of-sale spine; the capital founder does not set the emotional standard of the room; neither writes the menu. That form follows from the white paper's domain logic (WP pp. 07 to 09). What it does not settle is how the tension is brought: in writing or in person; before or after the domain owner has acted; with what the domain owner owes in return (an answer, a reason, a date).

**The chef partner will join a rule already in use.** Whatever the two founders settle now is what the third partner inherits. Writing it down is how they see it before they agree to it (as with the seat descriptions, 2.1.11).

**Options.** (a) A written rule: a founder raises a problem in another's domain as a tension, in person first and in writing after, and the domain owner owes a reason and a date; disagreement is said where the decision is made and not carried out of the room; the rule sits in the founding document beside how the partners decide (2.1.13). Commits the founders to a form they will sometimes find slow and to writing down things they would rather say once. (b) A stated norm, unwritten: the founders agree the form out loud and rely on the working-with-me exchange (1.1) to carry each person's preferences. Commits Sŏn to the chef partner learning it by asking. (c) No rule; the operating agreement's decision provisions (2.1.1) are the only structure. Commits the founders to settling disagreements by whoever is louder or more present, which the white paper calls politics (WP p. 11).

## 3. What the team hears, before and after a decision

### What the white paper holds

Onboarding orients every person to "the mission, the values, and the behavioral standard" before mechanics (WP p. 18). The candidate sheet withholds nothing (WP p. 18). The feedback channel closes the loop (WP p. 22). Open items are visible to an investor (WP pp. 15, 22) and the build's decisions are visible to an audience (WP p. 29). Nothing on whether the team hears about a founder-level decision before it is made, or how the founders talk about the house's condition in front of the people who work in it.

### Considerations

**Eli's problem is the founders' problem.** Her story of the manager whose value was transparency and who told his team everything, including "it's a mess and I'm totally stressed," is the book's clearest case of honesty done unmeasured (pp. 38 to 40, 54). A founder who documents the build in public for a year has a strong habit of saying things out loud. That habit is an asset toward an audience (WP p. 29) and a hazard toward a team: a lead who hears "we don't know if late night will work" from a founder at the pass hears something different from what a viewer hears on a stream. The team is not the audience.

**Three classes worth separating.** Decisions made (the team hears them, in a form and on a rhythm; 2.1.20 and 2.3). Decisions not yet made (does the team hear that a question is open, as an investor does, or only once it is closed?). The house's condition (how well it is doing, what is worrying the founders, what a bad month means for anyone's job). The white paper is silent on the second and third. Financial figures are not a source for this work and are not a class here; whether the founders speak about money in front of the team at all is a question for 5.5 and 2.2, not this chunk.

**Options.** (a) The team sees open items as the investor does: what is decided, what is open, who closes it, on the internal home. Commits the founders to a visible list and to living with questions from the team about it. (b) Decided, then told: open questions stay between the partners until closed; the team hears decisions with their reasons. Commits the founders to holding things that may leak anyway, given the public build. (c) By class: open items visible; the house's condition spoken by a founder only in the form the leads have agreed to carry. Commits Sŏn to a rule the leads help write (2.1.20).

## 4. A founder's own domain failure

### What the white paper holds

"When something goes wrong, the first question is not who failed. It is what allowed this to happen." Post-mortems are "private and process-focused." "Management absorbs blame from staff" (WP p. 17). The failure-mode reference exists so that "before intervening at the people level," the structural failure is named (WP p. 14). The founders are the first domain owners (WP pp. 03, 05).

### Considerations

**The hardest thing to say at Sŏn is about your own domain.** Her principle is mostly about saying a hard thing to a report. For a founder who owns the concept, or the model, or the stack, the hard thing is that the concept was wrong, the model missed, the stack failed, in writing, in a post-mortem the other founder reads. The white paper's rule reaches the founders by its own logic (universal problems are design problems, WP p. 17), but nothing says it does, and nothing says who reads a founder's post-mortem or whether the leads see it.

**The no-surprises rule applies upward.** "A formal review never contains new information" (WP p. 19). Turned toward the founders, a lead should never learn in a review that a founder has been unhappy with a system for a month. A founder who holds a concern about a lead's domain for a month has broken the rule the house holds the leads to.

**Options.** (a) A founder's domain failure gets the same process post-mortem as a floor failure, written by the founder, read by the other partners, with the leads seeing the process change and not the post-mortem. Commits the founders to writing about their own mistakes in the house's form. (b) The same, and the leads read it, on the reasoning that a house built to run without its founders should see how a founder's process fails. Commits the founders to a visibility most founders would not choose. (c) No rule; the founders handle their own failures between themselves. Commits Sŏn to the white paper's rule applying to everyone but the two people who wrote it.

## 5. Where Sŏn may diverge from the book

Her principle is a manager's discipline toward reports. At Sŏn it runs in three directions the book does not address: between equal partners with separate domains; from a founder to a team that is not the founder's audience; and from a founder about their own domain. The three tactics transfer whole. The settings do not.
