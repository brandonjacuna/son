# 1.1 Build self-awareness to build mutual awareness: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 1, pp. 35 to 51, with related self-awareness exercises at pp. 63 to 68 (workbook). Full text: `sources/extraction/build/s01/book.md`, `sources/extraction/build/s01/exercises.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## Where this sits

Chapter 1 lays out four operating principles Johnson returns to throughout the book: build self-awareness to build mutual awareness, say the thing you think you cannot say, distinguish between management and leadership, and come back to your operating system (p. 36). She frames all four as being about one thing, building trust: if you are not self-aware, self-disclosing, clear about what you are managing toward, or consistent, the people around you cannot trust you (p. 36). This section covers the first and, in her view, the most important, because it underpins the other three (p. 37).

## The core claim

Self-awareness is the key to great management. Many people think management is about other people; she argues it starts with understanding what you are good at and what you need to work on. You cannot build an effective, complementary team if you do not know your own strengths and gaps (p. 37).

Self-awareness has three components (pp. 37 to 38):

1. Understanding your underlying value system
2. Identifying your innate preferences, your work style and decision-making tendencies
3. Being clear about your own skills and capability gaps

Building your own self-awareness is what makes mutual awareness possible: once you can name your values and tendencies, you can talk about them with your team, and they can do the same, which is what lets a group of individuals function as a team (pp. 39 to 40).

## Understand your values

Her illustrated case is a manager, "Eli," who was well liked and delivered results but over-shared sensitive information with his team, creating anxiety around changes like a compensation redesign. Direct feedback did not change his behavior. At a values workshop, Eli told the story of learning as a child that his mother had died, only after being kept in the dark about her illness; his value, he realized, was transparency, formed by that experience (pp. 38 to 39).

Two lessons she draws from it (p. 39):

- You never know someone else's story. Difficult behavior usually has a deeper reason worth understanding.
- People do not always know why they act the way they do. Without seeing the belief driving a behavior, they cannot adapt it.

Once Eli named his value, he and Johnson could negotiate, at a meta level, what was appropriate to share and when. She calls this technique "going meta": naming your value directly in the moment ("What I care about is X because I want to honor my value of Y") to unblock a conversation rather than staying stuck in frustration (p. 40).

The values exercise she recommends (adapted from Stan Slap's *Bury My Heart at Conference Room B*, pp. 64 to 66):

1. Imagine yourself at 80, reflecting on your career. What gave your work life meaning?
2. Write down 10 core values that imagining surfaces.
3. Narrow to five, then to three.
4. Against each value, write the activities that show you living it.
5. Reflect on the positive outcomes of each value, then its trade-offs. Her example: valuing excellence delivers high-quality work but can mean missed deadlines.

The exercise can be run as a team, sharing the top three values in small groups; a team's shared values can become a starting point for its mission.

## Identify your work style preferences

The most familiar dimension is introvert versus extrovert; her own litmus test is "do you talk to think or think to talk" (she talks to think). Beyond that, preferences vary on things like whether someone likes defining a process or resists one, and understanding them tells you your likely weak points and who complements you (pp. 40 to 41).

She maps most style assessments onto a two-axis grid, introverted to extroverted and task-oriented to people-oriented, producing four quadrants she names herself, adapted from assessments she has seen (pp. 41 to 44):

- **Analyzer** (introverted, task-oriented): deliberate, data-seeking, rigorous; can struggle to act without data and to bring people along.
- **Director** (extroverted, task-oriented): strong opinions, biased to fast action, good at setting a vision; can dictate rather than build process, which disempowers others.
- **Promoter** (extroverted, people-oriented): charismatic, full of ideas, good at inspiring narrative; weaker on detail and follow-through.
- **Collaborator** (introverted, people-oriented): customer-focused, good at bringing others along; can overcomplicate to avoid leaving anyone out.

No quadrant is objectively better; fit depends on the situation. A technical outage calls for a director who can decide fast without building consensus first; a durable planning process calls for a collaborator, and probably a promoter too (pp. 43 to 44).

She names other assessment frameworks in passing: DiSC, Myers-Briggs, and her personal favorite, Insights Discovery, which she credits for giving her the vocabulary to describe herself as an unusual pairing of "supporter" (green) with "director" (red) tendencies (pp. 44 to 46).

The related exercise: place yourself on the introvert/extrovert by task/people-oriented grid, once for home and once for work, and compare (p. 67).

## Analyze your skills and capabilities

Two related but distinct questions (p. 46):

- **Skills**: what can you do, tactically. Binary and demonstrable, such as writing code or building a financial model.
- **Capabilities** (also called competencies): a step above skill, an innate ability to apply skills to a situation. Her test for whether a capability is innate: is doing it well as easy as breathing, something you have never had to consciously acquire.

Strengths are the sum of skills and capabilities, and both matter, but capability is what generalizes: her example is that "writing better emails" is a skill, while the underlying leadership communication capability transfers to every other channel (pp. 47 to 48).

The hazard she names explicitly: your biggest strength is also your biggest weakness (a line she credits to her business school dean, Jeffrey Garten). Teams become mirrors of their managers, so a manager who does not recognize this will unconsciously build a team of people who share the same blind spot, for example a team of strategists who never execute. The fix is to hire and elevate people whose strengths complement yours, not duplicate them, and to build personal mechanisms that force you out of your comfort zone, such as her rule to ask a question first before jumping to action (pp. 48 to 50).

The related exercise: list your strengths, then break each into skill versus capability, and innate versus acquired, using her communication example as the model (pp. 67 to 68).

## The self-awareness assessment

Signs you may lack self-awareness (pp. 50 to 51):

- You consistently get feedback from multiple sources that you disagree with.
- You are often frustrated that colleagues do not understand your direction or decisions.
- You feel drained at the end of the day and cannot say why.
- You cannot describe what kinds of work you do and do not enjoy.
- You have unresolved friction with your manager.

If any of these apply, she points to two other exercises in the book that build self-awareness further: writing a "Working with Me" document, and running career conversations with reports (covered later, in her Chapter 3 material).

## Exercises and templates

**Understand your values (pp. 64 to 66; the five-step exercise above).** Includes a sample list of values with one-line definitions (drawn from Slap's fuller list of 50) and a worksheet: value, work activities, positive outcomes, trade-offs, for up to ten values.

**Articulate your values (pp. 66 to 67).** A three-column worksheet, personal value, why it matters, and an example story, modeled on her own "impact" example: why she chose not to attend law school.

**Consider your work style preferences (p. 67).** Plot yourself on the introvert/extrovert by task/people-oriented grid, for home and for work.

**Identify your strengths (pp. 67 to 68).** List your strengths, then classify each as an innate or acquired skill, and an innate or acquired capability, following her "communication" worked example.
