# 1.3 Distinguish between management and leadership: considerations

What is worth knowing before doing the work in `tasks.md`. Topics follow the book's order. "WP p. N" is the white paper (`sources/extraction/s01/record.md`). Nothing here is a decision; where the old synthesis argued a position it appears as an option with its reasoning and what it commits Sŏn to.

## 1. Her cut and Sŏn's cut

### What the white paper holds

The white paper cuts the management layer on a different axis. "Every management function in a restaurant resolves into one of two orientations. The first is strategic: what needs to happen, what the standard is, what the organization is building toward. The second is coordinative: how it happens, how execution moves" (WP p. 10). These become two parallel co-leads. The Operations Lead "is accountable to the operational outcomes" and "oversees those systems and is accountable to their outputs, not their manual production." The Maitre d "owns the how of the experience," the service culture and emotional standard, team development, and recovery, and is "accountable to experience quality and team health." "These roles are parallel. Neither reports to the other. They are co-leads of different systems serving the same center, the culinary and conceptual vision" (WP p. 10). Bundling them into one General Manager is "the load-bearing model's final failure mode" (WP p. 10).

Management "sits at the center because that is where the most connections intersect. This is a systems function, not a rank" (WP p. 09). Functional designations (Lead Host, Chef Partner during service, Head of Beverage) "mark accountability in the moment, in the domain. They are not permanent elevations above anyone" (WP p. 10).

Two further things bear on her distinction. Stability at Sŏn is produced by the system: "the operating layer handles much of what a traditional operations manager once did by hand" (WP p. 10), and in a traditional house "the operator is the integration layer" (WP p. 21). Change enters through the process loop: an incident is captured at shift close, the process is updated, and "the next server inherits a system that has already learned from it" (WP p. 13).

### Considerations

**Her axis and the white paper's are not the same axis.** "What needs to happen, what the standard is" sounds like leadership; "how execution moves" sounds like management. But the white paper assigns both orientations to management functions, puts them in two parallel roles rather than two modes of one person, and requires each lead to carry both of her modes. The Maitre d sets the emotional standard of the room (direction) and develops the team (stability). The Operations Lead reads the systems (stability) and updates the architecture when a process fails (change). The partial fit is the trap.

**Overlaying her distinction on the leads does one of two things.** It ranks one lead above the other, the "leader" and the "manager," which the parallel structure forbids. Or it splits each lead into two people, a leader and a manager per domain, which reintroduces the manual-integration headcount the white paper removed (WP p. 22: eight leadership lines against fourteen).

**The old synthesis argued three moves, now options.** First, apply the distinction to problems, not people: technical problems (the ones with a fix) route to the process loop and the domain owner; adaptive problems (the ones that keep moving: a shift in who is in the room, a competitor, a service period not finding its customer) route to judgment, the domain owner's inside the domain and the founders' together when the problem crosses domains or touches the concept. The value of her distinction becomes a triage question asked of every recurring problem: does this have a fix, or does it need a judgment? A lead who keeps applying process fixes to an adaptive problem is building the wrong thing; a founder who keeps applying judgment to a technical problem is holding the house in their head. Second, direction lives at the center as a thing, not a rank: the culinary and conceptual vision the leads serve (WP p. 10), held by the founders as domain owners and written down, which is what makes it survive them. Third, management decreases as a person develops threads and direction stays constant: onboarding puts why before how (WP p. 18), training runs from procedure to judgment (WP pp. 14, 19), and the invisible advocate removes the manager from advancement (WP p. 11), so her observation that experienced people need mostly a leader is already the shape of Sŏn's progression.

**The departure from the book, stated plainly.** She holds that management creates stability. At Sŏn stability is a property of the system; the leads maintain it, they do not produce it by hand. A lead who is producing stability personally, holding the schedule in their head, being the integration layer between floor and kitchen, chasing the linen order, is not a good manager doing extra. They are the CPU failure and the node-overload failure at once (WP pp. 13, 14), and the fix is to restore the system, not to praise the lead. Most of the book's management apparatus assumes the manager is the stabilizer; that assumption has to be checked in every chunk from 3.1 on.

**One clarification.** She notes that management structures have worked for thousands of years and treats decentralized experiments as forays (p. 18). Sŏn's web is not one of those forays: it has a center, two named leads, documented competencies, and explicit domain ownership (WP pp. 09 to 11). It is a management structure. It is not one that holds weight from the top.

**Options on how Sŏn uses her distinction.** (a) As a triage rule on problems, with direction held at the center and no second axis on the leads. Commits Sŏn to teaching the triage question to both leads and to the founders holding the vision in writing. (b) As a second axis over the leads: each lead is read for both modes, and the founders coach toward leadership as she describes. Commits Sŏn to a vocabulary that can slide into ranking the two leads. (c) Not at all: the what-and-how split does the work and her words are not used. Commits Sŏn to losing the triage question, which is the one piece of her distinction the white paper does not already hold.

## 2. Direction, and the scheduled disappointments

### What the white paper holds

The leads serve "the culinary and conceptual vision" (WP p. 10). Brandon is "the public voice and the origin of the operating concepts" (WP p. 03); the chef partner owns the culinary vision (WP p. 03). The white paper has already scheduled several disappointments: the service period that does not open yet, the lunch that may come out of the plan entirely, the concept that stays unnamed until proven (WP pp. 36, 38). It does not say who speaks the vision to the team internally or how the leads carry it when no founder is in the building.

### Considerations

**Her leadership lives at Sŏn in the vision, and in the founders' obligation to hold it against pressure.** Linsky's line, disappointing people at a rate they can absorb, is the founders' job: the kitchen that wants lunch, the room that could open earlier, the team that wants the next concept named. The white paper has pre-committed the disappointments; the founders have to say them out loud, repeatedly, to people who will keep asking. Who says them and on what rhythm is the internal-voice question, decided in 2.1.20.

**A lead's leadership is real and bounded.** Inside their domain, each lead sets direction: the Maitre d sets the emotional standard of the room; the Operations Lead decides how the architecture changes when a process fails. Whether either lead is expected to grow into direction-setting for the house, as her senior managers must (p. 57), is a different question. At Sŏn the house's direction is held by the founding seats and written down (2.1); the leads' growth is in the depth and reach of their domains, not in altitude toward the center (WP p. 11). This is where her "managers hit a ceiling" (p. 57) does not transfer: there is no ceiling to hit because there is no ladder above the lead.

## 3. Leadership without a management role

### What the white paper holds

Below the leads the structure is "strands, not levels" (WP p. 10). Functional designations mark "accountability in the moment, in the domain" and are "not permanent elevations above anyone" (WP p. 10). Cross-strand fluency "is the real development marker," visible as more connections and "recognized in compensation as meaningful" (WP p. 11). A host "can become the Maitre d the city has never seen" (WP p. 19). The goal is internal succession, "to the point where the building rarely hires a manager from outside at all" (WP p. 19). Any qualified team member can create training modules and is paid for teaching (WP p. 19). Every new person is paired with a mentor who is "a proven cultural steward" (WP p. 18).

### Considerations

**Her third use of the distinction fits Sŏn well.** She kept high performers as leaders in non-manager roles rather than promoting them into management (p. 58). At Sŏn nearly everyone is in a non-manager role for good, because there are two leads and no ladder. The senior server who sets the tone of a section, the station lead who holds the line's standard, the mentor, the person who writes the training module: each is leading without managing anyone. The white paper already recognizes some of this (designations, cross-strand fluency in pay, paid teaching, the mentor). Whether Sŏn names it as leadership, and how, is open.

**What naming it commits Sŏn to.** If leadership without a role is named, it needs a form: a designation (4.1), a place in the progression structure (4.1 and the competencies), and a recognition in pay (5.5). If it is not named, the white paper's mechanisms still recognize the behavior, but nobody can be told "you are a leader here" without a role, and her retention argument (p. 58) has nothing to attach to.

**Options.** (a) Name it: leadership at Sŏn is a set of recognized behaviors and designations open to anyone, distinct from the two lead roles, with the recognition designed in 4.1 and 5.5. Commits Sŏn to defining the behaviors and to a designation that is not a rank. (b) Do not name it: the strands, the designations, and cross-strand pay already carry it. Commits Sŏn to relying on the mechanisms without the word. (c) Name it only in the mentor and teaching roles the white paper already holds. Commits Sŏn to a narrow form.

## 4. The word "manager"

### What the white paper holds

The white paper uses "management" as a function ("management sits at the center," "management absorbs blame from staff," WP pp. 09, 17) and uses "manager" mostly for the roles it is replacing ("a traditional operations manager," "a General Manager," WP pp. 10, 22). It calls the Maitre d "the Hospitality Manager in full deployment" (WP p. 10). It sets role naming aside: "Role naming convention, a separate session, titles held to be scale-independent and non-concept-specific" (WP p. 15).

### Considerations

A later session in the old program removed "manager" from the vocabulary entirely. That is a real choice with consequences for every document written before 4.1 settles the naming convention: the founding document, the candidate sheet (WP p. 18), the leads' role descriptions, and the working-with-me documents all have to call the layer something. The book uses "manager" throughout; the white paper's own usage is mixed. Whether the word is used for the two leads, used only for the function, or not used at all is a small decision that binds a lot of writing. The naming convention for every role stays in 4.1; this decision is only about the one word.

## 5. Where Sŏn may diverge from the book

Three places. Stability is a property of the system, not of managers (section 1). The leads do not need to grow into house-level direction to avoid a ceiling, because there is no ladder above them (section 2). And "leader in a non-manager role" is not the exception at Sŏn but the normal case (section 3). Her triage question survives all three.
