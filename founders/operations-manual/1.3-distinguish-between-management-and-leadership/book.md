# 1.3 Distinguish between management and leadership: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 1, principle 3, pp. 56 to 59. Full text: `extraction/build/s01/book.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## The distinction

Both are necessary for scaling a company; they are not the same thing (p. 57).

**Leaders** put forth a vision and set lofty goals that inspire others to forge ahead when the path is not clear. The clarity of the vision keeps everyone on the big picture and sustains motivation. Leaders do not have to be managers, but if they are not, they need to know how to work with and hire managers to build the teams that execute the vision. It often feels like leaders ask a bit too much; that is what provides motivation (p. 57).

**Managers** run the teams that do the actual building. "Management is all about human-centric execution." Great managers define goals, set operational cadences, and give each report a clear view of current performance and future aspirations. Their teams have high trust, experience the challenge and reward of hard work, and feel they are making progress. Managers do not initially have to be leaders, but the more senior they become the more they must develop leadership skills, or they hit a ceiling; eventually a manager must be able to set a vision and "make the team uncomfortable with a bit of heat" (p. 57).

Her quoted executives: Zanny Minton Beddoes, leadership is setting direction, standards, expectations, and tone, and management is implementing it, getting the processes, people, and teams right (p. 56); Reid Hoffman, management is playing the game well and reading the dashboard, leadership is setting the spirit and the belief that winning matters (pp. 58 to 59).

## Technical versus adaptive problems

Her second way to think about it, from Heifetz, Grashow, and Linsky's *The Practice of Adaptive Leadership*. Technical problems have a solution and an achievable resolution (a missed service-level target). Adaptive problems keep adapting; they are "an infinite game" (how to set product priorities as needs and competition evolve). "Managers are superb at solving technical problems. Tackling adaptive problems takes leadership." A great manager can get comfortable; a true leader is uncomfortable almost every day (pp. 57 to 58).

## What the distinction is for

Three uses she names:
- Retaining high performers she might have promoted into management but who were more effective as leaders in non-manager roles (p. 58).
- Pushing herself and others past technical problem management into adaptive leadership; her own Big Five profile reads as a classic manager, and she had to learn to be disagreeable and demand more (p. 58).
- Reframing a problem. Her coaching example: a product marketing leader kept seeking the perfect process to sync with a shifting roadmap; the realization that the situation called for continual adaptation and influence, not a one-time fix, freed her to lead (p. 59).

## Disappointing people at a rate they can absorb

Marty Linsky's line, which she repeats often. Leadership drives change; management creates stability. Stability matters, but confronting challenges and realizing new ideas require discomfort, so teams must sometimes abandon the stable and familiar for an uncertain new direction (pp. 59 to 60).

## Who needs which

Experienced employees mostly need a leader and a bit of a manager: the overarching vision, the milestones, and someone to pave the way through the systems outside the team, pushing them to think differently and aim higher. Less experienced employees mostly need a manager and a bit of a leader: help with tactics, day-to-day, and development (p. 60).
