## 3. Briefs

### 1.4.1 Decide what "operating system" means at Sŏn and what the software is called

**The question:** when a document at Sŏn says "the operating system," is it the human system of missions, goals, metrics, and rhythms, or the software? And what is the other one called?

**Why it matters now:** the white paper uses the phrase for both (WP pp. 03, 21 to 22). A lead who reads "the operating system is running" will not know whether to look at a screen or a calendar. 2.1.7 draws the seam between what the OS surface must do and how it is built; that seam cannot be drawn until the two objects have different names (cons. 1).

**Openers:**
- When you say "I own the operating systems" (WP p. 03), what are you picturing: a meeting, a page, a screen?
- Sideways: the shift close writes to the stack and the shift brief reads from it (WP pp. 13 to 14). Which of those two is the operating system, and which is the nervous system? Say why.
- If Dominic finished the OS surface tomorrow and the house had no cadence, would you say the operating system existed?

**Narrowing questions:**
- Option (a), (b), or (c) (cons. 1)?
- If (a): is the software "the stack," "the OS surface," or both depending on the layer?
- Does the next revision of the white paper adopt the convention, or does the white paper keep its own words and the manual translates?

**What the book says:** an operating system is the human system every team runs on: missions, goals, metrics, meeting structures, cadences (p. 61); it is the box you constructed (p. 61).

**White-paper default:** none; it uses the phrase both ways. The stack "moves the complexity that was always there off of people and into software" (WP p. 21), which is the relationship between the two, stated outright.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific. Nothing useful; the naming collision is Sŏn's own.

**Options:** cons. 1 (a), (b), (c), with what each commits Sŏn to.

**Watch for:** settling it by letting Dominic's usage win because the object is software; a convention that requires a qualifier every time, which writers will drop.

**A finished answer:** one sentence naming the two objects and their words, and a note on whether the white paper adopts it.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.

### 1.4.2 Decide which frameworks apply to everyone at Sŏn and which vary by unit

**The question:** what short list of components must every domain and every service period run on in the same form, and what is free to vary?

**Why it matters now:** her shortcut only works if the components are the same everywhere (p. 61). Sŏn's units differ on purpose: three service periods with different first customers (WP p. 35) and domains with different intelligences (WP pp. 10 to 11). The list has to be short enough to respect that and complete enough that a founder can open any unit's page and find the same things. 2.2 and 2.3 build whatever is on the list; 2.1.14 decides whether the reason-to-exist item is a charter.

**Openers:**
- You open the late-night page in month nine. What five things do you expect to see on it, in the same places as on dinner's page?
- Sideways: at Coqodaq, what did the kitchen and the floor each run on that the other did not, and did it cost anything?
- Her Introduction rule is that too many company-wide processes eat headspace (p. 20). Which of the white paper's mechanisms would the Maitre d resent being made uniform?
- What does "its own operating logic" (WP p. 34) mean for the early morning, concretely? What must it be free to do differently?

**Narrowing questions:**
- From the candidates in cons. 2: a reason to exist, leading-indicator metrics, the shift brief and close, a review rhythm, a feedback channel. In, out, or added?
- Goals per unit and a rhythm above the shift: uniform, or only where a unit needs them?
- For each item kept, which chunk builds it?
- Is the kitchen's list the chef partner's to amend once seated?

**What the book says:** few frameworks should apply to everyone, in four areas (pp. 20 to 21); every team runs the same components (p. 61).

**White-paper default:** the candidates listed in cons. 2, each stated outright in the white paper for the house as a whole; that they are the uniform set per unit is inferred. Each period having "its own operating logic" (WP p. 34) is stated outright.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Amazon's mechanisms (the written narrative, the weekly metrics review, the hiring bar) are the best-known case of a short uniform set enforced everywhere; the book cites *Working Backwards* for it (p. 20).
- Hotel groups with several outlets under one roof commonly standardize the pre-shift meeting, the review rhythm, and the customer-recovery standard across outlets and leave menu, pace, and service style to each; that split resembles Sŏn's.

**Options:** a short set as the default; a longer set that includes goals and a weekly rhythm per unit; or a minimal set (reason to exist and metrics only) with everything else left to the domain owner. Each commits 2.2 and 2.3 to a different amount of building.

**Watch for:** a list so long it is the white paper again; a list so short the founders become the integration layer between units, which is the failure the stack exists to remove (WP p. 21).

**A finished answer:** the list, each item with its builder chunk, and a sentence on what varies by unit; the kitchen's amendments held for the chef partner.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders; the chef partner for the kitchen's part once seated.

### 1.4.3 Decide which tempos the founders set deliberately fast and which the hour and the table set

**The question:** of service tempo, the build sequence, the build tempo, and a founder's presence on the floor, which do the founders set faster than is comfortable, which do they protect, and which belong to the hour and the leads?

**Why it matters now:** her advice is to set an uncomfortable pace because a growing company will anyway (p. 19). Sŏn has no growing headcount to set it. The white paper describes several tempos that point different ways (cons. 4), and the founders will carry the build's speed into dinner's first quarter unless they decide not to, because that is the quarter when the build engine idles and both of them are in the room. 2.3 designs the cadence at whatever tempo is decided here; 4.5 inherits how a founder behaves on the floor.

**Openers:**
- Tell me about a night on a floor when someone in charge tried to make the room go faster. What happened to the room?
- The matcha and the espresso are built to be fast at the counter because the labor was done in the back (WP pp. 04 to 05). Where else in the house is speed bought that way, and where is it being asked of the person at the point of service?
- It is the second month of dinner. The build has been fast for a year. You are in the room at nine. What are your hands doing?
- Sideways: what would make you add the early morning before dinner's quarter is up? Name the temptation, not the rule.
- The content engine is sized to run "without burning the founders out" (WP p. 30). Is that true of the rest of the build?

**Narrowing questions:**
- For each of the four tempos: set fast, protect, or leave to the hour and the leads?
- If service tempo is protected: what does protecting it look like on a short night? (Covers, not the floor; the mechanism decided in 2.1.4 if that rule is adopted.)
- A founder in the room during dinner's first quarter runs on the room's tempo: accept or decline by name?
- The founders' own learning pace: whose question is it? (The default hand-off is 6.1.)

**What the book says:** break through a plateau by practicing faster than is comfortable; a growing company sets that pace for you (pp. 18 to 19); routine first, then break it (p. 62).

**White-paper default:** cons. 4, option (a), assembled from statements the white paper makes outright: the morning fast by design (WP p. 05); dinner composed, late night quieter (WP p. 33); speed bought upstream (WP pp. 04 to 05, 21, 26); the sequence gated on proof (WP pp. 36, 38); the build fast by system and sized for the founders' bandwidth (WP pp. 18, 22, 30 to 31); learning self-paced (WP p. 11); bandwidth first, "mechanical, not aspirational" (WP pp. 12, 17). Assembling them into two metronomes is inferred.

**How others have handled it:** General practice, not Sŏn-specific.
- Restaurants that protect a composed dining room on a full night do it by controlling the door and the book, not by asking the floor to move faster; the reservation pacing is treated as an operating decision made in advance.
- Companies that document their build in public tend to run the content fast and the product slow, and say so; the white paper's own peer example ran fourteen months of documentation before opening (WP p. 29).

**Options:** cons. 4 (a), (b), (c), with what each commits Sŏn to.

**Watch for:** choosing (a) and then describing dinner's first quarter as the time to "get things done"; treating the build tempo as fast by effort rather than by system, which is the cooling failure the white paper names (WP p. 13); Brandon's own pace and energy creeping in, which is 6.1 and his document.

**A finished answer:** a ruling per tempo; the dinner-quarter consequence accepted or declined by name; the learning-tempo question handed to 6.1; 2.3 and 4.5 told.

**Needs agreement from:** both seated founders.
