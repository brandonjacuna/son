# 1.4 Come back to your operating system: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 1, principle 4, pp. 60 to 62, with the Introduction's passages on core frameworks (pp. 20 to 21) and the metronome (pp. 17 to 19), which this chunk also houses. Full text: `extraction/build/s01/book.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## The principle

The fourth of her four operating principles. The other three (self-awareness, saying the unsaid, management versus leadership) are about how you show up; this one is about what you build and keep coming back to. She articulated it late, in 2018, when a banker asked her what her secret power was. Her answer: "the ability to build a repeatable operating system for every team I manage" (p. 61).

## What a repeatable operating system is

Every team she runs has the same components: "clear missions, stated goals, metrics that matter, similar meeting structures, and weekly and quarterly cadences" (p. 61). Because the components are the same, she can switch contexts between teams without relearning each one. She calls it a common user interface for all of them (p. 61).

Two levels:
- **Team level.** A common operating approach across all of your teams gives you a management shortcut (p. 61).
- **Company level.** A common operating system that spans an entire company, including how people and teams are hired and developed, is "even more powerful" (p. 61). The four chapters that follow are that system's four core frameworks.

## Inside the box

Her business school professor's objection to "think outside the box": "To succeed, you actually want people thinking inside the box. The box you constructed!" (p. 61). She returns to it when she needs to frame a problem so a team sees the fundamental factors the same way, and as a reminder that the operating system "creates a foundation of consistent practices that we can all rely on, even when everything else is changing" (pp. 61 to 62).

## Routine first, then break it

Your system will be based on how you hire and develop your team and on the rhythms of how you operate: quarterly goals, a metrics meeting on Mondays, a team meeting on Tuesdays, weekly 1:1s, offsites for big-picture thinking. Teams vary, but these fundamentals create stability. There are times to break the rhythm, and "what makes doing so effective is the fact that you have a routine." Her analogies: figure drawing before going abstract, poetic meter before free verse, and the professor who taught every framework and then declared, "Now that you understand all of these frameworks, you can break them!" (p. 62).

## What the principles do not do

The three earlier principles create "an environment of ambitious trust," but none of them describes how you will drive results. Results come from the combination of the team environment and the team's execution, and that combination needs a set of core frameworks to work within. The rest of the book is those frameworks (p. 62). Bill Walsh, via *The Score Takes Care of Itself*: in that kind of environment you will not just win but repeatedly win (p. 62).

## From the Introduction: few frameworks, applied to everyone

Company-wide frameworks are indicators of priorities: the set of actions or processes everyone must perform the same way ("This is the way"). Enduring companies foster strong adherence to a small number of them; Amazon's combination of core frameworks and leadership principles is her example (p. 20). The constraint: massive coordination costs; too many company-wide processes and people spend their headspace following them; uniformity must outweigh variability. She names four areas where company-wide frameworks belong (foundations and planning; hiring; team development; feedback and performance) and allows a limited set of standardized cross-team activities, such as Stripe's product launches (pp. 20 to 21).

## From the Introduction: the metronome

Fitts and Posner found that skill acquisition runs through three stages, cognitive, associative, and autonomous, and that people plateau in the third: more practice no longer makes them faster (pp. 18 to 19). Ericsson's later work found that the typists who kept improving were those who deliberately practiced faster than was comfortable; Foer's memory coaching set a metronome 10 to 20 percent above his limit and accepted more errors to break through (p. 19). Her reading: to improve past the autonomous stage you need an uncomfortable pace, and a rapidly growing company that builds its systems as it goes sets that pace for you (p. 19). She hopes the book helps "set the metronome a little bit faster" (p. 25).
