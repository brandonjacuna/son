# 6.3 Consider your career: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Conclusion, pp. 498 to 503. Full text: `sources/extraction/build/s16/book.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## You are your own career coach

Leaders often spend so much energy on business outcomes and their reports' careers that they neglect their own. No one else knows what's inside your head, heart, and gut; waiting for a manager or outside force to tap you on the shoulder is a mistake, even though outside advice is welcome. Her own practice has been to periodically step back and ask whether she was learning what she wanted to learn and having the impact she wanted to have (p. 498). Careers tend to make sense only in hindsight; some pivotal moments feel monumental at the time (joining Stripe), others feel small and turn out to matter (moving from Google's consumer side to its B2B arm). Most people, in her view, wind toward a career path more like Chutes and Ladders than a straight line (pp. 498 to 499).

## Build career self-awareness

The same self-awareness discipline from managing energy applies to career choice: track which jobs and capabilities come naturally and which sap motivation, seeking the combination of aptitude (innate or acquired) and genuine motivation. Her example: she's good at fundraising but doesn't enjoy it, so she avoids roles where it would be more than half the job, common in senior academic, nonprofit, or cultural-institution roles. This is "pace layering" applied to yourself: track both day-to-day energy and longer-term energy curves (p. 499).

## Keep a personal document

Starting early in your career, keep a document noting the roles you've tried, with short entries answering (pp. 499 to 500):

- Were you good at it? How do you know? If not, why not?
- Did you enjoy it? Why or why not?
- What did it reveal about the work you do or don't want to do?
- Where did you have clear gaps in ability?
- What skills did you need to acquire, and was that easy or hard?
- Were you interested in the work? Did you want to keep learning?

Even a few bullets per role compound in value when reviewed every 6, 12, or 18 months. For an aspiring leader, the document also surfaces your complements, the abilities you should hire to augment rather than duplicate.

## The trap to avoid

The most common failure she's seen is getting stuck chasing someone else's definition of success, or staying in a role that doesn't play to your strengths or fulfill you. She flags her own awareness that having the financial position to make these choices is a privilege, and suggests using some of that privilege to help others who lack the same choices (p. 500).

## The 5-year and 6/12/18-month cadence

Her method for staying on a chosen path rather than drifting (pp. 500 to 501):

- **Every 5 years:** envision the shape of what you might want to be doing, not a title. Ask whether you're on track toward it; if not, understand why and whether to change course.
- **Every 6 months:** is this the right role to learn what you want to learn in the next 6 to 12 months, and are you clear on how and from whom you'll acquire that knowledge? If not, start adjusting.
- **Every 12 months:** should you be seeking role changes to stay on the 5-year path, and how do you make that known to your manager or mentors? She likes to have two or three potential directions in mind for the coming year, whether staying the course (for instance managing managers, or adding an adjacent team) or toggling to a new area and a new functional skill. Check that your ambitions match your demonstrated impact; more impact earns the right to more ambition.
- **Every 18 months:** is the 5-year aspiration still right? Consult the personal document and what the last 12 to 18 months revealed about your skills, energy, and motivators. Her own example: once imagining she'd become a CMO, then realizing, based on her motivators and demonstrated abilities, that the path ran instead toward product management and leading additional functions.

Above all: don't adopt someone else's career aspirations or path. Devise your own, and get comfortable keeping your own counsel, aligning head, heart, and gut (p. 502).

## Closing

Company building and managing people are both extremely hard and extremely rewarding. Understand your energy, your abilities, and the boundaries you need; if you're not feeling stable, take stock, delegate, reframe, and ask for help. As you build personal strength, be the manager your own manager can lean on, and the colleague others want to be near. When you feel less strong, return to your starting point: yourself (p. 502).

Quoted: Dongping Zhao, president of Anker Innovations, on the three levels of career skill: hard skills, soft skills, and the hardest, how you show up emotionally to keep making things possible (p. 502).

The book closes by naming its intent as a return to fundamentals: not innovative, often tactical, requiring the work, in hope of building confidence, self-awareness, the ability to say the hard thing, and eventually a reader's own operating system (pp. 502 to 503).
