# 5.10 Managing out, firing, and layoffs: what the book says

Claire Hughes Johnson, *Scaling People*, Chapter 5, pp. 454 to 466, with the Managing Out Checklist at pp. 481 to 482. Full text: `extraction/build/s15/book.md`, `extraction/build/s15/exercises.md`. This file is a quick reference; nothing here is about Sŏn.

## Where this picks up

This returns to Outcome 3 from the low-performer section: feedback, coaching, and formal review have run their course and the person has not improved and needs to leave. Sometimes the person and manager mutually reach that conclusion before or during the review process; sometimes it falls to the manager to deliver the news that the improvement bid failed. Either way, facilitating a departure takes work (p. 454).

Two paths: managing someone out, where manager and report jointly arrive at the conclusion that they should leave, so a formal termination conversation usually isn't needed; and firing, a formal termination conversation, which is rarer. Firing is one of the hardest things a manager does, so pull in support from your own manager and HR, including for a practice conversation (p. 454).

## Why it matters to act

Having to fire someone is a reminder of how important the hiring process is, but it's still essential to running a good organization. No hiring process is perfect, so there will be people who don't work out. Letting bad hires linger leaves organizational scars that take a long time to heal: high performers get demoralized watching low performers go unmanaged, and a bad hire at a senior level may hire others who also miss the bar, with effects that take years to undo. The operating rule: fire fast but fire well, because how forced departures are handled reflects on the manager, the team, and the company. The best outcome is mutual agreement that the person should leave (pp. 454 to 455).

Managing someone out should be a short process built on a few clear steps that culminate the feedback work already done (p. 455):

1. Pre-work: feedback, documentation, and preparation
2. The departure conversation
3. Logistics and next steps

## Pre-work

Pre-work is the performance management work already covered in the chapter, plus making sure the right people know. Preparation should include (pp. 455 to 456):

- Assembling all written feedback on performance issues, and any prior performance improvement plans and results
- Familiarizing yourself with local employment law (protected classes, discrimination law, and, in some places, intricate termination processes)
- Informing your manager and HR partner, if not already done

## The departure conversation

Junior roles tend to be more straightforward to manage out: standardized requirements make it easier to show where someone fell short, and fewer dependencies mean less need for a transition plan beyond communicating the departure. Senior departures are lengthier and more complex: the person was likely a high performer elsewhere, so the conversation has to be framed around fit with this specific role and company, and the departure will affect more people, so transition planning matters more (p. 456).

Her approaches apply in markets without intricate termination law.

### Junior-level conversations (one to two conversations, pp. 456 to 458)

Open by summarizing the performance improvement plan and what was agreed, then ask what the person thinks about their progress. If the employee proactively raises leaving, move to logistics. Otherwise, state plainly that you haven't seen enough progress and want to agree on a departure timeline. This is not up for negotiation on the decision itself; move quickly from the decision to planning the departure (in some places this includes negotiating an exit package).

Expect one of two responses: re-litigating, which happens less than 5 percent of the time (stay kind but firm, restate the reasons and the process, close by repeating the decision), or moving to next steps, where the amount of flexibility given depends on company and regulatory policy; she prefers letting people control what they still can, which moves things toward practical questions like how to inform people, when the last day is, and how to help. A second conversation often wraps up final logistics; aim to complete this in two sittings. Rare exceptions that justify pausing: a threat of legal action, or new information that causes you to reconsider. Don't let the process drag: an underperforming employee who stays is neither productive nor happy, and the longer they remain, the more damage to the team.

### Senior-level departure conversations (pp. 458 to 459)

These typically play out over a few months and usually skip a formal performance improvement plan. It's one of the rare situations where leaving some things unsaid is fine: half the time the senior leader sees it coming and volunteers that it may be time to leave, which is the best outcome because it makes a smooth transition more likely.

These conversations, taking at least two or three sessions, center on whether the role's needs are being met, framed around the gap between the role, the company's needs, and observed performance, not about the person. If the two sides don't see eye to eye, be more direct ("I don't see us closing the gap between what's needed and what you're delivering") and move to timing and transition. Negotiation, when needed, can cover severance, medical insurance, or timing relative to stock vesting; the less mutual the situation, the more negotiation to expect. The backstop tool is a severance matrix, a framework HR and employment legal should have built for termination departures; involve HR or employment legal directly if the negotiation gets difficult.

## Legal considerations

If an employee threatens legal action while being managed out, get the right support from HR, legal, or external counsel. Practical steps (pp. 459 to 460):

- Understand the employment laws of your country, sought out at the start of the managing-out process
- Know basic employment law, discrimination law, and protected classes
- Watch for words like "retaliation" or "discrimination." If they come up, pause the conversation, tell the employee this isn't the conversation you expected to have, and continue it with the right people in the room, including HR and legal, with or without you.

## Gross misconduct and layoffs

Two other termination types a manager should be prepared to understand, even though handling them directly is less common (p. 460).

**Gross misconduct (pp. 460 to 461).** When someone has clearly violated the code of conduct, the termination conversation is usually straightforward, though it may follow an internal investigation and so come days after the incident. One conversation is enough: explain the violation and lay out departure terms; because these violations are severe, plan for same-day departure. If an investigation is needed first, notify the person of the accusation and the process, and keep the wait for a conclusion short (she suggests roughly 24 hours). If the investigation clears the person or is inconclusive, shift to a feedback conversation about the perception created and what to do differently. This all assumes a code of conduct exists.

**Layoffs (p. 461).** A layoff is not a firing; it is role elimination due to restructuring, and it must meet that standard. Because layoffs often accompany broader organizational change, they need an overarching communication and change management plan (she cross-references managing through uncertainty at p. 323), best directed by HR and legal or outside consultants where in-house support doesn't exist. Communications should be tightly managed.

## When something bad happens to an employee

She opens this section with a six-word Slack message from a colleague: "My dad didn't wake up today" (p. 461). Every tragedy is unique, but she groups tough situations into three types (pp. 461 to 464):

**A one-time event** (death of a family member or friend, miscarriage, severe injury, divorce, lawsuit). This depends on a trust relationship already built through 1:1s and expectation-setting. Be empathetic, express an appropriate reaction, ask what the person needs and how they want to be supported, then work out what resources and coverage the team can provide. Don't ask about timelines or handover immediately; people usually signal within a few days how much time and what transition they need.

**An ongoing hardship** (mental illness, a sick family member, a failing relationship, substance abuse). Trickier because the employee may not disclose what's happening. The manager's job is to observe behavior and, if something seems wrong, check in using coaching and feedback techniques: share an observation rather than pry, for example naming a change in punctuality or mood and asking if something outside work is going on. Most people are waiting to be noticed and want to talk; others will deny it, in which case the manager has to judge severity and whether to involve HR. Balance empathy with business needs: help someone get resources if they need time off, while also planning how the work gets covered.

**Legal ramifications** (sexual harassment, discrimination). Know your legal obligations; in California, for example, a manager who receives a sexual harassment report is legally obligated to report it. If someone tries to disclose something "off the record," a manager may not be able to commit to confidentiality and should say so, then point to resources such as an anonymous reporting tool or HR. Whenever workplace abuse is alleged, make sure the employee has access to the right resources.

Across all three types: don't compartmentalize if something feels really off; stop, trust your intuition, and seek help, since the worst mistake is failing to act. At the same time, avoid alarmism and keep information confidential; don't recount personal details to other managers, and loop in your own manager only with care for confidentiality. She illustrates with an employee who stopped showing up with no notice; a wellness check arranged through HR and security eventually traced it to a serious mental health crisis, resolved with HR and her own manager deciding, with the employee's agreement, that the person wouldn't return to work (pp. 464 to 465).

## Closing the chapter

The chapter closes by naming the reward side of management that the preceding pages, focused on hard work and hard conversations, may have understated. She recounts finding an envelope of handwritten notes from people she'd managed while packing up to leave Google for Stripe, calling it the purest expression of her work: management touches people's lives and trajectories, and the payoff can arrive months or years later, meaning everything when it does (pp. 465 to 466).

## Exercises and templates

**Managing Out Checklist (pp. 481 to 482).** A short checklist mirroring the chapter's process: provide feedback on performance issues; document the feedback; if relevant, run a formal performance improvement process; familiarize yourself with local employment regulations; inform your manager and HR partner, if you have one; hold the coaching-out conversation with your report; agree on the timeline for leaving and the communication plan; announce the departure to the team or organization, with transition plan details if the role needs immediate coverage.
