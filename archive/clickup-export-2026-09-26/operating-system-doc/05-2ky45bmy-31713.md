# Session 4: The operating cadence, and the Chapter 2 exercises

This page is the Sŏn answer to the operating cadence section of Chapter 2 (Book p.135 to 143) and to the Chapter 2 exercises appendix (Book p.144 to 165). It is written to be run from. A person who has not read the book can read this page and know what happens on which day, who runs it, what it reads, what it records, and what it refuses to do.

Every position on this page is numbered and carries exactly one mark. (Recommended) is the team's position, ready for founder ratification. (Founder-gated) requires input the team cannot supply. (Chef-gated) is left open for the chef partner. (Team-filled) is populated once the team exists. Nothing on this page is final. A recommended position is a recommendation until Brandon ratifies it, and a founder-gated position is argued to the ceiling one founder can reach and then marked for what Dominic, the chef, or the team must close.

Vocabulary on this page follows the ledger. "Operating system," unqualified, is the human system of strategy, accountability, and cadence. The software is "the stack" or "the OS surface," never "the OS" alone. The customer is the customer. Service periods are named by time of day. Financials are not a context source for this program, and no figure on this page is a financial one.

## 1\. Her ask, and what Sŏn already holds

### 1.1 Her ask

Her operating cadence is "the schedule or rhythm with which your company, divisions, or teams provide progress reports and make decisions" (Book p.136). She gives the reason for it in one clause: "humans are fundamentally creatures of habit" (Book p.136), and a rhythm they can follow naturally is what turns an operating system into something that runs. Her examples are annual planning, quarterly business reviews, monthly all-hands, biweekly 1:1s, weekly snippets and team meetings with metrics reviews, and daily standups (Book p.136). Her cadences "will very likely mirror the company cadence, which is often driven by milestones in the calendar year" (Book p.136).

Four of her rules bind harder than the list. "The key is to have a cadence but not get stuck in it" (Book p.136). If there is no cadence yet, "observe what types of interactions and reviews are happening organically" and build from those (Book p.137). "Ideally, there will be one owner for each key structure in the operating cadence, and all of the other leaders will agree to honor the structure" (Book p.139). And "a good process should subtract from the number of meetings, not add more" (Book p.142).

Her process notes carry three more. A defensive process is one that "exist\[s\] because people are not aligned on who owns a particular decision" (Book p.141); the cure is an owner, not a process. "Revisit your processes every six months or so. More often than that can be destabilizing, but less often will cause people to switch to autopilot and stop engaging" (Book p.141). An experiment with a process states its duration, its check-in date, and its evaluation criteria in advance (Book p.142). She closes with the warning signs that a cadence has failed: unclear owners and timelines, everything moving too slowly, problems flagged without solutions, status not findable, no one able to name the decision-maker, people ceasing to show up, and "you hate updating the tracker or attending your own meetings" (Book p.142 to 143).

Her feedback questions on the cadence (Book p.138 to 139) are a readiness check in disguise: does everyone know the mission and objectives at each level and their own expected work; is there a timeline and are there measures against targets; are dependencies agreed; does everyone know who is accountable and when results are due; are there mechanisms to monitor, decide, and unblock; are there communication structures for updates and changes; is success recognized and are mistakes shared; do internal communications reinforce the values. Session 3's readiness test already carries most of these. Section 12 carries the rest.

### 1.2 What the record holds

The record holds four rhythms and a set of rules about rhythm itself. It does not hold a cadence for the center.

The floor rhythm. "The rhythm is simple. Beginning of shift, prepare for the customer. During service, serve them. End of shift, reflect and strengthen the relationship" (WP p.26). The shift brief and the shift close are named mechanisms, and their absence is a named failure: "The shift brief is not happening, or the system is not surfacing preferences, or post-shift documentation is not captured. The nervous system is degraded. The fix is system maintenance, not people management" (WP p.14). The close is already allocated: "Every server sends a thank-you to every customer they served that night" (WP p.26), and the cleaning that would otherwise fill that window is handed to an overnight crew so "server labor gets redirected to relationship-building" (WP p.26). The learning loop runs through the close: the system "captures the incident in the shift close, the appropriate lead reviews, the process that allowed the failure gets updated, and the next server inherits a system that has already learned from it" (WP p.13).

The review and development rhythm. "Reviews happen every three to six months and focus on development, growth, and the path forward rather than a verdict. The governing rule is no surprises: continuous communication means a formal review never contains new information" (WP p.19). Advancement is self-paced and system-scheduled: "when a person is ready, they schedule a competency conversation with the appropriate lead and are signed off" and "No one's advancement waits on a manager's attention or judgment. The system is the advocate" (WP p.19, p.11). "A ninety-day plan sets expectations and goals with regular check-ins and course correction" (WP p.18). Feedback runs upward through "a dedicated channel that syncs to the execution system" and "The loop gets closed" (WP p.22). Peer recognition runs "in the moment" through a platform (WP p.19). Post-mortems "are private and process-focused" and open on "what allowed this to happen" (WP p.17).

The brand rhythm. "A quick daily pulse on engagement and growth. A weekly read on what performed by category and on partnership return. A monthly strategic review of platform shifts and where to move" (WP p.31), with a three-tier approval framework attached (WP p.31). This is the one complete cadence in the record: three frequencies, a decision-rights frame, an owner. It runs now, pre-opening, and continues in service. It is the model the rest of this page follows.

The phasing. "Dinner opens first and runs on its own for at least a quarter, so the heart is solid before anything is added to it. The other core dayparts follow once dinner is steady, most likely the early morning coffee first and then late night. Lunch and brunch sit further back still, not to be revisited until the restaurant has operated for a full year" (WP p.36). "A daypart added as an afterthought, without its own reason to exist and its own operating logic, becomes a worse version of dinner, however well it is executed" (WP p.33 to 34).

The rules about rhythm. Canon is explicit and repeated that the room's own transitions are person-triggered: "Person-triggered transitions only. No automated timers" (Canon 1.0, section 07); the pre-service scene is a thirty to forty-five minute "programmatic scene before first seating," "Person-triggered" and "not clock-automated" (Canon 1.0, section 06); the late-night transition is "same person, same moment. Not announced" (Canon 1.0, section 08). The step-back architecture is "attunement-triggered, not timer-triggered" (Canon 1.0, section 08). The day is one arc: "No daypart is a reset," and "each is a continuation" (Canon 1.0, section 02). Empty space "is a specification, not a default, and it is protected against the constant pressure to fill it," and canon prohibits "staff who manufacture activity because standing still feels wrong" (Canon 1.0, section 01). Change is versioned: "A living document changes by versioned decision, never by drift" (Deck 3.0, p.94). And the record holds gate-not-date instances already: the sonic system's "Pre-opening full review is non-negotiable" (Canon 1.0, section 07) and the uniform system's "Wear test is the governing production gate" (Canon 1.0, section 08).

Bandwidth governs all of it. "Bandwidth is the organizational metabolic rate. It is what the system has available after the work of survival is complete... Bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results" (WP p.12). Cooling failure is the failure mode a cadence can cause: an organization that "runs people at full capacity until they stop, and calls it dedication until it becomes turnover" (WP p.13).

### 1.3 The gap

The record holds a floor rhythm, a development rhythm, a brand rhythm, and a phasing plan. It holds no rhythm for the center: how the partners meet, decide, and read each other's domains. It holds no weekly rhythm for the house. It holds no shift-native form of her 1:1, and the ninety-day plan's "regular check-ins" (WP p.18) are named but not placed. It holds no goal cycle calendar, no mid-period scoring, no house review procedure, and no mechanism reset. It states the review rhythm as a range, three to six months, not a rhythm. It names the property of the room's transitions, person-triggered, and not the person. Section 14 lists what the record says twice or not at all.

Every gap on this list is filled below by a position. None of the positions re-derives a decision the record already made.

## 2\. Two rhythms, not one

### 2.1 The argument

The session task states the shape: two rhythms, a pre-opening cadence now through the opening, and an in-service cadence from the opening forward. The shape is argued here rather than assumed, because the simpler design, one rhythm with two phases, is the one her book implies and the one most operators would draw.

The argument for two rhythms has four parts.

First, the record already runs two metronomes. Service tempo "is set by the daypart's register and the table, never by a clock and never by the founders" (ledger). Build tempo is fast by system and gated on proof. Those are not two speeds of one clock. One has no clock at all. Canon forbids the room a timer three times over (Canon 1.0, sections 06, 07, 08). The build runs on a calendar because a build has milestones and a calendar is how you review them. A cadence is the instrument that carries a tempo, and one instrument cannot carry a tempo that has a clock and a tempo that refuses one.

Second, her cadence is a single rhythm because her unit is the quarter. Her cadences "mirror the company cadence, which is often driven by milestones in the calendar year" (Book p.136); her goal form is quarterly (Book p.153); her review is quarterly (Book p.156). In a company that ships software, the quarter is the unit of work and the day is its detail. In a restaurant the day is the unit of work. The record says so: "Beginning of shift, prepare for the customer. During service, serve them. End of shift, reflect" (WP p.26). Canon says the day is one arc (Canon 1.0, section 02). A restaurant forced into a quarterly planning cycle inverts its own unit: the quarter becomes the thing and the service becomes an instance of it. Sŏn's in-service rhythm has a quarter in it, the house review, but the quarter is a read, not a plan. The plan is the season and the gate.

Third, the hazard the ledger names is exactly the hazard one rhythm with two phases would produce. The build habit of speed leaks into the room during the first quarter of dinner. A single rhythm carries its habits across the seam by construction: the same meeting, the same clock, the same people, now pointed at the room. Two rhythms with a named seam and a named protection do not. The two rhythms are not a convenience. They are the protection.

Fourth, the phasing means both rhythms run at once for a long time. Dinner opens, and the early morning is still being built. The early morning opens, and late night is still being built. Late night opens, and the first full year and the lunch ruling are still ahead. The build does not end at the opening; it ends at the last gate. So "phase" is the wrong word for it. Two rhythms coexist from the first training shift until the last gate closes, and the design question is which members they share and which they do not, not when one hands off to the other.

**P1 (Recommended).** Sŏn runs two operating cadences: a pre-opening rhythm, from now until the last proof gate closes, running on build tempo; and an in-service rhythm, from the first training shift forward, running on service tempo. They coexist. They share named members (section 2.5) and keep the rest separate (section 2.6), and the seam between them carries a protection (section 2.7).

### 2.2 What a cadence is at Sŏn

Before the two rhythms are drawn, the word is bounded. Four rules from the ledger and canon apply to every mechanism on this page.

A cadence runs without a lead's memory. Stability is a property of the system. "A meeting that runs because someone remembers to call it is not a cadence" (ledger). Every mechanism below is placed by the stack's schedule or by a fixed relation to an event the stack already knows, so that it runs on the day the lead who owns it is sick.

A cadence does not clock-automate the room. Canon's rule is exact: "Person-triggered transitions only. No automated timers" (Canon 1.0, section 07). A cadence is a rhythm of reviews and decisions, and it sits above the floor. Where a mechanism on this page touches the floor, the shift brief and the shift close, it is placed at the edges of service, not inside it, and it triggers nothing in the room. The question whether attunement over timer holds above the floor is answered in section 2.4.

A cadence costs bandwidth, and bandwidth is first. "Bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results" (WP p.12). Every mechanism on this page states what it costs and what it buys (section 10.3). Ma governs: empty space "is protected against the constant pressure to fill it" (Canon 1.0, section 01). That is a stronger version of her own "a good process should subtract from the number of meetings, not add more" (Book p.142), and it is tier-one canon. A mechanism that cannot say what it buys is not placed.

A cadence passes the playground test. "Does this build or foreclose conditions for agency?" (Canon 1.0, section 01). A mechanism that exists so someone above can check on someone below forecloses agency and reintroduces the cascade the ledger forbids. Every mechanism below is owned by the unit that holds the information, and the center integrates.

**P2 (Recommended).** A mechanism enters Sŏn's cadence only if it runs without a lead's memory, triggers nothing inside the room, states its bandwidth cost and what it buys, and passes the playground test. A proposed mechanism failing any one of the four is not placed.

### 2.3 The pre-opening rhythm

The pre-opening rhythm runs on build tempo: fast by system, gated on proof. Its unit of work is the proof gate. Its units of time are the week and the calendar quarter. Its participants are the partners, the hires as they arrive, and the people building the stack and the room. Its surfaces are the partners' page and the goal pages.

The gates are fixed by the ledger: pre-opening; dinner alone; dinner steady, early morning added; late night added; the first full year; the lunch ruling. "Dates are not gates. A calendar quarter is the cadence on which gates are reviewed, not the thing that opens them" (ledger). Section 8 places the gates and the charters. What the pre-opening rhythm holds is this:

Weekly, the partners' review (section 9.2). This is the build's working meeting. It reads each domain's state against the readiness rows, takes the decisions in the partners' range, and records them by versioned decision.

Quarterly, the gate review (section 8.1). The readiness test is run against the next gate. A gate opens when its rows hold. A gate that does not open is reviewed again the next quarter, and the rows that failed become the quarter's goal page.

Continuously, the brand cadence at its three frequencies (WP p.31), owned by Brandon, unchanged across the seam.

Per arrival, the onboarding of each founding seat and each lead. Canon holds a chef onboarding sequence in a fixed order, Culinary Philosophy, Brand Narrative, Tableware Taxonomy, Menu Design System (Canon 1.0, "What Remains"). It is the only founder-onboarding sequence the record holds, and it sits on this rhythm, gated on the chef ruling (section 9.3).

The pre-opening rhythm has no daily unit at the house level. The brand cadence has a daily pulse, but that is one domain's rhythm. A build does not need a daily house meeting, and canon's prohibition on manufactured activity (Canon 1.0, section 01) says not to invent one.

Ownership of the build's workstreams is partly in the record. Brandon owns the concept, the operating systems, and the experience; Dominic owns the capital, the financial architecture, and the technology build (WP p.03). The session task names four workstreams: capital, construction, hiring, systems build. Capital and the technology build are Dominic's by the record. The operating systems are Brandon's. The record extracts read for this session do not assign construction or hiring to a partner. That is a gap, and it is marked rather than filled by analogy.

**P3 (Recommended).** The pre-opening rhythm is: the partners' review weekly; the gate review quarterly, on the calendar quarter, opening a gate only when its readiness rows hold; the brand cadence at its existing three frequencies; the onboarding sequence per founding seat and per lead. It has no daily house-level unit.

**P4 (Founder-gated).** The owning partner for the construction workstream and for the hiring workstream. The record assigns capital and the technology build to Dominic and the operating systems and experience to Brandon (WP p.03) and is silent on the other two. The team's recommendation: hiring sits with Brandon's seat, because the operating systems and the experience are what the hires run, and construction sits with Dominic's seat, because it is capital in built form. Brandon and Dominic close this.

### 2.4 The in-service rhythm

The in-service rhythm runs on service tempo. Its unit of work is the service. Its units of time are the day, the week, the season, the quarter, and the year. Its participants are the whole house. Its surfaces are the shift brief, the shift close, and the team home.

The day. One arc across every open period, opened by a shift brief and closed by a shift close per period (section 3). The transitions inside the day are the room's, person-triggered, and this cadence does not touch them.

The week. The leads' review (section 5.1): the one cross-period, whole-house read, opening on the leading indicators.

The month. The check-in per person (section 4), the employee NPS pulse (section 6.1), the partners' domain read (section 9.4).

The season. The menu turn. Canon names the menu design system's "change cadence" as a requirement to be set (Canon 1.0, "What Remains"), and it is chef-gated. The season is the house's planning unit in the way the quarter is hers: it is when the room changes. Section 6.4 places what the cadence can place around it and marks the rest.

The quarter. The house review (section 6.2), the goal cycle (section 7.3), the gate review for every gate still open (section 8.1), and the whole-house gathering if approved (section 6.5).

The year. The mechanism reset and the principles revision (section 6.6), and the first-full-year gate.

Now the question the brief asks: attunement over timer is canon's floor rule; does it hold above the floor? The answer is that it holds in one direction and not the other. Above the floor, a review runs on a schedule, because a review is not a customer touch and a review that waits for attunement waits for a lead's memory, which is the failure the ledger names. But nothing above the floor triggers anything on it. The leads' review does not set a service's pace; the house review does not schedule a transition; the goal cycle does not tell a server when to approach. Attunement governs every act in the room. The clock governs every act about the room. The seam between them is the shift brief: the last scheduled thing before the room takes over, and the first thing after it is the shift close.

**P5 (Recommended).** The in-service rhythm is: the day, one arc, bounded by the shift brief and the shift close of each open period; the week, the leads' review; the month, the check-in, the employee NPS pulse, and the partners' domain read; the season, the menu turn (chef-gated); the quarter, the house review, the goal cycle, the gate review for any open gate, and the whole-house gathering if approved; the year, the mechanism reset and the principles revision.

**P6 (Recommended).** Attunement governs every act inside the room; a schedule governs every act about the room; and nothing scheduled above the floor triggers anything on it. The shift brief and the shift close are the only cadence mechanisms that touch the room, and they sit at its edges.

### 2.5 Where the two rhythms share members

Seven members are shared. Each keeps its form across the seam and changes only its content.

The brand cadence (WP p.31). Unchanged. It is the record's own proof that a rhythm can span both metronomes: its daily pulse and weekly read run now and run in service.

The goal cycle (section 7). The same goal page form, the same three states, the same quarterly scoring and mid-period scoring. Pre-opening, the goal pages carry the readiness rows. In dinner's first quarter they carry binary states and baselining goals. From the second quarter they carry goals relative to a baseline. Form constant, content by phase, per the ledger's "goals before a baseline."

The readiness test (section 12). It runs before dinner opens and at every gate after, so it is a member of both rhythms until the last gate.

The partners' review (section 9). Weekly in both, with the in-service form moved to a closed hour and reading the leads' review's record rather than duplicating it.

The gate review (section 8.1). The pre-opening rhythm's quarterly review continues in service as long as a gate remains, and in service it is a standing section of the house review rather than a separate meeting.

The three channels. "Important information travels three ways: spoken at the brief of every open service period, written on the team home, carried by the domain lead or mentor" (ledger). From the first training shift, every cadence artifact on this page reaches the team by all three or has not been communicated.

The mechanism map's form (section 10). One map, with a column for which rhythm each mechanism belongs to.

**P7 (Recommended).** The seven shared members are the brand cadence, the goal cycle, the readiness test, the partners' review, the gate review, the three channels, and the mechanism map's form. Each keeps its form across the seam and changes its content.

### 2.6 Where they do not

Seven things are not shared, and naming them is what keeps the hazard out of the room.

The daily unit. The pre-opening rhythm has none at the house level. The in-service rhythm is built on one.

The leads' review. It does not exist until the leads exist. Before the first training shift, a meeting of the leads is a build meeting on the pre-opening rhythm. From the first training shift it is the in-service rhythm's weekly read, and its agenda changes character entirely (section 5.2).

The season. In-service only. The build has no menu turn, and the pre-opening menu work is the chef's, on the chef's sequence.

The quarter's meaning. Pre-opening, a calendar quarter is when gates are reviewed. In service, a quarter is the house review and the goal cycle. Same interval, different object.

The tempo. Build tempo is "fast by system and gated on proof" (ledger). Service tempo has no clock. A build meeting has an end time. A service ends when the last table leaves.

The participant base. The pre-opening rhythm's participants are the partners and a handful of hires. The in-service rhythm's participant is the whole house, most of it hourly and on the clock only when the room is open.

The surfaces. The pre-opening rhythm writes to the partners' page and the goal pages. The in-service rhythm writes to the team home, in the languages the team speaks, and is spoken at the brief.

**P8 (Recommended).** The seven unshared members are the daily unit, the leads' review, the season, the quarter's meaning, the tempo, the participant base, and the surfaces. No pre-opening mechanism is renamed and carried into the room; each in-service mechanism is built on the in-service rhythm's own unit.

### 2.7 The seam, and the protection

From the first training shift through dinner's first quarter, and again at every later opening, both rhythms are live in the same building with the same people. This is when the hazard is real: the partners who have run a build at speed for a year walk into a room that is supposed to run on the register and the table. The record has already decided the room's protection at the level of the founders' presence: it runs on the service metronome. What this page adds is the operating rule that makes the protection mechanical rather than a matter of the founders' self-restraint.

Four parts.

No build meeting in the building during service hours. Service hours run from the first shift brief of the day to the last shift close. The partners' review and every other build mechanism sit outside that window, at a closed hour or on a closed day (section 5.4, section 9.4). A build conversation that has to happen during service happens somewhere else.

The room's mechanisms are frozen for dinner's first quarter, except for two classes. The freeze covers the cadence's own rhythms: the brief's spine, the close's spine, the check-in's interval, the leads' review's day and agenda. The two exceptions are the committed class (anything that "gates an opening, protects a non-negotiable or a food-safety condition, or has been stated to customers through the documented build," per the ledger) and system maintenance through the shift close, which is the record's continuous learning loop (WP p.13) and never stops. The distinction is Jaeyeonmi's: "a maintenance philosophy that distinguishes care from correction" (Canon 1.0, section 01). Care runs daily through the close. Correction waits for the mechanism reset. Section 6.6 holds the reset.

The first-quarter goals give the build tempo nothing to push on. They are binary states and baselining goals (ledger). A build habit of speed needs an improvement target to run at, and in dinner's first quarter the room has none. This is the ledger's own rule doing double duty as a protection.

The founders' floor presence runs on the service metronome (ledger). This page places nothing that changes it.

**P9 (Recommended).** The seam protection is: no build meeting in the building during service hours; the room's cadence mechanisms frozen for dinner's first quarter except the committed class and shift-close maintenance; first-quarter goals as states and baselines only; and the founders' floor presence on the service metronome, unchanged. The protection repeats at each later opening for that period's first quarter.

## 3\. The day

The day is the in-service rhythm's unit, and the record already holds its shape (WP p.26). What the record does not hold is the spine of the brief and the close: the fixed content that runs every time whether or not anyone remembers it. The carryover names the inversion exactly: Sŏn's problem is not too few team meetings. The brief already happens daily. The problem is that the daily meeting carries no structure.

### 3.1 The shift brief

The shift brief is the record's "Beginning of shift, prepare for the customer" (WP p.26), and its absence is named sequence corruption (WP p.14). The Realist's read is that two objects hide in one meeting: the operational readiness that a service cannot open without, and the team-making content that makes the gathering a team. Only the first is the cadence's. The second belongs to the culture seats and to Session 10, and it sits after the operational block, never inside it.

The spine, in order, every brief, every open period:

1. The readiness block. The 86 list. Allergies and dietary notes routed to the stations that need them. Recognized customers on the book tonight, surfaced by the stack, because "whether the building is actually recognizing and remembering customers" (WP p.22) is a leading indicator and it starts at the brief. Staffing gaps named. Station checks confirmed. This block is a checklist the rush cannot delete: a thirty-second brief on a short-crewed night still moves the 86s and the allergies.
2. The period's goal and its one measure, read from the service period's goal page (section 7). One sentence.
3. The one thing from this week's leads' review (section 5.2). One sentence, the same sentence at every open period's brief that week. This is the spoken channel of the three.
4. Recognitions from the peer platform since the last brief (WP p.19). Read aloud, not editorialized.
5. Team content, owned elsewhere, bounded by what time remains before the scene trigger.

The brief is spoken in the languages the team speaks. The readiness block is also on the OS surface, so a person who arrives late reads what was said.

Who runs the brief is the owner of the room for that period, and which seat that is, the floor manager or the Maitre d, is an open question in the record (`86akh5u9g`). The function is named here; the seat is held.

**P10 (Recommended).** The shift brief carries a fixed spine in fixed order: the readiness block, the period's goal and measure, the one thing from the leads' review, recognitions, then team content. The readiness block is a checklist the rush cannot delete and is on the OS surface as well as spoken.

**P11 (Founder-gated).** The seat that runs the brief is the owner of the room for that period. The function is placed; whether the floor manager and the Maitre d are one seat or two, and which owns the room, is held (`86akh5u9g`).

### 3.2 The shift close

The close is "End of shift, reflect and strengthen the relationship" (WP p.26). The record has already allocated the server's close labor: every server writes a thank-you to every customer they served (WP p.26), and the cleaning that would compete for the window is the overnight crew's (WP p.26). The close also carries the capture: "the system captures the incident in the shift close, the appropriate lead reviews, the process that allowed the failure gets updated" (WP p.13). Two demands sit in one window, and section 14 records that as a finding. The cadence's answer is that the capture is the stack's job to make short, and the window's length is the schedule's job to protect.

The spine, every close, every open period:

1. The capture. Incidents, preferences learned, anything the system should know before the next server inherits the table. Captured on the OS surface, by the person who held the table, in less time than the thank-you note takes. A capture that takes longer than the note is a stack failure, not a server failure.
2. The thank-you notes (WP p.26). Server labor, already funded.
3. The lead's one-line read on the night, on the OS surface: how the room ran, and one line on thermal load, because cooling failure is the failure mode the record says no restaurant measures (WP p.13). One line, not a form.
4. The close checklist for the stations, owned by the inward strand. Content chef-gated for the kitchen.

The close does not carry feedback about a person. That goes to the check-in (section 4) or the feedback channel (WP p.22). The close does not carry a post-mortem. Post-mortems are private and process-focused (WP p.17) and are scheduled by the lead who reviews the capture, off the floor.

**P12 (Recommended).** The shift close carries a fixed spine: the capture, the thank-you notes, the lead's one-line read including one line on thermal load, and the station close checklist. The capture must take less of the window than the note. The close carries nothing about a person and no post-mortem.

**P13 (Chef-gated).** The kitchen's close checklist and the kitchen's readiness block at the brief. The form is set here; the content is the chef partner's.

### 3.3 One arc, person-triggered

Canon's temporal arc says the day is one arc and no period is a reset (Canon 1.0, section 02). The white paper says each period needs "its own reason to exist and its own operating logic" (WP p.33 to 34). Section 14 records the two accounts. For the daily cadence the reconciliation is exact: each open period has its own brief and its own close, because each has its own charter and its own goal page, and the close of one period hands to the brief of the next through the capture, so that the arc is continuous in the system even where the crew changes. The dinner-to-late-night transition is the room's, "same person, same moment. Not announced" (Canon 1.0, section 08), and the cadence does not schedule it. Where two periods are open with the same crew and no break between them, the second period's brief is a standing thirty-second insert at the transition's moment, not a second gathering, and it carries only the readiness block and the second period's goal sentence.

**P14 (Recommended).** Each open period has its own brief and close; the close of one period hands to the brief of the next through the capture; and where periods run back to back with the same crew, the second brief is a thirty-second readiness insert, never a second gathering. The transition itself is the room's and is not scheduled.

## 4\. The check-in: the shift-native form of her 1:1

This is the load-bearing member of her operating system and the carryover is exact about it (`86ajgnhv9`): the function is not optional, the form is. The alternative to a shift-native form is what almost every restaurant does, which is nothing.

### 4.1 Why her form does not transfer

Her 1:1 is on her list of cadences as "Biweekly 1:1s" (Book p.136). As the carryover describes it, it is weekly or biweekly, rarely rescheduled, with a shared agenda document, notes, action items, and quarterly goals tracked inside it. Each of those assumptions fails at Sŏn, and it is worth being precise about which.

The clock. Hourly staff are on the clock when the room is open and are paid for the shift. A meeting outside the shift is unpaid labor, and No Exploitation Ever (Canon 1.0, section 01) closes that door before the schedule does. A meeting inside the shift competes with service, and service wins every time.

The frequency. Weekly per person, for a lead who is on the floor every service and has one pre-service window per day, does not fit. The lead's week has as many pre-service windows as the lead has shifts, and every check-in takes one slice of one window. Weekly is unfundable at tempo. That is the Realist's read and it is decisive.

The document. A shared agenda document assumes a desk, a laptop, and an hour between meetings to open it. A server reads the team home on a phone between services, in whichever of the team's languages is theirs.

The unit. A quarter is a knowledge worker's planning unit. A restaurant person's native units are the shift, the week's schedule, the ninety-day plan (WP p.18), and the season. Turnover is the baseline in this labor market, and a quarterly cycle can outlast a tenure.

The relationship. Her 1:1 is between a manager and a report. At Sŏn "nothing is assigned, reviewed, or escalated by a person standing above another person" (ledger). The two people in the room are the person and the lead of their domain, and the lead is a systems function, not a rank.

### 4.2 The function, taken apart

Her 1:1 carries six functions. At Sŏn, four of them are already carried by systems the record has built, and naming that is what makes a shift-native form small enough to fund.

Advancement is carried by the invisible advocate: the next skill set unlocks, the assessment auto-schedules, the ready-now pool fills, and "No one's advancement waits on a manager's attention or judgment" (WP p.11, p.19). The check-in does not carry it.

The formal read is carried by the review conversation every three to six months (WP p.19). The check-in does not carry it, and it does not pre-empt it.

Upward feedback about the house is carried by the dedicated channel (WP p.22). The check-in is a second door to the same channel, not a replacement for it.

Recognition is carried by the peer platform (WP p.19) and read at the brief.

Two functions remain uncarried. The first is the standing, paid, two-way conversation between a person and the lead of their domain about how the person is actually doing and what is in their way. The record promises it under the ninety-day plan as "regular check-ins and course correction" (WP p.18) and does not place it. The second is the record of that conversation, in a form the person can read and the review can rely on, so that "a formal review never contains new information" (WP p.19). The no-surprises rule is only true if something between the reviews carries the information. Nothing in the record does yet. That is the gap the check-in fills, and it is why the record's own word for it is used.

### 4.3 The check-in

The check-in is a fifteen-minute conversation between a person and the lead of their domain, on the clock, in the pre-service window, placed by the stack's schedule.

On the clock, by construction. The stack schedules the person's shift to begin fifteen minutes before the crew call on the day of their check-in. The check-in is the first fifteen minutes of a scheduled shift. It is paid because it is shift. Nothing happens off the clock. A check-in that is not on the schedule did not happen, and the readiness row in section 12 checks the schedule, not the lead's recollection.

In the pre-service window, never mid-service, never at the close. The pre-service window is the highest-leverage time a restaurant has and it is already funded. The close is not available: server labor at the close is already allocated to the thank-you note (WP p.26), and a check-in placed there takes it from the customer. The check-in ends before the shift brief begins.

Placed by the stack, not the lead. The schedule carries a rotation. When a person's interval elapses, the stack places their check-in on their next scheduled shift that the lead also works. The lead does not remember to call it. The person sees it on their schedule the way they see the shift. This is stability as a property of the system, and it is the difference between a check-in and a good intention.

With the lead of their domain. The Maitre d owns "Team development, training, and the invisible advocate function" for the floor (WP p.10), so the floor's check-ins are the Maitre d's. The kitchen's are chef-gated. Positions the record assigns to neither, the inward-facing roles the Operations Lead's domain touches, are team-filled once the roster exists.

The spine, spoken, the same three questions every time, in the person's language:

1. What is working.
2. What is in the way. The lead hears this as a system question, per the record: "the first question is not who failed. It is what allowed this to happen" (WP p.17).
3. What changed: the person's one goal, their progression state as the stack shows it, their schedule needs, anything they want the lead to know.

Then the person's read on the lead and the house, because "Team members weigh in on management effectiveness, operational improvements, and culture" (WP p.19) and the check-in is where that is asked rather than waited for. Then one line written on the person page, read back aloud, and the next date, which the stack has already set.

What the check-in refuses. It is not a verdict; the review is the review, and the review is not a verdict either (WP p.19). It is not a scheduling negotiation; the schedule is the stack's. It is not task assignment; the ledger forbids the form. It is not a pay conversation; compensation architecture is Session 13's. It is not discipline; that is Session 14 and 15. It is not a post-mortem. A check-in that becomes any of those has left its range, and the range is stated in section 4.6.

**P15 (Recommended).** Sŏn replaces her 1:1 with the check-in: fifteen minutes, on the clock as the first fifteen minutes of a scheduled shift, in the pre-service window, placed on the schedule by the stack when the person's interval elapses, between the person and the lead of their domain, with a fixed three-question spine, a fourth upward question, one written line read back, and the next date.

**P16 (Chef-gated).** The kitchen's check-in holder and any change to the spine for the inward strand.

**P17 (Team-filled).** The check-in holder for positions the record assigns to neither the Maitre d nor the chef partner, filled once the roster exists.

### 4.4 The person page

What replaces the shared 1:1 document for a person who does not open documents is not a document. It is a card on the team home, readable on a phone between services, in the person's language, and it holds five things:

1. The person's one goal, in one sentence, and its state (section 7).
2. The person's progression state, written by the stack from the invisible advocate, not by the lead.
3. The last three check-in lines, dated.
4. The next check-in date.
5. The person's ninety-day plan while they are on one (WP p.18), with its check-ins marked.

The person page is written in the room, at the check-in, by the lead, and read back before it is saved. It is the person's, and the person can read it at any time. It is the evidence base the review reads, which is what makes "no surprises" a mechanism rather than a wish: the review opens on the person page, and nothing that is not on it is new.

The lead does not write on the person page between check-ins. Anything the lead wants to say between check-ins is said in person and written at the next one, or goes through the feedback channel with the person's knowledge. A person page that accumulates notes the person has not heard is her shared document with the sharing removed.

**P18 (Recommended).** The person page on the team home replaces the shared 1:1 document: one goal and its state, the progression state from the stack, the last three check-in lines, the next date, and the ninety-day plan while active. Written only at the check-in, read back before saving, readable by the person at any time, and the evidence base the review opens on.

### 4.5 The rhythm

The carryover asks whether quarterly is the right unit, or whether the daypart and the season are the native cycles. The answer is that the check-in has two rhythms and neither is quarterly.

Inside the ninety-day plan: every two weeks. The record promises "regular check-ins" on the plan (WP p.18), and a person's first ninety days at Sŏn are the interval in which the stack's own progression is not yet the advocate, because the person has not yet unlocked anything. Six check-ins across ninety days, on the schedule, is what "planned rather than hoped for" (WP p.18) means in practice.

After the ninety-day plan: monthly. The argument is a squeeze from both sides. Weekly is unfundable at tempo (section 4.1). Quarterly fails no-surprises, because a review every three months with a check-in every three months is a review with nothing between it and the last one. Monthly is the longest interval at which the review every three to six months (WP p.19) can be true to its own rule, and the shortest a lead's pre-service windows can carry for a full roster. The season is not the check-in's unit; the season is the house's unit, and the check-in is the person's. The daypart is not its unit either; a person's check-in is with the lead of their domain, whichever period they work.

The frequency is a parameter. It is set here at two weeks and one month, and it is reset at the mechanism reset (section 6.6) against the check-in's own record: whether the rotation fit the lead's windows, and whether the reviews contained anything new.

The Realist's arithmetic, without figures. The monthly rotation fits only if the number of people whose check-ins the Maitre d holds is not larger than the number of pre-service windows the Maitre d works in a month. The record does not give the count and this program does not carry it. If the roster exceeds the windows, the interval stretches, and the stack reports the stretch on the leads' review agenda as a load reading on the lead. That is a node-overload signal, and the fix is structural, not a longer interval quietly accepted.

**P19 (Recommended).** The check-in runs every two weeks inside the ninety-day plan and monthly after it, on neither the quarter nor the season. Both intervals are parameters reset at the mechanism reset. If the roster exceeds the lead's monthly windows, the stretch is reported at the leads' review as a load reading on the lead, not absorbed.

### 4.6 The decision-rights entry

Written in the ledger's form: owner, range stated as may and may not, triggers with named destinations.

Owner: the lead who holds the person's check-in.

Range. The lead may move a check-in to the person's next scheduled shift that the lead also works, when the pre-service window is short-crewed or the brief needs the whole window. The lead may extend a check-in past fifteen minutes when the pre-service window has the slack, at the lead's own read. The lead may not cancel a check-in; a moved check-in keeps its place in the rotation. The lead may not run a check-in during service or at the close. The lead may not write on the person page outside a check-in. The lead may not carry a check-in's content to anyone but the person, except through the review conversation the person is part of, or through a post-mortem that names a process and not a person.

Triggers. A check-in moved twice goes to the leads' review agenda as a standing line, read as a load reading on the lead's windows, not on the person. A check-in moved three times goes to the house review as a node-overload entry (WP p.14). A check-in that surfaces a non-negotiable breach goes to the Maitre d and the Operations Lead together that day, and the destination for what follows is Session 14 and 15's to write.

**P20 (Recommended).** The check-in's decision-rights entry as stated: the holding lead owns it; the range permits moving and extending and forbids cancelling, running mid-service or at the close, writing outside the check-in, or carrying content elsewhere; and the triggers name the leads' review at two moves, the house review at three, and both leads on a non-negotiable breach.

### 4.7 The tempo test and the cost

The worst realistic night: a Friday, a full book, a green runner, a station short. The check-in falls on this day for one person. The lead reads the pre-service window and moves it, in range. The stack re-places it on the person's next shift with the lead on. The brief runs its readiness block. The person is paid for the fifteen minutes they were scheduled early, because the schedule was the schedule. The moved check-in is logged. Nothing broke. The design survives the night because the thing the night took was the thing the range let it take.

The cost, per person, is fifteen minutes of scheduled labor per month, and per lead a slice of most pre-service windows. The buy is the no-surprises review, the earliest warning the record says comes "from the people doing the work" (WP p.19), and a person page that keeps the person's goal alive between the brief and the review. Of all her mechanisms this is the cheapest and it is the one restaurants skip. That is the argument for it, and it is also the reason it has to be on the schedule: an unfunded fifteen minutes is the first thing a Friday deletes.

**P21 (Recommended).** The check-in survives the worst realistic night because its range lets the night move it and its rotation refuses to let the night delete it. Its bandwidth cost is fifteen scheduled minutes per person per month plus one slice of the holding lead's pre-service window per check-in.

## 5\. The week

### 5.1 The leads' review

Session 3 placed it: weekly, cross-period, opening on the leading indicators. This section gives it a day, an hour, a room, an attendance, and an agenda.

The day and the hour. The leads' review sits in a building that is either about to open or has just closed, and never inside service hours. Pre-opening and while dinner runs alone, it sits on the morning of the week's first service day, and it ends before that day's shift brief begins, with the pre-service scene window (Canon 1.0, section 06) untouched. Once the early morning is open, that morning no longer exists, and the review moves to the interval between the early morning's close and dinner's pre-service window, on the lowest-tempo day of the week. Which day that is comes from the reservation record once there is one. Whether the house has a closed day, which would be the natural home for the review, is not in the record extracts read for this session, and it is held.

The hard stop. The review has an end time and it holds. A leads' review that runs into the pre-service window takes the brief's time, and the brief is the mechanism the record says cannot be skipped (WP p.14). The Realist's rule: the review ends before the brief begins, every week, and a review that cannot fit its agenda shortens the agenda rather than the brief.

Attendance. The Operations Lead and the Maitre d, as co-leads of parallel systems (WP p.10). The chef partner, or the chef's delegate for execution, for the kitchen's read; that seat's presence is chef-gated. The founders do not own the review and do not attend by default. "The mark of these founders is a company built to run without them" (WP p.06), and the leads' review is the first place that is tested. The founders read the review's record. A founder attends by standing invitation for a stated reason, not by right.

Ownership. Her rule is one owner per structure (Book p.139). The leads' review has two co-leads and no one above them. The team's position is that the review is chaired in alternation, one lead one week and the other the next, and that the chair's only added duty is the clock and the record. Alternation is the form that keeps the parallel structure parallel; a permanent chair is the first step back toward a general manager, which the record names as the load-bearing model's last failure mode.

**P22 (Recommended).** The leads' review runs weekly, on the morning of the week's first service day while dinner runs alone and between the early morning's close and dinner's pre-service window once the early morning is open, on the week's lowest-tempo day, with a hard stop before the shift brief. It is attended by the two leads and the kitchen's seat, chaired in alternation, and read but not attended by the founders except by standing invitation.

**P23 (Founder-gated).** Whether the house has a closed day, and if so whether the leads' review and the house review sit on it. The team recommends that if a closed day exists, both reviews sit on it, because a review at a closed hour costs no service. The record is silent and the partners close this.

**P24 (Chef-gated).** The kitchen's seat at the leads' review: the chef partner, a delegate, or a written read.

**P25 (Team-filled).** The lowest-tempo day of the week, from the reservation record, once the early morning is open.

### 5.2 The agenda

The agenda is fixed, in order, and metrics are a standing topic (Book p.158). Every item names what it reads and where the reading comes from.

1. The leading indicators, from the stack, read first and read every week. The customer recognition rate for the week (WP p.22). The employee NPS pulse in the week it is taken (section 6.1). The cultural labor score when its definition and rhythm exist; until then, the line is present and empty, so that the gap is visible weekly (`86akh5u9x`). The number of shift-close captures and the number of process updates they produced, because "the process that allowed the failure gets updated" (WP p.13) is a rate the leads can read.
2. The week's captures. What the system learned. Which process was updated. Which capture is waiting on a lead's review past seven days. This is the record's learning loop, read weekly so that it does not silently stop.
3. The goal pages. Any change of state on any unit's page since last week: green to yellow, yellow to red, and back. Not a tour of every page. State changes only.
4. The interface between service periods, once more than one is open. What the closing period handed to the opening one, and what broke at the handoff. This is the field the daypart charter carries as provided and dependent interfaces (section 11.2), read weekly, because the record's bus failure is an interface failure.
5. The feedback channel. Items open past the loop-closure window (section 6.1). Each with its owner.
6. Load readings. Check-ins moved twice (section 4.6). The lead's thermal-load lines from the closes, read as a week (section 3.2). Any node-overload signal.
7. Decisions in the leads' range, taken and recorded. Escalations, each with its named destination.
8. The one thing. The single sentence each lead will carry into every open period's brief this week. The same sentence at every brief. This is the spoken channel, and it is written on the team home before the first brief.

Notes are taken by the chair on the team home, with action items and owners (Book p.158), where an owner is a lead, a domain, or a unit, never a person below another. The record is readable by the whole house, in the team's languages, subject to the transparency line, which is open (`86akh5uby`).

The lagging indicators, labor, cost, and revenue, are not on this agenda. They are the Operations Lead's domain (WP p.10) and the X-ray after the fracture (WP p.12); they are read at the house review, once, after the leading indicators, and this program carries no figure for them.

**P26 (Recommended).** The leads' review agenda is fixed in eight items: leading indicators, the week's captures, goal-page state changes, the inter-period interface, open feedback items, load readings, decisions and escalations with destinations, and the one thing for the week's briefs. Lagging indicators are not on it.

### 5.3 Presence, in a building about to open

Her "be present" rule is "minimize work distractions" and "snoozing Slack notifications" (Book p.157). The Sŏn form is sharper because the building is either about to open or has just closed, and the distractions are not notifications; they are the delivery at the back door and the reservation book changing. The rule is: no service radio in the room; no stack surface open except the agenda's reads; the review's room is not the floor; and the hard stop holds. The chair's clock is the only clock in the room. When the review ends, the leads walk to the brief, and the review's one thing becomes the brief's third line.

**P27 (Recommended).** Presence at the leads' review means no service radio, no stack surface beyond the agenda's reads, a room that is not the floor, and a hard stop the chair holds. The review's one thing is the brief's third line that day.

### 5.4 The partners' review

The partners' review is the founder cadence's weekly member, and section 9 holds its full design. Its placement on the week is stated here so the week is whole. Pre-opening, the partners' review is the build's working meeting, weekly, at a time the partners set. In service it is weekly, at a closed hour, outside service hours by the rule in section 2.7, and it reads the leads' review's record rather than duplicating it. The partners do not attend the leads' review to learn what happened; they read it.

**P28 (Recommended).** The partners' review is weekly on both rhythms; in service it sits at a closed hour outside service hours and reads the leads' review's record.

## 6\. The month, the quarter, the season, the year

### 6.1 The monthly reads

Three things run monthly, and none of them is a meeting.

The check-in (section 4).

The employee NPS pulse. The record sets the question and the direction: "would you recommend working here, tracked regularly rather than annually" (WP p.22). This page sets the rhythm: monthly, one question, answered on the OS surface off the floor and never on paper at tempo, anonymous. The pulse is read at the leads' review in the week it is taken and as a quarterly composite at the house review. Monthly is the frequency rule applied: the indicator can move within a month, so it is read within a month. Weekly would over-sample a question whose answer does not change weekly and would train the team to ignore it.

The partners' domain read (section 9.4): each partner reads the other's domain page and writes questions. Monthly, asynchronous.

The loop-closure window. "Feedback without follow-through breeds cynicism. The loop gets closed" (WP p.22). The record sets the rule and not the window. The window is: every item in the feedback channel is acknowledged, with an owner, by the next leads' review, which means within seven days; every item is resolved, scheduled with a date, or declined with a written reason on the team home within thirty days; any item open past thirty days is a standing line at the house review with its owner named. The window is a parameter and the mechanism reset revisits it.

**P29 (Recommended).** The employee NPS pulse runs monthly, one question, on the OS surface, off the floor, anonymous; read at the leads' review in the week taken and as a composite at the house review.

**P30 (Recommended).** The feedback loop-closure window is seven days to acknowledge with an owner and thirty days to resolve, schedule with a date, or decline with a written reason; anything older is a standing house review line.

**P31 (Founder-gated).** The rhythm and population of the cultural labor score. The record has one line (WP p.22) and Brandon originated the concept (`86akh5u9x`). The team's recommendation for the rhythm, once the question exists: monthly, alongside the pulse, because it measures the same population at the same frequency the record wants for the pulse.

### 6.2 The house review, procedurally

Session 3 translated the QBR into Sŏn's quarterly house review and set the five failure modes (WP p.14) as its standing scorecard. That translation stands. What this section supplies is the procedure, tested against a team that does not sit at desks.

Before. Each lead writes a memo for their domain; the kitchen's memo is chef-gated; the center integrates the memos into one house memo. Her rule is "Share the memo at least 24 hours in advance" (Book p.157). At Sŏn the memo is shared on the team home twenty-four hours ahead, to the partners and the leads, who read documents. The house does not receive the memo; the house receives the review's outcome through the three channels afterward. Her "we advise against linking out to supplementary materials" (Book p.157) survives whole, with a Sŏn reason added: the memo is the record, links rot, and a memo that needs a link is not finished. Her length rule is a six-page narrative (Book p.157). At Sŏn the length rule is stated as reading time instead of pages: the memo must be readable in the review's fixed twenty-minute reading block, and a memo that is not is shortened by its author before the review, which is her rule for the QBR leader (Book p.158) applied to the writer.

The memo's sections, from her outline (Book p.158 to 165), kept, replaced, or dropped:

*   Executive summary, "a candid assessment, not a summary of your successes" (Book p.158). Kept, as the first paragraph.
*   Narrative, "skewed toward lowlights" if goals were missed (Book p.159). Kept, in the Sŏn form: the quarter's goal-page results per unit, with the bandwidth flag on any goal met at a bandwidth cost.
*   Metrics performance (Book p.160). Replaced by the leading indicators over the quarter, then the lagging indicators once, as the X-ray. No figure is carried in this program.
*   Product P&L, operating expenses, headcount (Book p.161, p.163 to 164). Dropped. Financials are not a context source for this program. The Operations Lead's domain reads its own lagging indicators on its own surface, and whether any of that enters the house memo is the transparency line, held (`86akh5uby`).
*   Cross-functional focus areas with scores (Book p.162). Replaced by the five failure modes scorecard Session 3 set: tension slack, node overload, sequence corruption, skill invisibility, cooling failure (WP p.14), each with a one-line read and the evidence line from the quarter's captures and closes.
*   Progress on goals table (Book p.162 to 163). Kept, as the goal-page state table across the four units.
*   Prior QBR action items (Book p.163). Kept, and moved to the front: the review opens on the prior review's action items.
*   Top asks from users (Book p.164). Replaced by "Customer feedback about the team's experience" (WP p.23), which the record names as the qualitative side most operators never gather.
*   Team morale (Book p.160). Replaced by the employee NPS composite and the exit interviews read "for what could have been better" (WP p.23).
*   Additional supporting materials (Book p.165). Dropped. No links, no appendix.

During. The review sits at a closed hour, never inside service, and its presence rule is section 5.3's. The order: reading time, twenty minutes, announced closed (Book p.158). Top-of-mind topics gathered from the room and added to the agenda (Book p.158), prioritized by the chair. Prior action items. The leading indicators as the standing metrics topic. The five failure modes. The goal-page table. The customer and team qualitative reads. The gate review section for any gate still open (section 8.1). The next quarter's goal pages ratified. Notes with action items and owners (Book p.158), where an owner is a lead, a domain, or a unit. The chair is the center: the partners, who "integrate and name the commitments" (ledger), and whose in-service role is section 9.4's.

After. The notes are on the team home within twenty-four hours, in the team's languages, subject to the transparency line. Action items go into the tracker on the stack; which tool is the hub of record is open (`86akh5u6e`), and this page names the function and not the tool. The review's one thing reaches every open period's brief the next week through the leads' review. Her closing discipline, "Reflect on the quality of the QBR, including the quality of the metrics" (Book p.158), survives as one written paragraph appended to the record within the week, read aloud at the next review's opening. That paragraph is the quarterly light touch on the cadence itself, and it is what keeps the annual mechanism reset from being a surprise.

**P32 (Recommended).** The house review's procedure: memos per domain integrated by the center, shared twenty-four hours ahead to partners and leads, no links, bounded by a twenty-minute reading block; during, reading time announced closed, top-of-mind topics gathered, prior action items first, leading indicators as the standing topic, the five failure modes scorecard, the goal-page table, the qualitative reads, the gate section, next quarter's pages ratified, notes with owners; after, notes on the team home within twenty-four hours, action items to the tracker, and one reflection paragraph appended and read at the next opening.

### 6.3 Mid-period scoring and the change record

Session 3 adopted her mid-period scoring, three-state rubric, and change rules (Book p.155 to 156) and routed the placement here.

When. On the pre-opening rhythm, mid-quarter scoring happens at the partners' review closest to the midpoint between two gate reviews, and that review is designated the scoring week on the calendar in advance. On the in-service rhythm, it happens at the leads' review in the quarter's seventh week, designated the same way, so the score is on the page before the house review's memo is written. End-period scoring happens at the house review itself, and pre-opening at the gate review.

Who. The unit that holds the goal scores it. The person scores the person page at their check-in in the scoring month; the service period's page is scored by the owner of the room (seat held); the domain's page by its lead; the company's page by the center. The leads' review records the scores; it does not assign them. Nothing is scored by a person standing above another.

What. Each goal's state: on track, at risk, off track (Book p.155). Plus the bandwidth flag: a goal on track at a bandwidth cost is scored as a system failure "at the time, not reflected on afterward" (ledger), and the flag is a fourth state the page carries. Items at risk or off track are discussed at the scoring review "so that the team can find ways to redirect their energy and build in time to recover" (Book p.155).

The change record. Her rules survive whole: adding or dropping a measure is documented and impacted units notified; adding a goal documents what was deprioritized; dropping a goal is rare and the goal stays on the page for scoring (Book p.155 to 156). The Sŏn form: the change is written on the goal page as a versioned decision, dated, with the reason and what it displaced, per "A living document changes by versioned decision, never by drift" (Deck 3.0, p.94). Notification runs through the three channels. A goal that changed without a versioned entry has drifted, and drift is the failure the deck names.

**P35 (Recommended).** Mid-period scoring runs at the designated partners' review mid-way between gate reviews pre-opening and at the seventh-week leads' review in service; end-period scoring at the gate review and the house review. The holding unit scores; the review records. The states are on track, at risk, off track, and the bandwidth flag. Every change is a dated, versioned entry on the goal page with its reason and what it displaced, communicated through the three channels.

### 6.4 The season

The season is the house's planning unit in the sense her quarter is a planning unit: it is when the room changes. Canon lists the menu design system's requirements as "course structure, tasting menu versus à la carte, change cadence, number of items at service" (Canon 1.0, "What Remains"). The change cadence is chef-gated and canon says so.

What the cadence can place around the season without setting it: a menu turn is a change to the room, and by the three channels it reaches every open period's brief, the team home, and the domain lead or mentor before the first service that carries it. A menu turn that reaches dinner's brief and not the early morning's has not been communicated. The turn's training window is the Realist's concern: every new item is a tempo tax, and the pre-service windows before a turn carry the training, so the turn is placed on the calendar far enough ahead of its first service that the windows exist. How far is chef-gated and team-filled from the first turn's record.

Whether the season and the quarter align is the second question. The team's position is that they do not need to and should not be forced to; the house review reads the season's results whenever the season falls, and a turn mid-quarter is scored at the next house review with the turn date on the page.

**P36 (Chef-gated).** The menu change cadence, its length, and its training window.

**P37 (Recommended).** A menu turn is communicated through the three channels before its first service; its training sits in the pre-service windows ahead of it; and the season is not aligned to the quarter by force. The house review reads the season whenever it falls.

### 6.6 The year: the mechanism reset and the principles revision

Her rule is "Revisit your processes every six months or so" (Book p.141). Sŏn's answer runs through Jaeyeonmi: a maintenance philosophy that "distinguishes care from correction" (Canon 1.0, section 01). Care is continuous: the shift close updates the process that allowed a failure, every night (WP p.13), and the house review appends a reflection on itself every quarter (section 6.2). Correction is the mechanism reset, and it touches the cadence's own rhythms: the check-in's intervals, the loop-closure window, the leads' review's day, the brief's spine, the counts on the goal pages, the pulse's frequency. Her worry that a six-month interval is the balance between destabilizing and autopilot (Book p.141) is answered by the split: the autopilot risk is carried by daily care, and the destabilizing risk is carried by the reset's rarity. A cadence that changes its rhythms more than once a year is not a cadence the crew can learn.

The reset runs twice in the first year and annually after. The first is after dinner has run alone for its first quarter, which the record sets as the phased opening's first gate (WP p.36), and it is the moment the section 2.7 freeze lifts. The second is after the first full year, the record's own milestone for revisiting the plan (WP p.36). Thereafter, annually, at the house review closest to the anniversary of dinner's opening.

The reset's method is hers: state how long the mechanism was tried, when it was checked, and the criteria (Book p.142), applied in reverse, against the record each mechanism has produced. Each mechanism on the map is read against its own cost and buy (section 10.3), and one of three things happens: it continues, it changes by versioned decision, or it ends. Her warning signs (Book p.142 to 143) are the reset's checklist, with one Sŏn addition: a mechanism no one would miss is Ma's argument to end it.

The principles revision sits with the reset. Carryover `86akh3unw` is answered in section 11.1: version one is pre-opening and drawn from the record's decisions; the mandatory revision is at the same two points, after dinner's first quarter and after the first full year, and at each the principles are tested against moments the company has actually had, read from the captures and the post-mortems.

**P39 (Recommended).** The mechanism reset runs after dinner's first quarter, after the first full year, and annually thereafter at the house review nearest dinner's anniversary. ## 7\. The goal cycle

Session 3 built the metric layer, the definitions register, the four goal-bearing units, and the rules on committed versus aspirational and goals before a baseline. This section supplies the form, the counts, the calendar, and the name, and it tests her attainment numbers.

### 7.1 The name

Does Sŏn use "OKR"? No. The vocabulary rule is that a word carries its apparatus. "OKR" carries the quarter as the unit, three to five objectives per team, the cascade she describes as "Objectives nest up and down" (Book p.154), percentage attainment, and a document whose title changes from "Draft" to "On track" (Book p.153). Sŏn's goals ladder up from the unit that holds the information and never replicate down (ledger); the quarter is a read, not the unit; and attainment is not a percentage (section 7.4). A team that says "OKR" will draw her apparatus with it, and canon's lexicon has one voice. Sŏn's words are goal and measure. A goal is a state of the world at the end of a period. A measure is what tells you whether the state holds.

**P40 (Recommended).** Sŏn does not use the term "OKR." The goal page carries goals and measures. A goal is a state of the world; a measure is what shows the state holds.

### 7.2 The form and the counts

Her counts are three to five objectives per unit and one to five key results each (Book p.153). Her units are teams of knowledge workers with the quarter to work in. Sŏn's units are a company, a service period, a domain, and a person, and three of the four are run by people at tempo. The counts are set smaller and the argument is bandwidth.

The company page: at most three goals. The service period page: at most three. The domain page: at most three. The person page: one. Each goal carries one to three measures. The person's one goal is the thing they can say at the brief without reading it, and a person with three goals has none.

The page's fields per goal: the goal as a state; its measures, each either binary or relative to a named baseline; the long-term commitment it ladders to (`86akh3uhy`; the commitments are held, `86akh3tg0`, and the field is present and points at a placeholder until they land); its class, committed or aspirational, by the ledger's rule; its state, one of the four in section 6.3; the mechanism by which the measure moves the goal (section 7.5); its owner, which is the unit; and its change log as versioned entries.

Her "Publish: change 'Draft' to 'On track' in the document title" (Book p.153) becomes a state field on the page, and a page with no state is a draft. Her "Collaborate: get feedback and make sure you agree on the OKRs you've set" with partner teams (Book p.153) survives as the interface read: a service period's page is read by the adjacent period's owner before it is ratified, because the interface between periods is where the record's bus failure lives.

**P41 (Recommended).** The counts are at most three goals for the company, a service period, and a domain, one for a person, each with one to three measures. The goal page's fields are the state, the measures with baseline or binary form, the commitment it ladders to, the class, the current state, the causal mechanism, the owning unit, and the versioned change log. An adjacent period's owner reads a period's page before ratification.

### 7.3 The calendar on each rhythm

Pre-opening. The goal pages are the readiness rows (ledger: "Pre-opening goals are the readiness rows"). At each quarterly gate review, the rows that do not yet hold become the next quarter's page for the unit that owns them. There is no separate pre-opening goal-writing exercise; the readiness test is the goal cycle until dinner opens.

Dinner's first quarter. Every unit's page carries binary states and baselining goals only. A baselining goal reads: by the end of the quarter, this measure has a baseline, defined, sourced, calculated, and owned per the definitions register. No target on a rate. This is the ledger's rule and it is also the section 2.7 protection.

From the second quarter. Pages are written in the two weeks before the quarter, ratified at the house review, with targets relative to the baseline the first quarter produced. The person page's goal is set at the person's check-in in that window, by the person with the lead, not by the lead for the person. The service period's page is set by the owner of the room with the leads. The domain's by its lead. The company's by the center. Ratification is the house review's last agenda item (section 6.2).

Per arrival. A person who arrives mid-quarter has a ninety-day plan (WP p.18) and a person page from day one, and their first goal is the plan's, not the quarter's.

**P42 (Recommended).** The goal calendar: pre-opening, the readiness rows are the pages and failed rows roll to the next quarter's page; dinner's first quarter, states and baselining goals only; from the second quarter, pages written in the two weeks before the quarter and ratified at the house review, with the person page set at the check-in; per arrival, the ninety-day plan is the first page.

### 7.4 Attainment expectations

Her numbers: an expected score "in the 70 to 80 percent range over the quarter," must-hits at 20 to 30 percent of a team's goals, with "95 percent attainment or more" on them (Book p.153). Do they survive?

They do not, for three reasons, and the reasons are the record's rather than the team's taste.

There is no baseline. A percentage attainment expectation is a target on a rate, and the ledger forbids a target on a rate before a baseline exists. In the first year there is nothing to score a percentage against.

The record forbids asserting the unproven. An expected attainment rate is a claim about how the house will perform, made before it has performed. It is a forecast wearing the costume of a standard.

The committed class already does the must-hit's work, and does it better. A committed goal "gates an opening, protects a non-negotiable or a food-safety condition, or has been stated to customers through the documented build" (ledger). Those are binary conditions. They hold or they do not, and "95 percent attainment" on a food-safety condition is a sentence that should not be written. Her one surviving line here is the priority rule: "If these goals are trending behind, we expect to sacrifice other goals in service of hitting them" (Book p.153). At Sŏn a committed goal at risk displaces aspirational goals, and the displacement is a versioned entry on the pages it touches.

What replaces the numbers is the three-state rubric and the bandwidth flag, read at mid-period and end-period, and from the second year a house-level read of how many aspirational goals reached their state, which the mechanism reset uses to calibrate the counts. That read is a baseline, not a target.

**P43 (Recommended).** Her percentage attainment expectations do not survive. Committed goals are binary conditions and hold or do not; a committed goal at risk displaces aspirational goals by versioned entry; aspirational goals are scored by the three-state rubric and the bandwidth flag; and from the second year the proportion of aspirational goals reaching their state is read as a baseline for the mechanism reset, never set as a target.

### 7.5 The causal mechanism

"Key results flow from a theory of a plausible causal mechanism that connects the key result with the objective and with your own work" (Book p.154). This is the strongest line in her section and it is already Sŏn's constraint. The record's leading indicators are leading because they sit upstream of the outcome on a causal path the record names: bandwidth, then joy, then retention, then results (WP p.12). A measure with no stated mechanism is a lagging indicator wearing a leading costume, and the metric gate (section 7.7) refuses it.

The Sŏn form: every measure on a goal page carries one sentence stating how moving the measure moves the goal, and what the unit does that moves the measure. A measure whose sentence cannot be written is not a measure of that goal.

**P44 (Recommended).** Every measure on a goal page carries a one-sentence causal mechanism: how the measure moves the goal, and what the unit does that moves the measure. A measure without one does not enter the page.

### 7.6 Her pitfalls, row by row

"Using an activity or action as an outcome" (Book p.154). Survives, with a Sŏn precision: a pre-opening goal is a state ("the dinner charter is ratified"), not an activity ("draft the dinner charter"), and the readiness rows are already written as states.

"Having a key result that can't be measured in some way" (Book p.154). Survives. A binary state counts as measurable; a baselining goal counts as measurable because its measure is the existence of the baseline.

"Turning a roadmap directly into objectives and key results" (Book p.155). Survives, and it has a Sŏn name: the build plan is not the goal page. The gate is. A build task list on a goal page is the pre-opening version of this pitfall.

Writing more than three to five objectives, or one to five key results per objective (Book p.155). Replaced by Sŏn's smaller counts (section 7.2).

Two rows added. A goal met at a bandwidth cost scored as met: the ledger names it a system failure and the page carries the flag. A goal that replicates down from another unit's page: the ledger forbids replication; a goal is written once, by the unit that holds the information, and other units reference it.

**P45 (Recommended).** Her four pitfalls survive with the count row replaced by Sŏn's counts, and two rows are added: a goal met at a bandwidth cost scored as met, and a goal replicated down from another unit's page.

### 7.7 The metric gate

Session 3 built the definitions register: definition, source, calculation, owner, counterweight, mechanism. Her Objectives and Metrics Checklist (Book p.151 to 152) becomes the gate a proposed measure passes before it enters that register. Her rows, kept, changed, or dropped:

*   "Inspirational?" (Book p.151). Dropped as a gate row. Inspiration is a property of the commitment the goal ladders to, not of the measure. Replaced by: ladders to a long-term commitment (`86akh3uhy`).
*   "Actionable by your team?" (Book p.151). Kept, in the ledger's form: the unit that holds the information holds the measure.
*   "Related to your team's mission and vision?" (Book p.151). Changed: a unit at Sŏn has a charter's reason to exist, not a mission; the row reads "consistent with the unit's charter." The company's mission sentence is held (`86akh3tdr`).
*   "Related to the company's priorities and goals?" (Book p.152). Merged into the commitment row.
*   SMART (Book p.152). Specific and Measurable kept. Achievable dropped before a baseline exists and replaced by "has a baseline, or is a baselining goal." Results-oriented kept as "a state, not an activity." Targeted replaced by "relative to the baseline from the second quarter; binary before."
*   "Is the metric a leading indicator?" (Book p.152). Not a check at Sŏn. It is the constraint. The record has already rejected the conventional measures as the primary read: "Revenue, labor percentage, food cost: these exist because they have always existed. All lagging indicators. By the time they signal a problem, the damage is done" (WP p.22). The gate's form: a proposed lagging measure argues for itself against the record, in writing, on the register, and enters only as a counterweight or as the X-ray, never as a goal's measure.
*   "Does it have counterweight metrics?" (Book p.152). Kept and made the hardest row. Every measure names its counterweight, and the counterweight is always at least bandwidth: what this measure could be moved at the cost of. This is the row that protects against the failure the ledger names, a goal met at a bandwidth cost.
*   "Is there a framework to evaluate the metric?" (Book p.152). Kept as the register's definition, source, and calculation.
*   "Does the metric have an owner?" (Book p.152). Kept; the owner is the unit.
*   "Is the metric relative, not absolute?" (Book p.152). Kept from the second quarter; binary before a baseline.
*   "Does it take into account your existing user growth?" (Book p.152). Dropped as written; there is no growth baseline and the house does not compound by headcount. Replaced by the causal mechanism row (section 7.5): the measure moves for a reason the unit caused.

Three Sŏn rows added. The playground row: the measure does not "measure procedure execution without building toward judgment" (Canon 1.0, section 01). The tempo row, the Realist's: the measure is captured by the stack at tempo without a person watching a screen mid-service, or it is captured at the brief or the close, and never by a cook or server pausing to record it. The figure row: no target value is carried before a baseline, and no financial figure is carried in this program at all.

**P46 (Recommended).** The metric gate, in order: ladders to a commitment; held by the unit with the information; consistent with the unit's charter; specific; measurable; has a baseline or is baselining; a state not an activity; relative to baseline from the second quarter; leading, or argued in writing as a counterweight or X-ray only; names its counterweight, always including bandwidth; has definition, source, and calculation; owned by the unit; carries a causal mechanism; passes the playground test; is capturable at tempo without a person watching a screen; carries no target before a baseline and no financial figure.

## 8\. The gates and the daypart charters

### 8.1 Gates, not dates

The ledger's rule is the most important constraint on the pre-opening rhythm and it is repeated here in full: "Dates are not gates. A calendar quarter is the cadence on which gates are reviewed, not the thing that opens them." The gates are pre-opening; dinner alone; dinner steady, early morning added; late night added; the first full year; the lunch ruling.

The gate review is the quarterly meeting at which the readiness test is run against the next gate. Pre-opening it is its own meeting, the pre-opening rhythm's quarterly member. In service it is a standing section of the house review, because the people are the same and a second quarterly meeting fails Ma. A gate opens when every row holds at the review. A gate whose rows do not all hold does not open, and its failed rows are the next quarter's goal page for the units that own them (section 7.3).

Two refinements. First, the review is the check. A row is not met by a report that it is met; the checker named in the table checks it at the review. Second, an off-cycle gate review may be called when every checker has reported every row met, by both partners together (three, once the chef seat is filled). The off-cycle review runs the same test with the same checkers. What it does not do is shorten the record's minimum durations: "runs on its own for at least a quarter" (WP p.36) is a duration the record set, and a gate review cannot open the early morning before it has passed. Section 14 records the duration-and-gate double account.

What "dinner is steady" means, as the gate that opens the early morning, is open (`86akh5uaw`). This page places the review that will apply the definition and does not supply the definition.

**P47 (Recommended).** The gate review runs quarterly on the calendar quarter, as its own meeting pre-opening and as a section of the house review in service. A gate opens only when every readiness row holds at the review, checked by the named checker. Failed rows become the next quarter's page. An off-cycle review may be called by all partners together when every row is reported met, and no review shortens a minimum duration the record set.

**P48 (Founder-gated).** The definition of "dinner is steady" (`86akh5uaw`). The team recommends it be written as readiness rows in the same form as section 12, so that the gate is a test rather than a feeling, and that the rows draw on the leading indicators having produced a baseline plus the check-in and leads' review having run a full quarter without a moved-three-times or node-overload entry.

### 8.2 The charter's placement ahead of each opening

Ruled at the next gate review. Row 34 in section 12 checks it.

## 9\. The founder cadence

### 9.1 What the record holds, and does not

The record holds a floor cadence, a review cadence, an onboarding cadence, and a brand cadence (section 1.2). It holds nothing for the center (`86akh2qwp`). Founders own non-overlapping domains: Brandon the concept, the operating systems, and the experience; Dominic the capital, the financial architecture, and the technology build; the chef partner the culinary vision (WP p.03). "The mark of these founders is a company built to run without them" (WP p.06). The founders have no manager and are not each other's reports, and the working-with-me documents explicitly do not set this cadence and are its input.

Two constraints shape the design. First, equal partners with non-overlapping domains do not need a meeting to assign each other work; they need a meeting to integrate, to take the decisions in the reserved classes, and to read each other's domains so that a decision in one does not surprise the other. Second, the live constraint: two partners now, the third seat later, so the cadence has an interim form and a three-partner form.

### 9.2 The interim form

Weekly, the partners' review. Fixed day, fixed length, on the calendar. Agenda, in order: each domain's state against the readiness rows, one page per domain, read not presented; decisions in each partner's own range, recorded; decisions in the reserved classes, taken by the interim rule when ratified and held at the status quo until then (`86akh5u77`); cross-domain asks, each with an owner and a date; the knowledge-transfer register, read for what moved since last week; the one thing each partner carries into their domain this week. The record of the review is the partners' page: a decision log by versioned decision (Deck 3.0, p.94), a standing list of open cross-domain asks, and the readiness test's current state.

Monthly, the domain read. Each partner reads the other's domain page in full and writes their questions on it, asynchronously, before the first partners' review of the month, where the questions are the agenda's first item. This is how equal partners review each other's domains without either standing above the other: in writing, on the page, with the domain owner answering. The knowledge-transfer register is read in full at the monthly domain read rather than skimmed weekly.

Quarterly, the gate review (section 8.1), with the readiness test run and the working-with-me documents on the table as input to how the next quarter's partners' reviews run.

**P53 (Recommended).** The interim founder cadence is: the partners' review weekly, on a fixed day, with a fixed agenda ending in the one thing per domain, recorded on the partners' page as versioned decisions; the domain read monthly, asynchronous, each partner writing questions on the other's page; the gate review quarterly with the working-with-me documents as input.

**P54 (Founder-gated).** The interim two-partner decision rule (`86akh5u77`). Session 3's argued interim stands as the recommendation: domain decides after consultation outside the reserved classes; both or neither inside them with the status quo as default; lunch frozen. This page's partners' review runs on whatever rule is ratified and holds the status quo until one is.

### 9.4 The in-service form

In service, the founders' presence on the floor runs on the service metronome, and the founder cadence moves off the floor and out of service hours (section 2.7).
